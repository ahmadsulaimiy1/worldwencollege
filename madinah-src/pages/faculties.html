Faculty V

Professional Arabic & Special Purpose Studies

كلية العربية المهنية والدراسات التخصصية
In development — admitting no one

This faculty is not open. No Dean has been appointed and no programme within it admits students. It is published at that status, and not above it, because the College’s rule is that a place in the curriculum is earned by being necessary, teachable at a distance, examinable and staffed — and a faculty announced before it is staffed fails the last of those in front of everyone.

It will open on the appointment of a Dean, and the appointment will be entered here in the same way the Register of Asānīd enters the vacancy for a second holder of the ten readings.

What it is for. Languages for Specific Purposes is an established field with its own literature and its own methods: the Arabic a physician needs at a bedside is not the Arabic a diplomat needs at a table, and neither is taught well by a general course. This faculty exists to hold that work — and to hold the professional formation of teachers, which is not Da’wah and had been sitting inside it.

Academic structure

Programmes are taught to prescribed texts and examined against published syllabi.

Every programme is composed of taught courses, each with a syllabus, a set of prescribed texts and a stated method of assessment. Syllabi are issued to students on matriculation and are available to applicants from the Registry on request.

The classical texts of the discipline are used as the teaching basis throughout. Where a programme requires a text to be committed to memory, this is stated in the syllabus and forms part of the oral examination.

Classes are capped at twelve students. Instruction is by scheduled live class, with sessions recorded and made available to enrolled students for revision.

Reading the duration column

The durations below are those of a student at the regular pace, about twelve hours a week. A programme is defined by the body of work it requires — stated in notional hours of study — and the months are an outcome of the pace a particular student keeps, not a definition of the programme. One semester of full study is reckoned at 200 notional hours. The intensive and concentrated paces compress the weeks; they do not reduce the hours, the syllabus, the examination or the wording of the award. See Regulation 2.

The standard of a course

No course enters a syllabus with a field left blank.

Before a course is taught it carries a permanent code, its title in Arabic and in English, its level, its prerequisites, its notional hours divided into instruction, independent study and examination, the structure of a typical week, its instruments of assessment, the criterion for its gate published in advance, the recommended pace, and the exact form in which it will appear on a transcript.

Its learning outcomes are written as things a student can be seen to do. The College does not accept an outcome written as understand, know or be familiar with: none of them can be observed, an outcome that cannot be observed cannot be examined, and what cannot be examined must not be certificated — nor charged for.

Da’wah is a discipline, not an application of a language: it has its own classical literature and its own methods, and this faculty holds them. The Diploma in Qur’ānic Pedagogy, which prepares a teacher to conduct a class and examine to the College’s standard, has moved to the Faculty of Professional Arabic & Special Purpose Studies, where professional formation belongs.

What is taught

What earns a place in a syllabus.

A catalogue is easy to lengthen and hard to honour. A subject enters a programme of this College only where all four of the following hold.

Necessity

A graduate who lacks it cannot meet the stated outcomes of the level. A subject added because it rounds out a page is a subject the student pays for twice — once in fees and once in the weeks it takes from what they came for.

Teachable at a distance

It can be taught remotely and taught well, rather than merely delivered. Where a subject genuinely requires presence, the College says so and does not pretend a recording is the same thing.

Examinable

Its attainment can be evidenced. A subject the College cannot examine is a subject it cannot certificate, and therefore a subject it should not charge for.

Staffed

A qualified teacher holds it, or has been appointed to it. A programme with no appointed teacher is published as forthcoming with the semester in which it opens, and admits no student until it has one.

And what is not taught here

The language of the College is الفصحى — classical and modern standard Arabic. The colloquial varieties are not taught, and a student who wants Levantine, Egyptian or Gulf speech is told plainly that this is not the place for it: the purpose here is access to the tradition, and the tradition is written in fuṣḥā.

Explanation is given in English alongside Arabic at the certificate levels; instruction at the Diploma and Higher Diploma is conducted in Arabic, which is stated at the outset so that it is a goal a student works toward rather than a difficulty sprung on them. Naming what the College does not teach is part of describing what it does.
