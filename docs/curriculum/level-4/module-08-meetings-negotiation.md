# WEC — Level IV, Module 8: Meetings & Negotiation

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-4-upper-intermediate.md`'s Level IV build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 8, the learner
can: form and use non-defining relative clauses to add extra
information smoothly; disagree diplomatically in a professional
register; participate actively in a meeting, including proposing and
responding to agenda items; negotiate toward a compromise using
structured negotiation language; write accurate meeting minutes and a
professional follow-up email.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 8.1 ("My Colleague, Who Has Worked Here for Ten Years... —
Non-Defining Relative Clauses"); Lesson 8.2 ("I Hear What You're
Saying, But... — Negotiation & Meeting Language"); Module 8 Quiz (10
questions); Module 8 Assignment ("A Negotiation Roleplay & Meeting
Minutes").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** I hear what you're saying,
but... — Let's find some middle ground. — Would you be open to...? —
That's a good starting point, but... — Can I just come in here? — On
the condition that.../Provided that...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — negotiation
conditions):** *on the condition that*, *provided that*, *in exchange
for*, *as long as* — connectors specific to proposing and accepting
conditional compromises, distinct from Module 3's general professional
correspondence connectors.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *meet halfway* (each
side compromises equally), *hammer out [a deal]* (work through
difficulties to reach an agreement), *iron out [details]* (resolve
minor remaining problems), *hold firm (on a point)* (refuse to
compromise on something specific), *sweeten the deal* (make an offer
more attractive, often with an added benefit).

**BrE / AmE note:** British professionals typically check their
**diary** when scheduling ("Let me check my diary and get back to
you"), while American English uses **calendar** for the same everyday
scheduling sense (British "diary" can also mean a personal journal — a
different sense entirely — while American "diary" is almost always
the journal sense, not a schedule); using the wrong term isn't a
serious error, but recognising both avoids a moment's confusion in a
real meeting.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** meeting vocabulary (agenda, minutes,
action point, chair, quorum), negotiation vocabulary (leverage,
concession, stalemate, compromise, terms). Intercultural note: norms
around directness in disagreement, how much small talk precedes
business, and how negotiations are expected to proceed (rapid
back-and-forth vs. slower relationship-building) vary significantly by
culture and industry; this module teaches one widely used
international-professional-English convention, not a universal
standard.

## Lesson 8.1 — "My Colleague, Who Has Worked Here for Ten Years... — Non-Defining Relative Clauses"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
non-defining relative clauses correctly, using commas; (2) use them to
add extra, non-essential information smoothly within a sentence; (3)
distinguish non-defining relative clauses (extra information, can be
removed without changing the core meaning) from defining relative
clauses (Level III Module 2 — essential information, cannot be
removed); (4) use `which` correctly to refer back to an entire
preceding clause (not just a noun).

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 2 (defining relative
clauses — this lesson introduces the non-defining counterpart and the
critical distinction between the two).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows two similar sentences — one with
a defining relative clause, one with a non-defining one — and asks
learners what changes if the relative clause is removed from each,
before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "The colleague who reviewed the
proposal had some concerns" (defining — specifies *which* colleague;
removing the clause changes the meaning) vs. "My colleague, who has
worked here for ten years, had some concerns" (non-defining — extra
information about a colleague already identified by "my"; removing the
clause leaves the core meaning intact — we still know whose concerns
these are). Highlight explicitly: non-defining clauses are always set
off by commas and add extra, non-essential detail; `that` is never
used in a non-defining clause (only `who/which/whose`). Also model
`which` referring back to an entire previous clause: "The meeting ran
long, which frustrated several attendees" (`which` = the fact that the
meeting ran long, not a single noun).

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 8 sentence pairs and
decide whether each needs a defining or non-defining relative clause
based on context, adding commas correctly where needed.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 sentences about
real or invented colleagues, meetings, or projects, using at least 3
non-defining relative clauses (including at least one `which`
referring back to a whole clause), then read them to a partner, who
confirms which information was "extra" versus "essential."

**Speaking activity.** The partner confirmation exchange above,
followed by a brief whole-class share of one particularly natural-
sounding non-defining relative clause per pair.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think it
matters, in professional writing, whether information is 'essential'
or just 'extra'? Can you think of a situation where getting this
distinction wrong could actually cause confusion?" — connects the
grammar point directly to genuine professional-communication stakes,
not just accuracy for its own sake.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short professional
account (6-7 sentences, mixing defining and non-defining relative
clauses) and identify which type each clause is and why.

**Reading activity — extended reading & analysis (8 min).** Read a
short professional report excerpt (180-200 words) using both clause
types. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 analytical questions ("Find
one non-defining relative clause. What would be lost, if anything, if
it were deleted entirely?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 sentences about your own
workplace or study context, using at least 2 non-defining relative
clauses correctly, with commas.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill the natural intonation dip
and brief pause that typically surrounds a spoken non-defining
relative clause (a slight drop in pitch, as if in parentheses) —
distinct from the smoother, unpaused flow of a defining relative
clause integrated into the main sentence.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A clause-type sorting game: sort 10
example sentences into defining vs. non-defining relative clauses,
checking comma placement.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct comma use and
correct defining/non-defining distinction during independent practice
— the lesson's core target error.

**Homework.** Learners think of one real or invented workplace
negotiation scenario (a project timeline, a resource allocation, a
role assignment) and jot down each side's likely position, ready for
Lesson 8.2's negotiation roleplay.

**Revision.** Lesson 8.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
homework negotiation scenario.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one sentence combining a
defining and a non-defining relative clause in the same sentence,
correctly punctuated.

## Lesson 8.2 — "I Hear What You're Saying, But... — Negotiation & Meeting Language"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
disagree diplomatically in a professional register, without being
either too passive or too blunt; (2) participate in a meeting,
proposing and responding to agenda items appropriately; (3) negotiate
toward a compromise using structured conditional language (`on the
condition that/provided that`); (4) write accurate meeting minutes and
a professional follow-up email.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 8.1 (non-defining relative clauses,
for describing context/participants), Level III Module 4 (formal
opinion/agreement language, now extended into live negotiation).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor models one overly blunt disagreement
("No, that's wrong") and one diplomatically phrased one ("I hear what
you're saying, but I'd push back on one part of that") on the same
point, asking learners which is more likely to keep a negotiation
productive, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model a short negotiation exchange:
"A: We need this delivered by Friday. B: I hear what you're saying,
but that timeline is tight given the resources we have. Would you be
open to Monday instead? A: I could accept Monday, provided that we get
a progress update on Wednesday. B: That works — let's meet halfway
and confirm Wednesday and Monday." Highlight explicitly: **diplomatic
disagreement** (acknowledge, then push back specifically, not
personally); **proposing a condition** (`provided that/on the
condition that`); **finding middle ground** (`meet halfway`, a
genuine two-sided compromise, not just one side conceding). Then model
meeting participation language: proposing an agenda item (`Could we
add... to the agenda?`), politely interrupting (`Can I just come in
here?`), and confirming an action point (`So, to confirm, [name] will
handle X by [date]`).

**Guided practice (10 min).** In pairs, learners practise 4 short
negotiation exchanges from prompt cards, each requiring one diplomatic
disagreement, one conditional proposal, and one compromise phrase.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 8.1 homework
scenario, learners prepare their side's opening position, one
anticipated point of disagreement, and one possible conditional
compromise.

**Speaking activity — negotiation & meeting roleplay.** In pairs or
small groups, learners hold a full negotiation roleplay on their
prepared scenario (opening positions, diplomatic disagreement,
conditional proposals, reaching or failing to reach a compromise),
framed as part of a short meeting (one person acts as chair, opening
and closing the discussion and confirming action points) — this
lesson's core task, and the direct basis for Module 8's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is it always possible to
find a compromise that satisfies both sides equally? What happens,
professionally, when a negotiation genuinely can't reach middle
ground?" — a genuinely realistic question resisting the idea that
every negotiation training scenario has to end in tidy agreement.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short negotiation exchange
and identify the diplomatic disagreement, the conditional proposal,
and whether a compromise was reached.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short set of meeting minutes and
identify the agenda items discussed, the decisions made, and the
action points assigned (who, what, by when).

**Writing task (5 min).** Write brief minutes (5-6 bullet points) for
your Lesson 8.2 negotiation roleplay, including at least one action
point with a name and a deadline.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill calm, non-confrontational
intonation for diplomatic disagreement in a live negotiation — level
pitch, not rising sharply (which can sound challenging) or dropping
flatly (which can sound dismissive) — and clear, confirming intonation
when stating an agreed action point.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A negotiation-phrasal-verb matching game
(meet halfway, hammer out, iron out, hold firm, sweeten the deal)
matched to negotiation scenarios.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that disagreement stays
diplomatic and specific (not vague or personal) and that any
compromise reached is genuinely two-sided, during the roleplay.

**Homework.** Learners write a short professional follow-up email
(recycling Module 3's email structure) summarising their negotiation's
outcome and confirming next steps, ready for Module 8's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 8.1 scenario-naming
recap. Module 8's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a moment where their
negotiation nearly reaches a stalemate, then practise language for
proposing a genuinely creative alternative to break the deadlock.

## Module 8 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-4.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. Which sentence uses a non-defining relative clause correctly? — (a)
   The colleague who reviewed the proposal had concerns. (b) My
   colleague, who has worked here for ten years, had concerns. ✓ (c)
   My colleague that has worked here for ten years had concerns. (d)
   My colleague, that has worked here, had concerns.
2. "The meeting ran long, ___ frustrated several attendees." (referring
   to the whole previous clause) — (a) that (b) which ✓ (c) who (d)
   whose
3. If a non-defining relative clause is removed from a sentence, the
   core meaning: — (a) is completely lost (b) stays essentially
   intact ✓ (c) becomes grammatically incorrect (d) reverses entirely
4. "I hear what you're saying, ___ I'd push back on one part of
   that." — (a) so (b) but ✓ (c) because (d) if
5. "I could accept Monday, ___ we get a progress update on
   Wednesday." — (a) provided that ✓ (b) because (c) so (d) unless
6. Which phrase means "each side compromises equally"? — (a) hold firm
   (b) meet halfway ✓ (c) sweeten the deal (d) hammer out
7. In British English, checking your schedule for a meeting is often
   called checking your: — (a) calendar (b) diary ✓ (c) planner (d)
   agenda
8. Which phrase means "resolve minor remaining problems"? — (a)
   hammer out (b) iron out ✓ (c) hold firm (d) meet halfway
9. "So, to confirm, [name] will handle X by [date]." This sentence is
   an example of: — (a) a diplomatic disagreement (b) an action point
   ✓ (c) a rhetorical question (d) a non-defining relative clause
10. Which phrase means "refuse to compromise on something specific"?
    — (a) meet halfway (b) sweeten the deal (c) hold firm ✓ (d) iron
    out

## Module 8 Assignment — "A Negotiation Roleplay & Meeting Minutes"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Complete two parts based on a
real or invented workplace negotiation scenario. **Part A (speaking):**
Record yourself (or perform with a partner) a negotiation roleplay,
90 seconds to 2 minutes, including at least one diplomatic
disagreement, one conditional proposal (`provided that/on the
condition that`), and a clear outcome (a compromise reached, or an
honest acknowledgement that agreement wasn't reached). Use at least
one non-defining relative clause naturally within the roleplay. **Part
B (formal correspondence, continuing this level's genre range):**
Write brief meeting minutes (5-8 bullet points, including at least one
action point with a name and deadline) and a short professional
follow-up email summarising the outcome.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct non-defining relative clause
  formation, correct negotiation conditional structures.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 3 distinct meeting/negotiation words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — diplomatic disagreement, a conditional
  proposal, and a clear outcome in Part A; complete minutes with an
  action point and a follow-up email in Part B.
- **Communicative quality** — does the negotiation sound genuinely
  professional and realistic, and do the minutes accurately and
  concisely capture what was decided?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the spoken negotiation's
  tone diplomatic throughout (never blunt or passive), and is the
  written correspondence's register consistently professional?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 8
completed for the learner.
