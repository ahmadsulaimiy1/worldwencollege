# WEC — Level IV, Module 5: Science, Technology & Ethics

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-4-upper-intermediate.md`'s Level IV build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 5, the learner
can: use the passive voice across present perfect, modal, and future
forms to discuss technology and ethics with appropriate academic
distance; report questions and commands accurately, using a range of
advanced reporting verbs; participate in a panel discussion,
representing and defending a distinct position among several; write a
compare-and-contrast essay evaluating two technologies or ethical
approaches.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 5.1 ("It Has Been Argued That... — Passive Voice II, Advanced
Tenses"); Lesson 5.2 ("She Asked Whether... — Reported Speech II &
a Panel Discussion"); Module 5 Quiz (10 questions); Module 5
Assignment ("A Compare-and-Contrast Essay — Two Technologies or
Approaches").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** It has been argued/suggested
that... — This technology should be regulated... — Concerns have been
raised about... — She asked whether/if... — He urged them to... — On
balance,.../When weighed against...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — formal comparison
and evaluation):** *on balance*, *when weighed against*, *by
comparison*, *by contrast* — more formal comparison connectors than
Level III's `whereas/in contrast`, suited to weighing two technologies
or ethical positions systematically rather than casually contrasting
two customs.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *grapple with [an
issue]* (struggle seriously to understand or resolve it), *call into
question [something]* (cause doubt about its validity), *shed light on
[something]* (help explain or clarify it), *raise concerns about
[something]*, *strike a balance (between two things)* (find a
reasonable middle position).

**BrE / AmE note:** British English distinguishes **programme**
(a general plan, schedule, or broadcast — "a programme of research," "a
TV programme") from **program** (specifically computer software,
matching American spelling in this one technical sense); American
English uses **program** for both meanings without distinction — a
genuinely useful spelling nuance for a technology-focused module,
since British academic/technical writing does observe this split.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** technology/ethics vocabulary (algorithm,
regulation, innovation, unintended consequence, safeguard, dilemma),
academic hedging language (it could be argued, arguably, to some
extent). Intercultural note: attitudes toward new technology —
enthusiasm, caution, regulatory appetite — vary significantly by
country, generation, and institution; this module's panel discussion
is designed so learners argue an assigned position, not necessarily
their personal one, specifically to practise fair representation of
views they may not hold.

## Lesson 5.1 — "It Has Been Argued That... — Passive Voice II, Advanced Tenses"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
present perfect passive correctly (`has/have been + past participle`);
(2) form modal passive correctly (`modal + be + past participle`, e.g.
`should be regulated`, `could be misused`); (3) form future passive
correctly (`will be + past participle`); (4) choose the passive
deliberately for academic distance when discussing technology and
ethics.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 5 (present/past simple
passive — this lesson extends the passive system across further
tenses and modals).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one active sentence about a
technology issue and models converting it through several passive
forms (simple, perfect, modal, future) and asks learners what changes
in meaning/emphasis each time, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "It has been argued that artificial
intelligence will transform the workplace. This technology should be
regulated to prevent misuse. Concerns have been raised about data
privacy. The industry will be significantly affected by new
legislation." Highlight explicitly: present perfect passive reports an
ongoing or recently established claim/debate (`has been argued`);
modal passive expresses necessity, possibility, or recommendation
about an action (`should be regulated`, `could be misused`); future
passive predicts a future effect on something (`will be affected`) —
each form serves a genuinely distinct communicative function in
technology/ethics discussion, not just grammatical variety for its own
sake.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 active sentences
about technology/ethics topics into the passive form indicated
(present perfect, modal, or future), checking that the resulting
sentence still makes clear communicative sense.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 sentences about a
technology or ethical issue of their choice, using at least 3
different passive forms from this lesson (present perfect, modal,
future), then explain to a partner why they chose the passive rather
than active voice for each.

**Speaking activity.** The partner explanation exchange above — a
direct check that learners understand the passive's *function*
(academic distance, focus on the issue not the arguer), not just its
form.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think academic
and journalistic writing about technology and ethics often uses
passive constructions like 'it has been argued that' rather than
naming exactly who argued it? Is this always a good thing?" — a
genuinely critical B2 question, since vague passive attribution
(`it has been argued`) can also be used to obscure who actually holds
a view — worth naming as a real limitation of the structure, not just
its strength.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short news-style report
about a technology issue (6-7 sentences, mixing passive forms) and
identify each passive construction and its tense/modal.

**Reading activity — extended reading & analysis (8 min).** Read a
short technology-ethics article excerpt (180-200 words) using a range
of passive constructions. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 analytical
questions ("Where does the writer use passive voice to avoid naming a
source, and does this weaken or strengthen the article's
credibility?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 sentences about a technology or
ethical issue, using present perfect, modal, and future passive at
least once each.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill stress on the past
participle in passive constructions across longer verb phrases ("has
been REGulated," "should be adDRESSed") and clear, level intonation
appropriate to neutral, academic-register reporting.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A technology/ethics vocabulary matching
game (algorithm, regulation, innovation, unintended consequence,
safeguard, dilemma) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct formation across
all three advanced passive forms and, more importantly, deliberate
(not random) choice of passive for genuine academic distance, during
independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose two related technologies or two ethical
approaches to the same issue (for compare-and-contrast writing later)
and jot down 2-3 notes on each, ready for Lesson 5.2's panel
discussion.

**Revision.** Lesson 5.2 opens with learners briefly naming their two
chosen technologies/approaches.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one present continuous
passive sentence as a recognition-level review ("The technology is
being adopted rapidly," recycling Level III Module 5's extension
preview, now consolidated into the fuller passive system).

## Lesson 5.2 — "She Asked Whether... — Reported Speech II & a Panel Discussion"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
report yes/no questions correctly using `whether/if`; (2) report
wh-questions correctly, with correct word order (no inversion); (3)
report commands and strong recommendations using advanced reporting
verbs (`urged, insisted, warned`); (4) participate in a panel
discussion, representing an assigned position and responding to other
panellists' points.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 6 (reported speech for
statements — this lesson extends to questions and commands, with a
wider range of reporting verbs).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor asks the class a direct question
("Is this technology ethical?") and immediately models reporting it
("I just asked whether this technology was ethical") before formal
presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: direct — "Is this ethical?" →
reported — "She asked whether/if it was ethical." Direct — "What are
the risks?" → reported — "He asked what the risks were" (highlight: no
question-word inversion in the reported form — a common error worth
naming directly). Direct — "Regulate this now!" → reported — "The
panellist urged the committee to regulate it immediately." Introduce a
small set of advanced reporting verbs beyond `say/tell/ask` —
`urged, insisted, warned, claimed, argued` — each carrying its own
nuance of how something was said, not just that it was said.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 direct
questions/commands (a mix of yes/no questions, wh-questions, and
commands, all technology/ethics-themed) into reported speech, choosing
an appropriate reporting verb for each.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In small groups (3-4), learners are
each assigned a distinct position on a technology-ethics issue (e.g.
"strongly favours minimal regulation," "favours strict regulation,"
"favours a middle-ground, case-by-case approach") and prepare 2-3
points for their assigned position, including at least one passive
construction from Lesson 5.1.

**Speaking activity — panel discussion.** Groups hold a structured
panel discussion: each panellist states their position, responds to at
least one other panellist directly (using reported speech to
accurately relay what that panellist said — `You argued that...,
but...`), and the discussion closes with each panellist briefly
summarising where they'd be willing to compromise — this lesson's core
task, and the direct basis for Module 5's assignment's discussion
element.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "When you represented a
position in this discussion that wasn't necessarily your own personal
view, did preparing good arguments for it change how you think about
that position at all? Why might that be useful?" — connects directly
to the module's stated intercultural/perspective-taking purpose.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short panel exchange and
note each panellist's position and one instance of accurate
reported-speech relay of another panellist's point.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written panel-discussion
transcript excerpt and identify its reported questions/commands and
the reporting verbs used.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short summary (5-6 sentences) of
your panel discussion, using reported speech to relay at least 2
different panellists' positions accurately.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill composed, professional
intonation for panel-discussion turn-taking — a brief, polite
interruption phrase ("If I could just come in here...") and clear,
non-confrontational stress when directly referencing another
speaker's point.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An advanced-reporting-verb matching game
(urged, insisted, warned, claimed, argued) matched to example direct
quotations, discussing the nuance each verb adds.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct reported-question/
command formation and accurate, fair representation of other
panellists' points during the discussion.

**Homework.** Learners begin drafting their compare-and-contrast essay
outline using their Lesson 5.1 homework notes on two technologies or
ethical approaches.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 5.1 technology/
approach-naming recap. Module 5's Quiz and Assignment draw on both
lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a reported command using
a stronger reporting verb than `urged` (`insisted that... must...`),
noting the increased force this conveys.

   (future passive) — (a) is (b) was (c) will be ✓ (d) has been

## Module 5 Assignment — "A Compare-and-Contrast Essay — Two Technologies or Approaches"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write a compare-and-contrast
essay, 350-450 words, evaluating two related technologies or two
different ethical/regulatory approaches to the same issue. This is
this level's fourth writing genre — **compare-and-contrast writing**.
Structure your essay clearly (either point-by-point or
subject-by-subject organisation). Include: at least 2 passive
constructions from Lesson 5.1 (present perfect, modal, or future); at
least one reported-speech sentence relaying a claim or argument you've
encountered about either option; at least one formal comparison
connector (`on balance/when weighed against/by comparison/by
contrast`); and a reasoned conclusion about which option you find more
compelling, or under what conditions each might be preferable.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct advanced passive formation,
  correct reported speech.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct technology/ethics words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — both options genuinely compared (not just
  described separately with no real comparison), a passive
  construction, a reported claim, and a reasoned conclusion all
  present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the comparison genuinely
  balanced and specific (not superficial), and is the conclusion
  actually justified by the comparison rather than asserted?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the essay's organisation
  clear and consistent (not switching structure mid-essay), and is the
  register appropriately formal and academic throughout?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 5
completed for the learner.
