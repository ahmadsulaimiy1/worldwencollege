# WEC-LC — Level IV, Module 4: Arguing a Position

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-4-upper-intermediate.md`'s Level IV build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 4, the learner
can: concede a point gracefully using advanced concession language
without abandoning their overall position; distinguish a genuine
concession from a rhetorical trap; participate in a full formal debate
(a motion, opening statement, rebuttal, closing statement); discuss
what effective leadership communication looks like, using the
module's language under real time pressure.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 4.1 ("While It's True That... — Advanced Concession
Language"); Lesson 4.2 ("The Motion Is... — Formal Debate
Structure"); Module 4 Quiz (10 questions); Module 4 Assignment
("A Formal Debate — Leadership Communication").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** While it's true that..., this
doesn't undermine... — Granted,...; nevertheless,... — It would be
naive to deny that..., yet... — I'd like to propose the motion
that... — In rebuttal,... — To conclude, the case for/against this
motion is...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — advanced
concession):** *granted*, *admittedly*, *while it's true that*, *it
would be naive to deny that* — more sophisticated concession openers
than Level III's `that's a fair point, however`, each signalling that
the speaker has genuinely weighed the counter-point before restating
why their position still stands.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *hold up (under
scrutiny)* (remain valid when closely examined), *poke holes in [an
argument]* (identify weaknesses in it), *come round to [a viewpoint]*
(gradually start to agree with it), *dig in* (refuse to change
position, often stubbornly), *give ground* (make a concession,
especially reluctantly).

**BrE / AmE note:** **moot point** has genuinely drifted apart in
meaning between the two varieties: in British English (and the term's
original legal sense, still standard in both), a moot point is one
that is **open to debate or uncertain** — worth discussing precisely
because it isn't settled; in informal American usage, "moot" has
increasingly come to mean **irrelevant or no longer worth discussing**
(because circumstances have made the question pointless) — a genuine
semantic drift worth knowing, since the two meanings are almost
opposite.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** debate vocabulary (motion, proposition,
opposition, rebuttal, floor, adjudicator), leadership vocabulary
recycled from Level III Module 4 (decisive, accountable, delegate,
inspire) now used as live debate content. Intercultural note: debate
as a formal competitive format (with motions, sides, and
adjudication) is one widely used international-academic convention,
not a universal way of resolving disagreement — this module teaches it
as a useful structured *skill*, not the only legitimate way to discuss
a difficult topic.

## Lesson 4.1 — "While It's True That... — Advanced Concession Language"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
advanced concession phrases (`granted, admittedly, while it's true
that, it would be naive to deny that`) to acknowledge a genuine
counter-point; (2) follow a concession with a clear reason your
overall position still holds; (3) distinguish a genuine, confidence-
building concession from a rhetorical trap (conceding something that
actually undermines your whole argument); (4) evaluate whether an
argument "holds up under scrutiny."

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 4 (formal opinion
language, the basic agree/partially-agree/disagree/concede scale —
this lesson develops the concession end of that scale considerably).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one opinion, then models two
different concessions to the same counter-point — one that
accidentally undermines their whole position, one that strengthens it
— and asks learners to identify the difference, before formal
presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "Granted, remote work can make
spontaneous collaboration harder. Nevertheless, the flexibility it
offers most employees outweighs that cost for the majority of roles."
Then: "While it's true that some roles genuinely require in-person
presence, this doesn't undermine the case for flexible policies more
broadly — it simply means policies should account for role-specific
exceptions." Highlight explicitly: a **strong** concession
acknowledges something real and specific, then explains precisely why
the overall position still holds (often by narrowing what's being
conceded); a **weak** or trap-like concession accidentally admits
something that actually breaks the whole argument — a distinction
worth testing critically, not just producing mechanically.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 6 argument-plus-
counter-point pairs and, for each, write a strong concession (using
this module's language) that acknowledges the counter-point without
undermining the overall position.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners choose one of their own
opinions from earlier levels' debate work (or a new one) and prepare a
concession to the strongest counter-argument they can think of, then
present both the concession and their maintained position to a
partner, who evaluates whether the concession was genuine and whether
the position still "held up."

**Speaking activity.** The partner evaluation exchange above, followed
by a short whole-class discussion of one example where a concession
made an argument *more* persuasive, not less.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think
acknowledging a weakness in your own argument can sometimes make you
more persuasive, not less? Is there a risk to conceding too much?" —
directly extends Level III's parallel question, now asking learners to
think critically about the *limits* of the technique as well as its
value.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short structured argument
including a concession (6-7 sentences) and evaluate whether the
concession is strong (narrows and reasons) or weak (undermines the
whole position).

**Reading activity — extended reading & analysis (8 min).** Read a
short opinion-piece excerpt (180-200 words) that includes at least one
concession. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 analytical questions
("Does the writer's concession genuinely strengthen their argument, or
does it actually weaken it? What would you have conceded differently,
if anything?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short paragraph (5-6 sentences)
presenting an opinion, a genuine concession using this module's
language, and a clear explanation of why your position still holds.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill measured, confident
intonation on concession openers (`Granted...`, `Admittedly...`) —
neither dismissive nor overly apologetic — followed by a clear
strengthening of tone on the "nevertheless" clause that restates the
position.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A concession-strength card-sorting game:
sort 8 example concessions into "strengthens the argument" vs.
"accidentally undermines it."

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that concessions are
genuine and specific (not vague) and that the maintained position is
clearly reasoned, during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a position on a leadership-related
motion (provided by the instructor, or self-chosen) and prepare 2-3
reasons plus one anticipated counter-argument, ready for Lesson 4.2's
formal debate.

**Revision.** Lesson 4.2 opens with learners briefly stating their
homework position in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners prepare a concession to
their own anticipated strongest counter-argument in advance, so it's
ready to deploy naturally during the debate.

## Lesson 4.2 — "The Motion Is... — Formal Debate Structure"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
understand and use standard formal-debate structure and vocabulary
(motion, proposition, opposition, rebuttal); (2) deliver a structured
opening statement for a position; (3) rebut an opposing argument
directly and specifically, not just restate your own position; (4)
deliver a closing statement that synthesises the debate, including at
least one graceful concession.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 4.1 (concession language), Level
III Module 4 (the 3-round mini-debate — this lesson formalises that
into a full structured format).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states a debate motion ("This house
believes that remote work should be the default for all office-based
roles") and asks learners to quickly generate one argument for and one
against, before formal presentation, surfacing that most real motions
have genuine merit on both sides.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the formal debate structure
explicitly: **the motion** (a clear, debatable statement, "This house
believes that..."); **proposition** (arguing for) and **opposition**
(arguing against); **opening statements** (each side states its
position and 2 main reasons); **rebuttal** (each side directly
addresses and challenges the *other* side's specific points — not just
repeating their own); **closing statements** (each side synthesises
their case, ideally including one graceful concession using Lesson
4.1's language, before a final restatement of their position).
Highlight explicitly that rebuttal must engage with what the other
side *actually said*, not a generic restatement — the skill that
most distinguishes a strong debater.

**Guided practice (10 min).** In small groups, learners are assigned
proposition or opposition on a provided motion and prepare a 2-point
opening statement together, then practise rebutting a sample opposing
point provided by the instructor.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Groups finalise their opening
statement and prepare at least one anticipated rebuttal point for the
other side's likely argument, using their Lesson 4.1 homework
preparation.

**Speaking activity — formal debate.** The class holds a full
structured debate on the leadership-related motion from Lesson 4.1's
homework: opening statements (proposition, then opposition), a
rebuttal round (each side responds directly to the other's opening),
and closing statements (each side, including at least one genuine
concession) — this level's most sustained, formal speaking task, and
the direct basis for Module 4's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "After the debate, has
anyone's actual opinion shifted, even slightly? What was it that moved
you, if anything — a specific piece of reasoning, or something else?"
— genuinely tests whether the debate functioned as real persuasive
communication, not just a performance exercise.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short formal debate
exchange (an opening statement and a rebuttal) and identify whether
the rebuttal genuinely engages with the opening statement's specific
points.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written debate transcript
excerpt (opening statement + rebuttal + closing) and label each
structural part.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write your side's closing statement (4-5
sentences) as a clean written version, including at least one
concession.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill confident, persuasive
delivery pace and pausing for a formal opening/closing statement —
deliberate emphasis on key reasons, and a composed, non-defensive tone
when delivering a rebuttal.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A debate-vocabulary matching game
(motion, proposition, opposition, rebuttal, floor, adjudicator) with
simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that rebuttals genuinely
engage with the other side's specific points (not a generic
restatement) and that closing statements include a real concession,
during the debate.

**Homework.** Learners write a short written reflection (3-4
sentences) on which side's argument they found most compelling and
why, ready for Module 4's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 4.1 position recap.
Module 4's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners argue the side they
personally disagree with, as an exercise in genuine perspective-taking
and argument construction independent of personal belief.

## Module 4 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-4.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "___, remote work can make spontaneous collaboration harder." (an
   advanced concession opener) — (a) Because (b) Granted ✓ (c)
   Therefore (d) So
2. What distinguishes a strong concession from a weak/trap-like one? —
   (a) A strong concession is longer. (b) A strong concession
   acknowledges something specific without undermining the overall
   position. ✓ (c) A strong concession always agrees completely with
   the other side. (d) A weak concession is always false.
3. "___ some roles genuinely require in-person presence, this doesn't
   undermine the broader case." — (a) While it's true that ✓ (b)
   Because (c) So (d) Unless
4. In a formal debate, what is "the motion"? — (a) a side's final
   score (b) a clear, debatable statement the debate is about ✓ (c) a
   type of rebuttal (d) the adjudicator's decision
5. What should a rebuttal do? — (a) simply restate your own opening
   argument (b) directly address and challenge the other side's
   specific points ✓ (c) ignore what the other side said (d) only ask
   questions
6. In British English (and originally), a "moot point" is one that
   is: — (a) irrelevant (b) open to debate or uncertain ✓ (c) always
   false (d) already decided
7. Which phrase means "identify weaknesses in an argument"? — (a) hold
   up (b) poke holes in ✓ (c) come round to (d) dig in
8. A closing statement in a formal debate should ideally include: —
   (a) a completely new argument (b) at least one graceful concession
   ✓ (c) no reference to the other side (d) only a summary of the
   motion
9. Which phrase means "refuse to change position, often stubbornly"? —
   (a) give ground (b) come round to (c) dig in ✓ (d) hold up
10. Which phrase means "make a concession, especially reluctantly"? —
    (a) give ground ✓ (b) dig in (c) hold up (d) poke holes in

## Module 4 Assignment — "A Formal Debate — Leadership Communication"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Record yourself (or perform
live, alone or with a partner) delivering a formal debate contribution
on a leadership-related motion of your choice, 2-3 minutes total.
Include: a clear opening statement with your position and at least 2
reasons; at least one direct rebuttal of a specific opposing point
(real or anticipated); at least one advanced concession using this
module's language (`granted/admittedly/while it's true that`),
followed by a clear reason your position still holds; and a closing
statement synthesising your case.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, varied use of concession and
  debate structures.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 3 distinct debate/concession phrases
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — opening statement, a specific rebuttal, a
  genuine concession, and a closing statement all present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the reasoning genuinely
  persuasive and specific, does the rebuttal actually engage with the
  opposing point rather than ignoring it, and does the concession
  strengthen rather than undermine the overall position?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register appropriately
  formal throughout, and does the contribution stay respectful and
  evidence-based, even under the pressure of rebuttal?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 4
completed for the learner.
