# AIPC — Level IV, Module 9: Academic Writing II

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-4-upper-intermediate.md`'s Level IV build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 9, the learner
can: read and accurately represent the main claim of a source text;
synthesise two sources into one coherent argument, identifying
agreement, disagreement, or complementary angles between them; use
basic citation mechanics to attribute ideas to their source clearly
and consistently; deploy the level's full range of coherence and
cohesion devices in one extended piece of research-based writing.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 9.1 ("According to Source A... — Synthesising Multiple
Sources"); Lesson 9.2 ("Bringing It All Together — Coherence,
Cohesion & the Research-Based Essay"); Module 9 Quiz (10 questions);
Module 9 Assignment ("A Research-Based Essay from Supplied Sources").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** According to Source A,...,
while Source B suggests... — Both sources agree that... — This is
corroborated by... — Taken together, these sources suggest... — (Source
A) / (Source B) — a genuine point of tension between these two
perspectives is...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — synthesis):** *both
sources agree that*, *while Source A suggests..., Source B
indicates...*, *taken together*, *this is corroborated by* — the
level's most academically demanding connector set, specifically for
holding two sources in relation to each other rather than discussing
either alone.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *draw together [ideas]*
(combine them into one coherent whole), *tie in with [something]*
(connect naturally or logically to it), *cross-reference [two
things]* (check them against each other), *build a case (from
multiple sources)* (construct an argument supported by more than one
source), *corroborate [a claim]* (confirm it using independent
evidence or a second source).

**BrE / AmE note:** quotation-mark convention differs by academic
style tradition, and both are genuinely standard: British academic
writing commonly uses **single quotation marks** (' ') as the default
for a direct quotation, with double marks reserved for a quote-within-
a-quote; American academic writing commonly uses **double quotation
marks** (" ") as the default, with single marks reserved for a
quote-within-a-quote — neither is wrong, but consistency within one
piece of writing matters more than which convention is chosen.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** citation vocabulary (source, citation,
attribution, corroborate, synthesis, in-text citation), the module's
consolidated cohesion toolkit (nominalisation, ellipsis, substitution,
non-defining relative clauses, formal connectors) drawn from across
Level IV. Intercultural note: formal citation systems (APA, MLA,
Chicago, and others) vary by academic discipline and country, and full
mastery of any one system is beyond this level's scope (deferred to
Level V+, per `docs/curriculum-framework.md`); this module teaches the
underlying *habit and logic* of honest, consistent attribution, which
transfers to any formal system later.

## Lesson 9.1 — "According to Source A... — Synthesising Multiple Sources"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
accurately identify a source's main claim and key supporting points;
(2) compare two sources, identifying where they agree, disagree, or
offer complementary angles; (3) use basic citation mechanics
(`(Source A)`-style attribution) consistently; (4) begin synthesising
two sources into one coherent point, rather than summarising each
separately.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 9 (basic citation
awareness); Level IV, Module 2 (genuine paraphrasing) and Module 7
(summary skills) — this lesson combines both into multi-source work
for the first time.

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor reads two short, generic invented
"source" statements on the same topic that partially agree and
partially disagree, and asks learners to identify exactly where they
align and where they differ, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model reading two short supplied source
excerpts (Source A and Source B, generic/invented, 80-100 words each,
on the same topic) and identifying: **Source A's main claim**,
**Source B's main claim**, **where they agree**, **where they
disagree or offer a different angle**. Then model synthesis explicitly:
"According to Source A, remote work improves focus; Source B largely
agrees, though it notes this depends heavily on having a dedicated
workspace (Source B). Taken together, these sources suggest that the
benefit is real but conditional." Highlight explicitly: genuine
synthesis is not "Source A says X. Source B says Y." (two separate
summaries) but a single sentence or paragraph that *relates* the two
claims to each other.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given two more short source
excerpts and identify each one's main claim, then write one sentence
using `both sources agree that` or `while Source A suggests..., Source
B indicates...` to relate them.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners read a new pair of source
excerpts (provided) and write a short synthesised paragraph (4-5
sentences) that genuinely combines both sources' perspectives, using
basic citation mechanics for each claim, then swap with a partner, who
checks whether the paragraph synthesises or merely summarises each
source in turn.

**Speaking activity.** Partners discuss one point of genuine tension
between the two sources and how they each chose to handle it in their
synthesis — comparing different reasonable approaches to the same
material.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think it's
tempting to just summarise sources one after another rather than truly
synthesising them? What does genuine synthesis require that summary
alone doesn't?" — directly targets the lesson's core, genuinely
difficult skill.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone verbally
synthesising two sources (6-7 sentences) and identify where they
state agreement, disagreement, and their own overall conclusion.

**Reading activity — extended reading & analysis (8 min).** Read two
short source excerpts (150 words each) on a generic academic/
professional topic. Answer 2 literal questions (one per source) and 2
analytical/synthesis questions ("Where do these two sources most
directly disagree? What might explain the difference?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 2-3 sentences synthesising the two
reading-activity sources, using at least one citation mechanic and one
synthesis discourse marker.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill clear stress on source
attribution when speaking ("According to SOURCE A..." with a brief
pause before naming the source) so a listener can clearly track which
claim belongs to which source.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A synthesis-language matching game (both
sources agree that, while X suggests Y indicates, taken together, this
is corroborated by) matched to example synthesis contexts.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine synthesis
(relating sources to each other), not parallel summary, and consistent
citation mechanics, during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners re-read their two Lesson 9.1 source excerpts
and jot down one additional point of connection or tension they
haven't yet used, ready for Lesson 9.2's full essay draft.

**Revision.** Lesson 9.2 opens with learners briefly sharing their
additional point in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners identify a plausible reason
*why* the two sources might disagree (different context, different
timeframe, different assumptions) rather than simply noting that they
do.

## Lesson 9.2 — "Bringing It All Together — Coherence, Cohesion & the Research-Based Essay"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
deploy the level's full cohesion toolkit (nominalisation, ellipsis/
substitution, non-defining relative clauses, formal connectors)
within one extended piece; (2) structure a research-based essay that
synthesises two sources into a genuine argument, not just a report of
what each source says; (3) maintain coherence (logical idea
progression) across multiple paragraphs; (4) draft a complete
research-based essay using supplied source material.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 9.1 (synthesis, citation
mechanics), and — explicitly recycled here — Module 2 (thesis/essay
architecture), Module 6 (nominalisation), Module 7 (ellipsis/
substitution), Module 8 (non-defining relative clauses).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one short paragraph missing
clear logical progression (ideas jump around) and one revised version
with clear coherence, asking learners what specifically was fixed,
before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the research-based essay structure
explicitly, building on Module 2's essay architecture: **introduction**
(context, a thesis that takes a position informed by both sources);
**body paragraphs** (each developing one point, synthesising both
sources where relevant, using citation mechanics); **conclusion**
(restating the synthesised position's significance). Then model
weaving in the cohesion toolkit within one worked example paragraph,
annotated aloud: a nominalisation ("the implementation of this
policy"), a non-defining relative clause ("Source A, which surveyed
over a thousand employees, found..."), substitution ("Source B reached
a similar conclusion, and Source A did so too"), and a formal
connector ("Moreover, both sources note..."). Highlight explicitly:
**coherence** is the logical flow of ideas (does each sentence/
paragraph follow sensibly from the last); **cohesion** is the
grammatical "glue" (the specific devices) that makes that flow
readable — related but distinct concepts, both essential to strong
academic writing.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners revise a provided paragraph
with weak coherence (ideas in a confusing order) by reordering
sentences and adding appropriate connectors, then identify which
cohesion devices from this level's toolkit could strengthen it
further.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 9.1 synthesis
work, learners draft their research-based essay's introduction and
first body paragraph, deliberately incorporating at least 2 different
cohesion devices from the toolkit.

**Speaking activity.** Learners read their draft introduction aloud to
a partner, who identifies the thesis and checks whether it genuinely
reflects a synthesis of both sources (not just one).

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Looking back across this
entire level, which of the cohesion devices you've learned (passive
voice, nominalisation, ellipsis/substitution, relative clauses,
formal connectors) do you find yourself using most naturally now, and
which still feels effortful? Why might that be?" — a genuinely
reflective, level-spanning question appropriate to this capstone
lesson.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short research-based
talk that synthesises two sources and identify at least 3 different
cohesion devices used.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a model research-based essay
excerpt (200-220 words) and annotate it for coherence (logical
progression) and specific cohesion devices used.

**Writing task (5 min).** Continue drafting your essay's second body
paragraph and conclusion.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill sustained, well-paced formal
delivery appropriate to presenting a research-based argument aloud (if
sharing a draft verbally) — this level's most demanding register,
combining Module 6's presentation pacing with Module 2's academic
tone.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A cohesion-device identification
relay: learners find one example of each of the level's five major
cohesion devices within a single provided dense paragraph.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine coherence
(logical progression) and correct, purposeful (not mechanical) use of
cohesion devices during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners complete their full research-based essay draft
for Module 9's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 9.1 connection-point
recap. Module 9's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a brief acknowledgement
of a limitation in their own synthesis (what the two sources together
still don't fully answer) as a sophisticated closing move.

## Module 9 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

`sql/seed-curriculum-level-4.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. What is genuine synthesis, as opposed to parallel summary? — (a)
   summarising Source A, then separately summarising Source B (b)
   relating two sources' claims to each other in one coherent point ✓
   (c) only using Source A and ignoring Source B (d) copying both
   sources word-for-word
2. "___ Source A suggests remote work improves focus, Source B notes
   this depends on having a dedicated workspace." — (a) While ✓ (b)
   Because (c) So (d) Since
3. "Taken together, these sources ___ that the benefit is real but
   conditional." — (a) suggest ✓ (b) suggests (c) suggesting (d)
   suggested
4. What does "coherence" refer to in academic writing? — (a) the
   specific grammatical devices used (b) the logical flow and
   progression of ideas ✓ (c) the total word count (d) the citation
   style used
5. What does "cohesion" refer to in academic writing? — (a) the
   logical flow of ideas only (b) the grammatical "glue" (specific
   devices) that makes that flow readable ✓ (c) the essay's overall
   length (d) the number of sources used
6. In American academic writing, the default quotation mark for a
   direct quote is usually: — (a) single (' ') (b) double (" ") ✓ (c)
   angled («») (d) none are used
7. Which phrase means "confirm a claim using independent evidence or a
   second source"? — (a) draw together (b) tie in with (c)
   corroborate ✓ (d) cross-reference
8. "This is ___ by a second, independent study." — (a) corroborated ✓
   (b) drawn together (c) tied in (d) cross-referenced
9. Which best describes this level's stated goal for citation
   mechanics? — (a) full mastery of one formal system (APA/MLA/
   Chicago) (b) the underlying habit and logic of honest, consistent
   attribution ✓ (c) avoiding citation entirely (d) memorising citation
   rules without applying them
10. Which phrase means "combine ideas into one coherent whole"? — (a)
    tie in with (b) draw together ✓ (c) corroborate (d) cross-reference

## Module 9 Assignment — "A Research-Based Essay from Supplied Sources"

**Instructions given to the learner:** You will be given two short
supplied source excerpts (generic, invented, 100-120 words each) on
the same topic, representing partially agreeing, partially
disagreeing, or complementary perspectives. Write a research-based
essay, 400-500 words, that synthesises both sources into one coherent
argument. This is this level's **capstone, eighth writing genre**.
Your essay must include: a clear thesis informed by both sources; at
least 2 genuine synthesis statements (not parallel summary) using this
module's discourse markers; consistent basic citation mechanics
throughout; and at least 3 different cohesion devices from this
level's toolkit (nominalisation, ellipsis/substitution, non-defining
relative clauses, formal connectors), used purposefully.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, varied use of the level's
  cohesion devices.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct citation/synthesis words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — a synthesised thesis, genuine synthesis
  (not parallel summary), consistent citation, and at least 3 cohesion
  devices all present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — does the essay genuinely engage
  with both sources' claims (including any tension between them), and
  is the final position actually justified by that engagement, not
  just asserted?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — does the essay read as one
  logically progressing, well-glued piece of formal academic writing —
  the level's most demanding coherence-and-cohesion standard?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 9
completed for the learner.
