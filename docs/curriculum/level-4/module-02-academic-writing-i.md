# WEC-LC — Level IV, Module 2: Academic Writing I

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-4-upper-intermediate.md`'s Level IV build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 2, the learner
can: write a strong, specific, arguable thesis statement; structure a
full 5-paragraph argumentative essay (introduction, three body
paragraphs, conclusion); genuinely paraphrase a source idea without
copying its structure or vocabulary; use formal cohesion devices
(`moreover, furthermore, nevertheless, consequently`) to connect ideas
across paragraphs.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 2.1 ("Taking a Position — Thesis Statements & Essay
Architecture"); Lesson 2.2 ("In Other Words — Paraphrasing Without
Losing Meaning"); Module 2 Quiz (10 questions); Module 2 Assignment
("An Argumentative Essay").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** This essay will argue
that... — Moreover,.../Furthermore,... — Nevertheless,.../However,...
— Consequently,.../As a result,... — In other words,... — This
suggests/indicates that...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — formal academic
cohesion):** *moreover, furthermore* (adding a further, reinforcing
point — stronger and more formal than Level III's `as a result`);
*nevertheless* (a formal contrast marker, stronger than `however`,
often used to introduce a point that stands despite what preceded it);
*consequently* (a formal cause-effect marker) — the level's first
explicit step into genuinely academic-register cohesion, distinct from
Level III's more conversational connector sets.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *lay out [an argument]*
(present it clearly and in order), *flesh out [an idea]* (develop it
with more detail and substance), *hinge on [something]* (depend
critically on it), *boil down to [something]* (be reducible to its
essential point), *make a case for/against [something]* (argue in
favour of or opposed to it).

**BrE / AmE note:** a genuine false-friend in academic vocabulary:
British **dissertation** often refers to an extended piece of
independent research writing at undergraduate or master's level,
while American English reserves **dissertation** for doctoral-level
work and uses **thesis** for undergraduate/master's — the reverse of
what many learners might guess; both varieties use **thesis
statement** (this module's focus) identically, which is a different,
narrower sense of the word from the "long research document" meaning.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** essay-architecture vocabulary (thesis,
introduction, body paragraph, evidence, counter-argument, conclusion),
paraphrasing vocabulary (source, plagiarism, original wording,
restate). Intercultural note: the direct, thesis-first essay structure
taught here is one widely used international-academic-English
convention (and the one WEC-LC teaches for consistency and clarity),
not the only valid way to organise a persuasive argument across
academic traditions worldwide.

## Lesson 2.1 — "Taking a Position — Thesis Statements & Essay Architecture"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) write
a thesis statement that is specific and genuinely arguable (not just a
topic or an obvious fact); (2) outline a 5-paragraph essay
(introduction with a hook and thesis; three body paragraphs, each with
a topic sentence, evidence, and brief analysis; a conclusion that
restates the thesis's significance); (3) distinguish a strong thesis
from a weak one; (4) use `moreover/furthermore` to add a reinforcing
point within a paragraph.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 9 (paragraph structure,
topic sentences, basic citation awareness — this lesson formalises
that into a full multi-paragraph essay).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows three candidate "thesis"
sentences on the same topic — one too broad/obvious, one just a
topic with no position, one genuinely specific and arguable — and asks
learners to identify which is strongest and why, before formal
presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: weak — "Social media is a topic that
affects many people" (not arguable, too vague); better — "Social media
is bad" (arguable but too broad/simplistic); strong — "Social media
platforms should be required to disclose how their algorithms rank
content, because users currently cannot make informed choices about
what they see." Highlight explicitly: a strong thesis is specific,
takes a genuine position someone could reasonably disagree with, and
often previews *why* (the reasoning). Then model the 5-paragraph
skeleton on the board: **Introduction** (a hook, brief context, the
thesis) → **Body 1** (strongest point, topic sentence + evidence +
brief analysis) → **Body 2** (second point) → **Body 3** (third point,
or a counter-argument addressed) → **Conclusion** (restate the
thesis's significance, not just repeat it).

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners evaluate 8 candidate thesis
statements (a mix of weak and strong) and rewrite the weak ones to
make them specific and arguable.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners choose a topic they can
genuinely argue about and write their own thesis statement, then
outline their 5-paragraph essay skeleton (thesis + 3 body-paragraph
topic sentences + a note on the conclusion's angle), sharing with a
partner who checks the thesis is specific and arguable.

**Speaking activity.** Learners explain their essay outline aloud to
their partner in under a minute, using `moreover/furthermore` to
connect their planned body points — a spoken rehearsal of the essay's
logical structure before writing it.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think a thesis
that's too broad or too obvious makes for a weaker essay, even if
every individual sentence in the essay is grammatically correct?" —
directly interrogates the difference between grammatical correctness
and genuine argumentative quality, central to this level's "evidence
& argument quality" rubric criterion.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone outlining an essay
plan aloud (thesis + 3 points + conclusion angle) and note each part
on a simple outline worksheet.

**Reading activity — extended reading & analysis (8 min).** Read a
short 5-paragraph argumentative essay (200-220 words) on a generic
topic. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 analytical questions
("Identify the thesis. Does each body paragraph's topic sentence
genuinely support it, or does one paragraph drift off-topic?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write your own thesis statement plus one
body-paragraph topic sentence that clearly supports it.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill stress and pacing for
`moreover/furthermore/nevertheless/consequently` at the start of a
sentence — these longer, more formal connectors carry their own
stress pattern and are typically followed by a brief pause, distinct
from shorter connectors like `and/but`.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An essay-architecture vocabulary
matching game (thesis, introduction, body paragraph, evidence,
counter-argument, conclusion) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that thesis statements are
genuinely specific and arguable (not just a topic) during independent
practice — the lesson's core target skill.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their thesis statement and 3-point
outline, ready for Lesson 2.2's drafting and paraphrasing work.

**Revision.** Lesson 2.2 opens with learners briefly sharing their
finalised thesis in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a brief counter-argument
note to their outline (what someone who disagrees might say) to
address in Body 3 or the conclusion.

## Lesson 2.2 — "In Other Words — Paraphrasing Without Losing Meaning"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
genuinely paraphrase an idea by changing both wording and sentence
structure, not just swapping a few synonyms; (2) recognise when a
"paraphrase" is actually too close to the original (a form of
plagiarism, even if unintentional); (3) use `consequently/nevertheless`
correctly to connect paraphrased ideas to your own argument; (4) draft
a complete 5-paragraph argumentative essay.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 2.1 (thesis, essay architecture),
Level III Module 2 (basic paraphrasing, now developed further).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one original sentence and two
"paraphrases" — one that only swaps a few words (still too close), one
that genuinely restates the idea differently — and asks learners which
is the real paraphrase and why, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: original — "Many researchers believe
that remote work increases employee satisfaction, though the evidence
remains mixed." Weak paraphrase (too close): "Lots of researchers
think remote work increases worker satisfaction, though the proof is
mixed." Genuine paraphrase: "While the connection between remote work
and job satisfaction is not fully settled, a considerable body of
research points toward a positive link." Highlight explicitly: a
genuine paraphrase changes sentence structure and word choices
substantially while preserving the original meaning exactly — swapping
synonyms alone is not enough, and is a common, often accidental form
of plagiarism worth naming directly at this level.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 6 original sentences
and, for each, identify which of two provided "paraphrases" is
genuine and which is too close to the original, explaining their
reasoning.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners practise paraphrasing 4
short source-style sentences (provided, generic/invented, not tied to
a real publication) into genuine paraphrases, then swap with a
partner, who checks whether each paraphrase is genuinely restructured
and whether the meaning is preserved.

**Speaking activity.** Partners discuss one paraphrase each found
particularly difficult to reword without changing the meaning, and why
— a genuine reflection on the paraphrasing skill's real difficulty.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think
paraphrasing well is actually a harder skill than it first seems? What
does it require you to do that simply copying doesn't?" — connects the
mechanical skill to a genuine cognitive point: paraphrasing requires
real understanding, not just word substitution.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone paraphrasing a short
claim aloud and evaluate whether their paraphrase is genuine or too
close to a model original read first.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short original passage and one
paraphrase of it, identifying exactly what changed (structure,
vocabulary, sentence combining/splitting) while the meaning stayed the
same.

**Writing task (5 min).** Draft your essay's introduction paragraph
(hook + brief context + thesis) from Lesson 2.1's outline, incorporating
one genuine paraphrase of an idea (your own invented "source"
sentence, paraphrased).

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill clear, deliberate delivery
when reading a paraphrase aloud, differentiated from reading a direct
quotation (which would typically be marked by a brief pause and
slightly different pacing to signal "these are someone else's exact
words") — a subtle but genuine spoken-register distinction.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A paraphrase-quality card-sorting game:
sort 8 example paraphrase pairs into "genuine paraphrase" vs. "too
close to the original."

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that paraphrases
genuinely restructure the sentence (not just synonym-swap) and
preserve meaning accurately, during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners draft the remaining paragraphs of their
5-paragraph essay (body paragraphs and conclusion) for Module 2's
assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 2.1 thesis recap.
Module 2's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners paraphrase the same
sentence twice, in two genuinely different ways, comparing which
version fits their essay's tone better.

## Module 2 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-4.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. Which is the strongest, most specific and arguable thesis
   statement? — (a) Social media is a topic that affects many people.
   (b) Social media is bad. (c) Social media platforms should be
   required to disclose how their algorithms rank content, because
   users cannot currently make informed choices. ✓ (d) Many people use
   social media every day.
2. In a 5-paragraph essay, what does the conclusion typically do? —
   (a) introduce a brand new argument (b) restate the thesis's
   significance, not just repeat it ✓ (c) simply repeat the
   introduction word-for-word (d) list every source used
3. "The first point supports the thesis well. ___, the second point
   provides even stronger evidence." — (a) Moreover ✓ (b) Because (c)
   So (d) If
4. Which is a genuine paraphrase (not just synonym-swapping)? — (a)
   "Many researchers believe X" -> "Lots of researchers think X." (b)
   "Many researchers believe X, though evidence is mixed" -> "While the
   connection is not fully settled, a considerable body of research
   points toward X." ✓ (c) "Many researchers believe X" -> "Many
   researchers believe X." (d) "Many researchers believe X" -> "X is
   believed by many researchers." (word order only)
5. Swapping only a few synonyms while keeping the same sentence
   structure as the original is: — (a) always acceptable (b) a form of
   genuine paraphrasing (c) a common, often accidental form of
   plagiarism ✓ (d) required by academic convention
6. In American English, an extended piece of independent research
   writing at the doctoral level is usually called a: — (a) thesis (b)
   dissertation ✓ (c) paper (d) report
7. "The evidence is limited. ___, the argument remains worth
   considering." — (a) Consequently (b) Nevertheless ✓ (c) Moreover
   (d) Because
8. Which phrase means "present an argument clearly and in order"? —
   (a) flesh out (b) hinge on (c) lay out ✓ (d) boil down to
9. A body paragraph's topic sentence should: — (a) introduce a
   completely unrelated idea (b) clearly support the essay's thesis ✓
   (c) restate the conclusion (d) always start with "Moreover"
10. Which phrase means "depend critically on something"? — (a) flesh
    out (b) hinge on ✓ (c) lay out (d) boil down to

## Module 2 Assignment — "An Argumentative Essay"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write a full 5-paragraph
argumentative essay, 300-400 words, on a topic of your choice that you
can genuinely argue a position on. This is this level's second
writing genre — **the argumentative essay**. Your essay must include:
a clear, specific, arguable thesis statement in the introduction;
three body paragraphs, each with a topic sentence, supporting
evidence or reasoning, and brief analysis; at least one genuine
paraphrase of an idea (not synonym-swapped); at least two of this
module's formal cohesion devices (`moreover/furthermore/nevertheless/
consequently`); and a conclusion that restates the thesis's
significance.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, varied sentence structures
  appropriate to formal academic writing.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct essay-architecture or
  argument words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation
  from this module.
- **Task completion** — thesis, three supported body paragraphs, a
  genuine paraphrase, and a conclusion all present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** *(new at this level)* — is the
  thesis genuinely specific and arguable, is each body paragraph's
  evidence or reasoning actually convincing, and does the essay
  address the strongest version of the topic, not a weak or obvious
  claim?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — does the essay read as one
  connected, logically structured academic piece, with formal cohesion
  devices used correctly and register consistently formal throughout?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 2
completed for the learner.
