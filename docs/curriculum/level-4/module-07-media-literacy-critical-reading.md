# WEC-LC — Level IV, Module 7: Media Literacy & Critical Reading

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-4-upper-intermediate.md`'s Level IV build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 7, the learner
can: identify an author's purpose, bias, and tone in an authentic-
style text; recognise common rhetorical techniques and explain their
effect; infer implicit meaning and justify the inference with textual
evidence; use ellipsis and substitution to write more economical,
cohesive English; write a summary-and-critique of a media text.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 7.1 ("Reading Between the Lines — Identifying Bias, Tone &
Rhetorical Technique"); Lesson 7.2 ("It, This, One — Ellipsis &
Substitution for Cohesion"); Module 7 Quiz (10 questions); Module 7
Assignment ("A Summary-and-Critique of a Media Text").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** This implies.../The tone here
suggests... — Arguably,... — It could be inferred that... — The
writer appears to favour... — This technique is used to... — In
summary, the piece argues...; however, it overlooks...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — critical-reading
meta-language):** *this implies*, *the tone here suggests*,
*arguably*, *it could be inferred that* — hedged, analytical language
for describing what a text does and suggests, rather than simply what
it states, distinct from the module's own object of study (the
text being analysed).

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *read into [something]*
(interpret a deeper or additional meaning, sometimes more than is
warranted), *play down [an issue]* (minimise its apparent importance),
*play up [an issue]* (exaggerate its apparent importance), *gloss over
[something]* (address briefly and superficially, avoiding real
detail), *spin [a story]* (present information in a biased way to
create a favourable or unfavourable impression).

**BrE / AmE note:** **op-ed** — an American-originated term (short for
"opposite the editorial page," where such pieces traditionally
appeared) — has spread internationally, including into British media,
though British publications have traditionally been just as likely to
use **opinion piece** or **comment piece** for the same genre; all
three terms are now widely understood internationally.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** media-analysis vocabulary (bias, tone,
rhetoric, framing, loaded language, objectivity), cohesion vocabulary
(ellipsis, substitution, antecedent, reference). Intercultural note:
what counts as "neutral" versus "biased" media, and how much overt
opinion is considered acceptable in supposedly factual reporting,
varies by media tradition and country; this module teaches
transferable analytical *tools* (identifying bias, tone, technique),
not a claim that any particular media tradition is the objective
standard.

## Lesson 7.1 — "Reading Between the Lines — Identifying Bias, Tone & Rhetorical Technique"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
identify an author's likely purpose and bias in a text; (2) describe a
text's tone using precise vocabulary, not just "positive/negative"; (3)
recognise common rhetorical techniques (rhetorical questions,
repetition, appeals to emotion or authority) and explain their
intended effect; (4) infer implicit meaning and justify it with
specific textual evidence.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 6 (basic media-
literacy evaluation checklist — this lesson develops that into full
rhetorical and tonal analysis).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor reads two short opening sentences on
the same event, written in noticeably different tones (one neutral,
one clearly favourable or unfavourable), and asks learners to
describe the difference in one word each, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model a short analysis aloud: "This piece
uses the rhetorical question 'Can we really afford to wait?' to create
a sense of urgency — the tone here suggests real alarm, not neutral
reporting. The writer appears to favour immediate action, and this is
reinforced by the repeated phrase 'time is running out.'" Highlight
explicitly: **purpose** (to inform, persuade, entertain, or some
combination); **bias** (a leaning revealed through word choice,
selective facts, or framing — not necessarily dishonest, but present);
**tone** (the emotional colouring of the language — urgent, sceptical,
celebratory, dismissive); **rhetorical technique** (a deliberate
device used to persuade or engage, such as a rhetorical question,
repetition, or an appeal to authority/emotion) — four distinct,
namable things to look for, not a single vague "is this good or bad"
judgement.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 3 short text excerpts
(generic, invented, representing different tones/biases) and identify
the purpose, tone, and at least one rhetorical technique in each,
using this lesson's analytical vocabulary.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners choose one excerpt and
write a short analysis (4-5 sentences) identifying its purpose, tone,
bias, and one rhetorical technique with its intended effect, then
compare their analysis with a partner's.

**Speaking activity.** The partner comparison above, followed by a
brief whole-class discussion of any excerpt where analyses genuinely
differed — surfacing that skilled critical reading can still produce
reasonable disagreement, not just one "correct" answer.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is it possible for a text
to be biased and still be honest and useful? Or does bias always mean
you should distrust a source?" — a genuinely sophisticated B2
question resisting the oversimplification that "biased" automatically
means "untrustworthy."

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short persuasive speech
excerpt (6-7 sentences) and identify its tone and at least one
rhetorical technique used.

**Reading activity — extended reading & analysis (8 min).** Read a
longer authentic-style opinion piece (200-220 words) on a generic
topic. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 analytical questions
("Identify the writer's likely bias. What specific word choices or
selected facts support your answer?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short analytical paragraph (5-6
sentences) identifying the purpose, tone, and one rhetorical technique
in a text of your choice (from class materials or elsewhere).

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill analytical, evaluative
intonation when describing a text's tone or bias aloud — a measured,
slightly detached delivery appropriate to objective analysis, distinct
from the more animated intonation of persuasive speaking itself
(Module 4/6).

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A tone-vocabulary expansion game:
learners sort 12 precise tone words (urgent, sceptical, celebratory,
dismissive, cautious, indignant, and others) by rough emotional
"temperature," moving beyond simple positive/negative labels.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that analyses name
specific textual evidence (word choices, structure, selected facts),
not just vague impressions, during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners find (or recall) a short real text excerpt
(an article, an opinion piece, an advertisement) and bring notes on
its purpose, tone, and one technique, ready for Lesson 7.2's
summary-and-critique work.

**Revision.** Lesson 7.2 opens with learners briefly sharing their
homework text's tone in one word.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners identify a second
rhetorical technique in the same homework text and compare its effect
to the first.

## Lesson 7.2 — "It, This, One — Ellipsis & Substitution for Cohesion"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
ellipsis correctly to omit repeated words where meaning stays clear;
(2) use substitution (`one/ones, so/not, do so`) to avoid repeating a
noun or clause; (3) recognise ellipsis and substitution when reading,
correctly identifying what's been omitted or replaced; (4) write a
concise, cohesive summary-and-critique of a media text.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 7.1 (critical-reading analysis),
Level III Module 2 (summarising basics — this lesson adds the
grammatical tools for genuinely economical summary writing).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one repetitive sentence pair
("She wanted to go to the concert, but she couldn't go to the
concert") and one economical version using ellipsis ("She wanted to
go to the concert, but she couldn't"), asking learners what was
removed and why it still makes sense, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model **ellipsis**: "She wanted to go, but
couldn't [go]" (the repeated verb phrase is omitted because it's
recoverable from context). Model **substitution**: "I liked the first
article, but not the second one" (`one` substitutes for the repeated
noun); "He said the report was biased, and I think so too" (`so`
substitutes for the whole reported clause); "She read the article
carefully, and he did so too" (`do so` substitutes for a repeated
verb phrase). Highlight explicitly: both ellipsis and substitution
avoid clunky repetition and are hallmarks of genuinely fluent,
cohesive written English — and recognising them is essential for
reading dense academic or journalistic text accurately, since a
reader must correctly identify what an omitted or substituted element
actually refers to.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners rewrite 8 repetitive sentence
pairs using ellipsis or substitution as appropriate, then identify
the antecedent (what's being referred to) in 4 given
ellipsis/substitution examples.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write a first-draft
summary (4-5 sentences) of their Lesson 7.1 homework text, deliberately
using at least 2 instances of ellipsis or substitution to keep it
economical, then swap with a partner, who checks whether the omitted/
substituted meaning is still clear.

**Speaking activity.** Partners discuss one place where ellipsis or
substitution in their summary could be ambiguous, and revise it
together — a direct check on the real risk of the technique
(unclear reference) alongside its benefit (economy).

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think overusing
ellipsis or substitution in a written summary could actually make it
harder to understand, even though each individual technique makes a
sentence shorter?" — a genuinely useful stylistic caution, balancing
the lesson's tools against the risk of overuse.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short spoken summary using
ellipsis and substitution naturally, and identify what each instance
refers back to.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written summary-and-
critique example and identify its use of ellipsis/substitution, its
summary section, and its critique section.

**Writing task (5 min).** Revise your independent-practice summary
into a full summary-and-critique: add 2-3 sentences evaluating the
original text's purpose, tone, and at least one rhetorical technique,
using Lesson 7.1's analytical vocabulary.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill natural sentence rhythm
around ellipsis and substitution in spoken English — these
constructions often carry a brief pause or slight stress shift onto
the substituted word (`the first ONE`) to signal the omission/
substitution clearly to a listener.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An ellipsis/substitution identification
game: learners find and mark all instances of ellipsis and
substitution in a short provided text.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that ellipsis/substitution
is used correctly (clear antecedent, not ambiguous) and that the
critique section engages with Lesson 7.1's analytical categories,
during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their summary-and-critique for
Module 7's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 7.1 tone-word recap.
Module 7's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners identify one place in a
provided dense academic-style text where they had to pause and work
out what an ellipsis or substitution referred to, discussing why it
was momentarily unclear.

## Module 7 Assignment — "A Summary-and-Critique of a Media Text"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write a summary-and-critique,
250-300 words, of a real or realistic media text (an article, opinion
piece, or advertisement) of your choice. This is this level's sixth
writing genre — the **summary-and-critique**. Structure it in two
clear parts: **a summary** (3-4 sentences, genuinely condensed,
using at least 2 instances of ellipsis or substitution for
economy); and **a critique** (5-6 sentences, identifying the text's
purpose, tone, at least one specific bias or rhetorical technique with
its intended effect, and your own reasoned evaluation of whether the
text is persuasive, fair, or misleading).

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, clear ellipsis and substitution
  use (no ambiguous reference).
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct media-analysis words used
  correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — a genuine summary and a genuine critique, both
  present and clearly distinguished.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the critique's identification
  of bias/technique supported by specific textual evidence (not just
  asserted), and is the overall evaluation reasoned rather than a bare
  opinion?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the summary genuinely
  condensed (not just a shortened copy), and is the critique's
  register appropriately analytical and measured throughout?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 7
completed for the learner.
