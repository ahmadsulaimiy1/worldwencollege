# WEC — Level V, Module 3: Leadership & Persuasion

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 3, the learner
can: use inversion correctly and naturally for rhetorical emphasis;
reframe an issue persuasively using framing language; deliver a
leadership speech with a clear vision, rationale, and call to action;
negotiate as a decision-maker under real stakes; write a position
paper that persuasively argues and defends a clear stance.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 3.1 ("Rarely Have I Seen... — Inversion for Rhetorical
Emphasis"); Lesson 3.2 ("Leading the Room — Framing, Persuasion &
Advanced Negotiation"); Module 3 Quiz (10 questions); Module 3
Assignment ("A Position Paper & Leadership Speech").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** Rarely have I seen... — Not
only did..., but... — Only then did... — Under no circumstances
should... — This isn't just about X, it's about Y. — What's really at
stake here is...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — reframing and
raising stakes):** *this isn't just about X, it's about Y*, *what's
really at stake here is*, *the real question is* — persuasive framing
language that shifts how an audience perceives an issue's importance
or scope, a genuinely powerful leadership-communication technique
distinct from simple argument-and-evidence.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *rally behind [a
cause]* (unite in support of it), *win over [an audience]* (persuade
them to support your view), *drive home [a point]* (emphasise it so
it's fully understood and remembered), *set the tone* (establish the
mood or standard for what follows), *take the lead (on something)*
(assume responsibility and initiative).

**BrE / AmE note:** company-naming conventions differ: British
companies commonly append **Plc** (Public Limited Company) or **Ltd**
(Limited) to a company name, while American companies commonly append
**Corp./Inc.** (Corporation/Incorporated) — genuinely useful to
recognise when reading business writing from either variety, since
these suffixes signal comparable but not identical legal company
structures.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** leadership vocabulary (vision, mandate,
stakeholder buy-in, accountability, conviction), persuasion vocabulary
(framing, appeal, resonate, credibility, momentum). Intercultural
note: leadership communication style — how much emotional appeal
versus data-driven argument is expected, how directive versus
consultative a leader's language should sound — varies significantly
by culture and organisational context; this module's techniques are
presented as a flexible toolkit, not a single "correct" leadership
voice.

## Lesson 3.1 — "Rarely Have I Seen... — Inversion for Rhetorical Emphasis"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
inversion correctly after a negative/limiting adverbial (`Rarely have
I..., Never before has..., Only then did..., Under no circumstances
should...`); (2) form `Not only... but also...` inversion correctly;
(3) use inversion deliberately for rhetorical emphasis in persuasive
speech and writing; (4) recognise when inversion strengthens a
sentence versus when it sounds forced or overused.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level IV, Module 2 (formal academic
register — inversion is a further, more marked step into formal,
rhetorical register).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one plain sentence and its
inverted, more emphatic equivalent ("I have rarely seen such
dedication" vs. "Rarely have I seen such dedication") and asks
learners which sounds more forceful and why, before formal
presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "Rarely have I seen a team respond
to a challenge with such resolve. Not only did we meet the target, but
we exceeded it by a considerable margin. Only then did we realise the
full scale of what we'd achieved. Under no circumstances should we
lose sight of what got us here." Highlight explicitly: inversion
(auxiliary before subject, as in a question, but used in a statement)
follows a negative or limiting adverbial placed at the front of the
sentence for emphasis — a genuinely marked, formal, rhetorical
structure, appropriate to a leadership speech's key moments, not
routine narration; overusing it across an entire speech would sound
stilted, so it works best reserved for a small number of genuinely
emphatic points.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 plain sentences into
their inverted, emphatic equivalents, checking correct auxiliary
placement for each of the four patterns modelled.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 4-5 inverted
sentences about a real or invented achievement or turning point (a
team success, a personal milestone), then read them aloud to a
partner, who rates how emphatic/natural each one sounds.

**Speaking activity.** The partner rating exchange above, followed by
a brief whole-class share of the single most effective inverted
sentence per pair, with the class discussing why it worked.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think a
rhetorical device like inversion is more effective when used sparingly
rather than throughout an entire speech? What happens to its impact if
overused?" — a genuinely sophisticated C1 question about rhetorical
economy, directly relevant to the module's persuasive-writing focus.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short speech excerpt (6-7
sentences, including 2-3 inverted sentences) and identify each
inversion and the emphasis it creates.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read a short speech transcript excerpt (150-180 words) using inversion
for emphasis. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 evaluative questions
("Does the inversion in this speech feel earned and effective, or
overused? At which specific moment does it work best, and why?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 3-4 inverted sentences suitable for a
short leadership speech on a topic of your choice.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill strong sentence stress on
the fronted adverbial in an inverted sentence ("RARELY have I seen...")
and a brief, deliberate pause immediately after it — the intonation
pattern that makes inversion land as genuinely emphatic rather than
merely grammatically unusual.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An inversion-pattern matching game:
match 8 fronted adverbials (rarely, never before, not only, under no
circumstances, only then, seldom, on no account, not until) to
correctly inverted sentence completions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct inversion
formation across all four patterns and, more importantly, whether
learners can judge when it strengthens versus overloads a sentence,
during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a real or invented cause, initiative, or
position they'd want to advocate for as a leader, and jot down 2-3
reasons, ready for Lesson 3.2's speech and negotiation work.

**Revision.** Lesson 3.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
homework cause in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a `Seldom` or `Not
until` inverted sentence as an additional pattern, checking the
auxiliary placement remains correct.

## Lesson 3.2 — "Leading the Room — Framing, Persuasion & Advanced Negotiation"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
reframe an issue persuasively, shifting its perceived scope or
importance; (2) structure and deliver a short leadership speech
(vision, rationale, call to action); (3) negotiate as a decision-maker
under genuine competing pressures, not just a peer-to-peer exchange;
(4) write a position paper that persuasively argues and defends a
clear stance.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 3.1 (inversion), Level IV Module 8
(negotiation and diplomatic disagreement — this lesson raises the
stakes to leadership-level decision-making).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one issue framed narrowly
("This is a scheduling problem") and then reframed more broadly
("This isn't just about scheduling — it's about whether we can deliver
on our promises to our clients"), asking learners which framing feels
more compelling and why, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model framing language explicitly: "This
isn't just about budget — it's about whether we're serious about
growth. What's really at stake here is our credibility with every
stakeholder we've made commitments to." Then model a short leadership
speech structure: **vision** (a compelling statement of what could be
true), **rationale** (why this matters now, with reasoning), **call to
action** (a specific, clear ask of the audience). Then extend Level
IV's negotiation model to leadership stakes: a leader negotiating
must often balance competing stakeholder interests, not just two
sides' preferences — model briefly acknowledging multiple
stakeholders' concerns before proposing a path forward ("I recognise
this affects the team's workload, our timeline, and our client
commitments — here's how I'd propose balancing those").

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners practise reframing 4 narrowly-
stated issues into more compelling, higher-stakes framings, then
practise the vision-rationale-call-to-action structure on a
provided prompt in pairs.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 3.1 homework
cause, learners develop a short leadership speech (vision, rationale,
call to action) including at least one inverted sentence and one
reframing statement, and rehearse it once.

**Speaking activity — leadership speech & negotiation.** Learners
deliver their leadership speech to a small group (or the class), who
then role-play as stakeholders with competing concerns; the speaker
negotiates a path forward, acknowledging multiple stakeholder
interests explicitly — this lesson's core task, and the direct basis
for Module 3's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is reframing an issue to
make it feel more significant always an honest rhetorical technique,
or can it become manipulative? Where do you think the line is?" — a
genuinely important ethical question about persuasive technique,
appropriate to this level's move into intellectual and rhetorical
sophistication.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short leadership speech
excerpt and identify its vision, rationale, and call to action.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written position statement
that uses framing language, and identify how the issue is framed and
what effect that framing has on the reader.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write your leadership speech's vision and
call-to-action statements as clean written text (2-3 sentences each).

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill confident, inspiring
delivery for a leadership speech's vision statement (wider pitch
range, stronger stress on key aspirational words) versus more measured,
grounded delivery for the rationale section — a genuine register-
shifting skill within a single speech.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A leadership/persuasion vocabulary
matching game (vision, mandate, stakeholder buy-in, accountability,
conviction, framing, resonate, credibility) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for a genuine three-part
speech structure, effective reframing, and acknowledgement of multiple
stakeholder interests during the negotiation, during the speaking
activity.

**Homework.** Learners draft their position paper using their
leadership speech's content as a starting point, ready for Module 3's
assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 3.1 cause-naming
recap. Module 3's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a second reframing
statement addressing a different stakeholder's likely objection,
practising audience-specific persuasive framing.

## Module 3 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-5.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "Rarely ___ such dedication." (inversion) — (a) I have seen (b)
   have I seen ✓ (c) I seen (d) did I saw
2. "Not only ___ the target, but we exceeded it." (inversion) — (a) we
   met (b) did we meet ✓ (c) we did meet (d) met we
3. "Under no circumstances ___ we lose sight of this." — (a) we
   should (b) should we ✓ (c) we shall (d) shall we
4. When is inversion for rhetorical emphasis most effective? — (a)
   used in every single sentence (b) used sparingly, at genuinely
   emphatic moments ✓ (c) never used in speeches (d) only used in
   casual conversation
5. "This isn't just about budget -- it's about whether we're serious
   about growth." This is an example of: — (a) inversion (b) framing
   language ✓ (c) a citation (d) a mixed conditional
6. A leadership speech commonly follows which structure? — (a)
   conclusion, rationale, vision (b) vision, rationale, call to action
   ✓ (c) call to action only (d) no particular structure
7. In British business writing, a company name is often followed by:
   — (a) Corp. (b) Inc. (c) Plc ✓ (d) LLC
8. Which phrase means "emphasise a point so it's fully understood and
   remembered"? — (a) rally behind (b) win over (c) drive home ✓ (d)
   set the tone
9. When negotiating as a leader with multiple stakeholders, an
   effective approach is to: — (a) ignore competing interests (b)
   acknowledge multiple stakeholders' concerns before proposing a path
   forward ✓ (c) only address the loudest stakeholder (d) avoid
   proposing any solution
10. Which phrase means "unite in support of a cause"? — (a) win over
    (b) rally behind ✓ (c) drive home (d) take the lead

## Module 3 Assignment — "A Position Paper & Leadership Speech"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Complete two parts on one real
or invented cause, initiative, or position you'd advocate for as a
leader. **Part A (writing, this level's third genre): a position
paper**, 350-450 words, that clearly states your position, argues for
it with reasoning and framing language, acknowledges at least one
competing stakeholder concern, and closes with a clear call to action.
Use at least 2 inverted sentences for emphasis. **Part B (speaking):**
Record yourself (or perform live) a leadership speech, 90 seconds to 2
minutes, based on your position paper, with a clear vision, rationale,
and call to action, including at least one reframing statement.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct inversion formation, used
  appropriately and not overused.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct leadership/persuasion
  words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — position, reasoning, framing language, a
  stakeholder acknowledgement, and a call to action all present in
  Part A; vision, rationale, call to action, and a reframing statement
  in Part B.
- **Rhetorical effectiveness** — does the paper and speech genuinely
  persuade, using framing and emphasis effectively rather than
  mechanically inserting the required elements?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register appropriately
  authoritative and persuasive throughout, and does the speech's
  delivery reinforce (not undercut) the written paper's argument?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 3
completed for the learner.
