# WEC-LC — Level V, Module 8: Professional Advocacy

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 8, the learner
can: use advanced passive constructions to build a formal, credible
case; apply crisis-communication principles under pressure; defend a
position in a panel discussion against direct, pointed challenge while
staying composed; write a strategic proposal that builds and defends a
case for a course of action.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 8.1 ("The Case Has Been Made... — Advanced Passive Under Formal
Pressure"); Lesson 8.2 ("Responding Under Pressure — Crisis
Communication & Panel Discussion Under Challenge"); Module 8 Quiz (10
questions); Module 8 Assignment ("A Strategic Proposal & Crisis
Response").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** It has been established
that... — The claim is supported by... — Concerns have been raised
and must be addressed. — We are aware of the situation and are taking
the following steps... — The evidence clearly shows... — Crucially,...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — advocacy
emphasis):** *the evidence clearly shows*, *it is well established
that*, *the case rests on*, *crucially* — language for building and
signalling the strongest points of a formal case, distinct from the
hedging language of Module 4 (this module's context — advocacy —
calls for confident, well-supported assertion, not tentative
qualification).

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *stand by [a claim/
decision]* (continue to support it, especially under challenge), *back
down (from a position)* (concede or retreat, sometimes under
pressure), *hold one's ground* (maintain a position firmly despite
challenge), *own up to [a mistake]* (admit it honestly, a genuine
crisis-communication skill), *get ahead of [a story/issue]* (address a
problem proactively before it escalates).

**BrE / AmE note:** the legal profession is structured differently by
name: British English distinguishes **barrister** (who represents
clients in court, especially higher courts) from **solicitor** (who
handles legal advice, documentation, and preparation, typically
outside court) — two distinct professional roles; American English
uses **lawyer** or **attorney** for both functions, without this
formal split — a genuinely useful distinction to know when reading or
discussing British professional/legal contexts.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** advocacy vocabulary (case, claim,
burden of proof, credibility, corroboration), crisis-communication
vocabulary (transparency, accountability, mitigation, stakeholder
trust, reputational risk). Intercultural note: expectations around how
directly an organisation should acknowledge fault during a crisis, and
how much legal caution should shape public communication, vary by
country, industry, and legal system; this module teaches transparent,
honest crisis communication as WEC-LC's own recommended standard, not
a claim about universal practice.

## Lesson 8.1 — "The Case Has Been Made... — Advanced Passive Under Formal Pressure"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
advanced passive constructions to build a formal, credible case; (2)
distinguish confident, well-supported assertion from either
overclaiming or excessive hedging (a genuine register shift from
Module 4); (3) structure a case with a clear claim, supporting
evidence, and an anticipated counter-argument addressed; (4) recognise
when passive voice strengthens advocacy writing versus when active
voice would be more direct and forceful.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level IV, Module 5 (passive voice II —
this lesson applies it specifically to formal advocacy register, a
distinct communicative purpose from academic/scientific reporting).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor presents one claim stated tentatively
and the same claim stated with confident, evidence-backed passive
constructions ("Some people think this might work" vs. "It has been
established, through extensive review, that this approach is
effective"), asking learners which sounds more credible in a formal
advocacy context, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "It has been established that the
proposed approach reduces costs significantly. The claim is supported
by independent analysis conducted over the past year. Concerns have
been raised about implementation timelines, and these must be
addressed directly, not dismissed." Highlight explicitly: advanced
passive here builds credibility by foregrounding the *claim and its
support* rather than the arguer — genuinely persuasive in formal
advocacy, distinct from Module 4's cautious hedging register, though
still requiring genuine evidence behind it, not passive voice used to
disguise a weak claim as an established fact.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 active, tentative
sentences into confident, passive-supported advocacy statements,
checking that each remains honestly proportionate to genuine (even if
invented, for practice) supporting evidence.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 sentences
building a case for a real or invented proposal, using advanced
passive constructions confidently, then read one to a partner, who
evaluates whether it sounds credibly supported or overclaimed.

**Speaking activity.** The partner evaluation exchange above, followed
by a brief whole-class discussion of the line between confident
advocacy and overclaiming.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Where do you think the
line is between confidently advocating for a position and
overstating the evidence behind it? Is this always easy to judge from
the outside?" — a genuinely important ethical/rhetorical question,
directly relevant to professional advocacy's real-world stakes.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short formal case
statement (6-7 sentences, using advanced passive) and evaluate whether
its claims sound proportionate to the evidence cited.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read a short strategic-proposal-style excerpt (180-200 words) building
a case using advanced passive constructions. Answer 2 literal
questions and 2 evaluative questions ("Identify one claim that sounds
well-supported and one that sounds potentially overclaimed. What's the
difference in how each is phrased?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 sentences building a case for a
real or invented proposal, using advanced passive constructions
confidently and proportionately.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill confident, level, credible
delivery for advocacy statements — steady pacing and clear stress on
key evidence words, avoiding both an aggressive, overselling tone and
an apologetic, undermining one.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An advocacy-vocabulary matching game
(case, claim, burden of proof, credibility, corroboration) with simple
definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct advanced passive
formation and genuinely proportionate, credible (not overclaimed)
advocacy statements during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a real or invented proposal they'd
advocate for professionally, and jot down its core claim and 2-3
supporting points, ready for Lesson 8.2's crisis-communication and
panel-discussion work.

**Revision.** Lesson 8.2 opens with learners briefly stating their
homework proposal's core claim.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one sentence explicitly
addressing an anticipated counter-argument using advanced passive
("It might be objected that..., but this has been addressed by...").

## Lesson 8.2 — "Responding Under Pressure — Crisis Communication & Panel Discussion Under Challenge"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) apply
core crisis-communication principles (acknowledge, provide facts, show
accountability and action, avoid speculation); (2) own a mistake or
problem honestly without either over-apologising or deflecting; (3)
defend a position in a panel discussion against direct, pointed
challenge while staying composed; (4) write a strategic proposal that
builds and defends a case.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 8.1 (advanced passive, advocacy
register), Level IV Module 4 (advanced concession language — this
lesson applies composed defence under real pressure, not just
structured debate).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor presents one poorly handled crisis
response (defensive, evasive, speculative) and one well-handled one
(acknowledging, factual, action-oriented) on the same invented
scenario, asking learners to identify the specific differences, before
formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model crisis-communication principles
explicitly: **acknowledge** (`We are aware of the situation`) — not
denying or minimising; **provide facts** (`Here is what we currently
know...`) — avoiding speculation about what isn't yet confirmed;
**show accountability and action** (`We are taking the following
steps...`) — genuine ownership, not vague reassurance; **avoid
over-promising** — commit only to what can genuinely be delivered.
Then model panel discussion under challenge: a panellist maintains
their position calmly under a direct, pointed question (`How do you
respond to critics who say this approach has already failed?`),
using `stand by/hold one's ground` language while still genuinely
engaging with the substance of the challenge, not just repeating the
original claim unchanged.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners practise responding to 4
crisis scenarios (prompt cards) using the four-part crisis-
communication structure, checking each response avoids speculation and
genuinely commits to action.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 8.1 homework
proposal, learners prepare for a panel discussion: 2-3 anticipated
challenging questions and composed, substantive responses that hold
their ground without becoming defensive.

**Speaking activity — panel discussion under challenge.** Learners
present their proposal to a small panel (their classmates), who pose
genuinely challenging questions; the presenter responds under real
time pressure, staying composed and substantive — this lesson's core
task, and the direct basis for Module 8's assignment's speaking
element. A brief crisis-communication roleplay (a separate short
scenario) follows, applying the four-part structure live.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is it always right to
'hold your ground' when challenged, or is there a point where
genuinely reconsidering your position is the more credible response?
How would you tell the difference in the moment?" — directly connects
back to Level IV's concession-language work, asking learners to judge
when composure should give way to genuine reconsideration.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short crisis-communication
response and evaluate whether it follows the four-part structure
(acknowledge, facts, accountability, no over-promising).

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written crisis statement
and a panel-discussion exchange under challenge, identifying strong
and weak moments in each.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short crisis-communication statement
(5-6 sentences) for an invented scenario, following the four-part
structure.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill calm, steady intonation
under pressure — critical for both crisis communication and panel
challenge — avoiding a rising, defensive pitch or a flat, dismissive
one, staying level and genuinely engaged throughout.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A crisis-communication vocabulary
matching game (transparency, accountability, mitigation, stakeholder
trust, reputational risk) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine application
of the four-part crisis structure and composed, substantive (not
defensive or evasive) responses under panel challenge.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their strategic proposal, incorporating
their panel discussion's strongest points and responses, ready for
Module 8's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 8.1 proposal recap.
Module 8's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners prepare a crisis-
communication response to a genuinely difficult, realistic scenario of
their own invention, applying all four principles under a
self-imposed time constraint.

## Module 8 Assignment — "A Strategic Proposal & Crisis Response"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Complete two parts. **Part A
(writing, this level's ninth genre): a strategic proposal**, 400-500
words, building and defending a case for a real or invented course of
action. Use advanced passive constructions confidently and
proportionately (at least 3), address at least one anticipated
counter-argument, and use at least 2 advocacy-emphasis discourse
markers from this module. **Part B (speaking):** Record yourself (or
perform live) two short responses: (1) a panel-discussion response to
a genuinely challenging question about your proposal, holding your
ground while substantively engaging with the challenge; (2) a brief
crisis-communication statement (60-90 seconds) for an invented
scenario, following the four-part structure (acknowledge, facts,
accountability, no over-promising).

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct advanced passive formation.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct advocacy/crisis-
  communication words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/
  collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — a claim, evidence, a counter-argument
  addressed, and advocacy markers in Part A; a substantive panel
  response and a complete four-part crisis statement in Part B.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the proposal's case genuinely
  proportionate to its stated evidence (not overclaimed), and does the
  panel response genuinely engage with the challenge rather than
  deflecting?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register consistently
  confident, formal, and credible throughout Part A, and does Part B's
  delivery stay composed and substantive under simulated pressure?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 8
completed for the learner.
