# AIPC — Level V, Module 5: Cross-Cultural Communication

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 5, the learner
can: use a graded range of politeness modality precisely, from
neutral to highly indirect; recognise indirect speech acts (what's
implied, not just literally said); apply positive- and negative-
politeness strategies appropriately; negotiate diplomatically across
different cultural communication norms; write an analytical paper
examining a cross-cultural communication case study.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 5.1 ("Would It Be Possible To... — Advanced Modality for
Politeness & Pragmatics"); Lesson 5.2 ("Reading the Cultural
Context — Politeness Strategies & Intercultural Negotiation"); Module
5 Quiz (10 questions); Module 5 Assignment ("An Analytical Paper —
A Cross-Cultural Communication Case Study").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** Would it be possible to...? —
I wonder if you might... — I don't suppose you could...? — If it's
not too much trouble,... — Might I suggest...? — I don't want to
impose, but...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — graded
indirectness):** *would it be possible to*, *I wonder if you might*, *I
don't suppose you could*, *if it's not too much trouble* — a genuine
scale of politeness modality, each phrase signalling a different
degree of deference and indirectness for the same underlying request,
distinct from earlier levels' simpler polite-request forms.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *smooth over [a
misunderstanding]* (resolve tension diplomatically), *bridge the gap
(between cultures/perspectives)*, *lose something in translation*
(have meaning or nuance fail to carry across languages or contexts),
*get off on the wrong foot* (start an interaction badly), *find common
ground* (identify shared interests or values despite differences).

**BrE / AmE note:** British English uses **cheers** informally to mean
"thank you" (alongside its more universal use meaning "goodbye" or as
a drinking toast) — a usage that would sound distinctly unusual from
an American speaker, who would say "thanks" in the same situation;
recognising this avoids momentary confusion about what's actually
being expressed.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** pragmatics vocabulary (implicature,
indirect speech act, face, politeness strategy, deference), cross-
cultural vocabulary (norm, convention, high-context/low-context
communication, cultural competence). Intercultural note: this entire
module is, by design, about the fact that directness/indirectness
norms, how much small talk precedes business, and how much explicit
disagreement is acceptable all vary significantly by culture — the
module's own explicit content, not a caveat appended to it.

## Lesson 5.1 — "Would It Be Possible To... — Advanced Modality for Politeness & Pragmatics"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use a
graded range of politeness modality accurately, from neutral to highly
indirect; (2) recognise an indirect speech act — understanding what's
actually being requested or implied, not just the literal words; (3)
respond appropriately to an indirect request; (4) choose the right
degree of politeness modality for a given social/professional
distance.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level II, Module 5 (basic polite requests)
— this lesson develops that foundation into a full graded system.

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor says "It's quite cold in here" in a
meeting-room context and asks learners what the speaker is actually,
indirectly asking for (someone to close a window or adjust the
heating), before formal presentation — surfacing the gap between
literal meaning and communicative intent.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model a graded politeness scale: "Close the
window" (a bare imperative, direct) → "Could you close the window?"
(a standard polite request) → "Would it be possible to close the
window?" (more indirect/deferential) → "I don't suppose you could
close the window, could you?" (highly indirect, very deferential) →
"It's quite cold in here" (an indirect speech act — no request form
at all, but a clear implied one). Highlight explicitly: choosing the
right degree of indirectness depends on social distance, power
relationship, and the size of the imposition — a genuinely
sophisticated pragmatic judgement, not just a grammar choice.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 8 scenarios varying
in social distance/imposition and choose the most appropriate
politeness level for a request in each, justifying their choice.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 requests at
different points on the politeness scale for 5 different scenarios
(a close colleague, a new client, a senior executive, a stranger, a
close friend), then compare choices with a partner, discussing any
disagreement about appropriate directness.

**Speaking activity.** The partner comparison above, followed by a
brief whole-class discussion of one scenario where politeness-level
judgement genuinely varied between pairs.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think being too
indirect can sometimes cause as much miscommunication as being too
direct? Can you think of an example where an indirect request was
simply missed?" — a genuinely sophisticated C1 question about the real
communicative risk of indirectness, not just its politeness benefit.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short exchange containing
an indirect speech act (6-7 sentences) and identify what's actually
being requested or implied beneath the literal words.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read a short workplace-communication excerpt (150-180 words)
containing several requests at different politeness levels. Answer 2
literal questions and 2 evaluative questions ("Is the politeness level
used in each request well-matched to the apparent social distance and
size of imposition? Where, if anywhere, does it seem mismatched?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write the same request at three different
politeness levels for three different addressees (a close colleague,
a new business contact, a senior executive).

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill rising, tentative intonation
for highly indirect requests ("I don't suppose you could...?") versus
more level, confident intonation for standard polite requests — a
genuine prosodic marker of politeness degree, not just word choice.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A politeness-scale ordering game: order
9 request forms from most direct to most indirect.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that politeness-level
choices are well-justified by social distance/imposition reasoning,
not arbitrary, during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners think of one real or invented cross-cultural
communication misunderstanding (a genuine or plausible scenario, kept
generic and non-stereotyping) and jot down what happened, ready for
Lesson 5.2's analysis and negotiation work.

**Revision.** Lesson 5.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
homework scenario.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one indirect speech act
of their own invention and have a partner guess its intended meaning.

## Lesson 5.2 — "Reading the Cultural Context — Politeness Strategies & Intercultural Negotiation"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
distinguish positive politeness (building rapport/solidarity) from
negative politeness (respecting autonomy, minimising imposition); (2)
recognise how directness norms vary across cultural communication
styles, without stereotyping; (3) negotiate diplomatically, adapting
your communication style to your counterpart's apparent expectations;
(4) analyse a cross-cultural communication case study critically.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 5.1 (politeness modality), Level IV
Module 8 (negotiation and diplomatic disagreement — this lesson adds
cross-cultural adaptability to that foundation).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor models two different ways of starting
a business meeting — one jumping straight to the agenda, one opening
with extended relationship-building small talk — and asks learners
which might be expected in different professional contexts they know
of, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the positive/negative politeness
distinction explicitly: **positive politeness** emphasises shared
interests, compliments, and inclusion ("We're all in this together —
I really value your input here"); **negative politeness** emphasises
respecting the other person's autonomy and minimising imposition ("I
don't want to take up too much of your time, but..."). Both are
genuinely polite, just oriented differently — and which is expected
more strongly varies by context and culture. Then model intercultural
negotiation language: explicitly checking understanding across
potential communication-style gaps ("I want to make sure I've
understood your position correctly — could you confirm...?"), and
flexibly adapting register/directness based on a counterpart's
apparent style, rather than assuming one's own default style is
universal.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners identify 6 example utterances
as primarily positive-politeness or negative-politeness strategies,
discussing what each is designed to achieve.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In pairs, learners roleplay a short
negotiation where each person is assigned a different communication
style preference (one more direct, one more indirect/relationship-
focused) and must adapt to reach a working agreement, explicitly
checking understanding at least once.

**Speaking activity — intercultural negotiation.** The negotiation
roleplay above, extended to a full 90-second-2-minute exchange,
observed by a third learner who notes moments of adaptation and any
remaining friction — this lesson's core task, and the direct basis
for Module 5's assignment's negotiation element.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is it the responsibility
of one person to adapt to another's communication style in a
cross-cultural interaction, or should both sides meet somewhere in the
middle? Does it depend on the context?" — a genuinely important,
non-trivial question about intercultural communication ethics and
practice.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short intercultural
negotiation exchange and identify one moment of successful adaptation
and one moment of potential friction or miscommunication.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written cross-cultural
communication case study (generic, invented, respectfully framed) and
identify the miscommunication, its likely cause, and how it might have
been avoided.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short analysis (5-6 sentences) of
your Lesson 5.1 homework scenario, identifying the likely
communication-style factors involved.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill flexible register-shifting
mid-conversation — moving fluidly between a more relationship-focused,
warmer delivery and a more efficient, task-focused one as a negotiation
progresses, mirroring real adaptive communication.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A pragmatics vocabulary matching game
(implicature, indirect speech act, face, politeness strategy,
deference) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine adaptive
behaviour (not just one fixed style) and explicit understanding-
checking during the negotiation roleplay.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their case-study analysis for Module
5's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 5.1 scenario recap.
Module 5's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners propose one concrete
communication-style adjustment that could have prevented their case
study's miscommunication, going beyond simply describing what went
wrong.

## Module 5 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-5.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. Which is the most indirect, highly deferential request? — (a)
   Close the window. (b) Could you close the window? (c) I don't
   suppose you could close the window, could you? ✓ (d) Window,
   please.
2. "It's quite cold in here" said in a meeting is an example of: — (a)
   a direct imperative (b) an indirect speech act ✓ (c) a formal
   citation (d) a mixed conditional
3. What determines the appropriate degree of politeness indirectness?
   — (a) the time of day only (b) social distance, power relationship,
   and size of imposition ✓ (c) the speaker's age only (d) nothing;
   it's random
4. Which best describes "positive politeness"? — (a) respecting
   autonomy and minimising imposition (b) emphasising shared interests
   and inclusion ✓ (c) using only formal language (d) avoiding all
   requests
5. Which best describes "negative politeness"? — (a) being rude on
   purpose (b) respecting the other person's autonomy and minimising
   imposition ✓ (c) emphasising shared interests (d) refusing to
   negotiate
6. In British English, "cheers" used informally can mean: — (a) only
   "goodbye" (b) "thank you" ✓ (c) "hello" (d) "please"
7. Which phrase means "resolve tension diplomatically"? — (a) bridge
   the gap (b) smooth over ✓ (c) lose in translation (d) find common
   ground
8. Which phrase means "identify shared interests despite differences"?
   — (a) get off on the wrong foot (b) lose in translation (c) find
   common ground ✓ (d) smooth over
9. In intercultural negotiation, explicitly checking understanding is:
   — (a) unnecessary if both speak English (b) a useful way to bridge
   potential communication-style gaps ✓ (c) considered rude in all
   cultures (d) only needed at the very end
10. What is a genuine risk of being too indirect in a request? — (a)
    it is always more polite with no downside (b) it can be missed
    entirely, causing as much miscommunication as being too direct ✓
    (c) it is grammatically incorrect (d) it always sounds rude

## Module 5 Assignment — "An Analytical Paper — A Cross-Cultural Communication Case Study"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write an analytical paper,
350-450 words, examining a real or invented cross-cultural
communication case study (kept generic and respectfully framed, not
stereotyping any real culture). This is this level's fifth writing
genre — **the analytical paper**. Your paper must include: a
description of the miscommunication or communication challenge;
analysis using this module's pragmatics vocabulary (positive/negative
politeness, indirect speech acts, face); at least 2 requests or
statements at different politeness levels, analysed for their
appropriateness; a discussion of the likely underlying cause; and a
concrete, actionable recommendation for avoiding similar
miscommunication in future.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, graded politeness modality used
  accurately throughout the analysis.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct pragmatics/cross-cultural
  words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — case description, pragmatic analysis, a
  cause discussion, and a concrete recommendation all present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the pragmatic analysis genuinely
  insightful (not superficial), and is the recommendation specific and
  actionable rather than generic advice?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register appropriately
  analytical and respectful throughout (never stereotyping a real
  culture), and does the paper read as one connected, well-reasoned
  analysis?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 5
completed for the learner.
