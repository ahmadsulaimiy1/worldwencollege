# WEC — Level V, Module 9: Style & Voice

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 9, the learner
can: edit their own writing for concision and precision, removing
clutter without losing meaning; deliberately vary register and tone
for a specific audience and purpose while maintaining a consistent
underlying voice; write a concise, action-oriented executive report;
deliver an efficient executive briefing to a time-constrained senior
audience.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 9.1 ("Cutting the Clutter — Editing for Concision &
Precision"); Lesson 9.2 ("Finding Your Voice — Varying Register &
Tone for Audience & Purpose & the Executive Briefing"); Module 9 Quiz
(10 questions); Module 9 Assignment ("An Executive Report &
Briefing").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** In short,... — Put simply,...
— The bottom line is... — To be precise,... — Bottom line up
front:... — I'll keep this brief.

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — concision and
directness):** *in short*, *put simply*, *the bottom line is*, *to be
precise* — language that explicitly signals compression, essential to
the executive register this module builds toward, where a reader's or
listener's time is the scarcest resource.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *trim down [a
document]* (remove unnecessary length or detail), *pare back
[unnecessary detail]* (reduce something to its essential core), *get
straight to the point* (state the main idea immediately, without
preamble), *distil [the key point]* (extract the essential meaning
from a larger body of information), *tighten up [a piece of
writing]* (make it more concise and precise through editing).

**BrE / AmE note:** British English commonly uses **PA** (Personal
Assistant) for the role supporting a senior executive with
administrative and scheduling tasks, while American English more
commonly uses **EA** (Executive Assistant) for the equivalent, closely
related role — both terms exist in both varieties to some extent, but
the "default" abbreviation differs, worth knowing when reading or
writing executive-context correspondence from either variety.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** editing vocabulary (redundancy,
concision, precision, clarity, register), executive-communication
vocabulary (bottom line up front, executive summary, action item,
key takeaway). Intercultural note: expectations around directness and
brevity in executive communication vary — some professional cultures
value extensive context before a recommendation, others expect the
recommendation first; this module teaches the "bottom-line-up-front"
convention as a widely used international-business standard, not the
only valid approach.

## Lesson 9.1 — "Cutting the Clutter — Editing for Concision & Precision"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
identify and remove redundant words and phrases from your own
writing; (2) replace vague or weak phrasing with precise language; (3)
choose actively between active and passive voice deliberately for
effect, not by default; (4) edit a piece of writing for concision
while preserving its full meaning.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** All prior levels' register and cohesion
work — this lesson consolidates it into a deliberate self-editing
discipline, the module's defining skill.

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one wordy, cluttered sentence
and its edited, concise equivalent (preserving the same meaning) and
asks learners to identify exactly what was removed or changed, before
formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model an editing pass aloud: "Due to the
fact that there has been a significant increase in the number of
customer complaints in recent months, it is something that we are
going to need to think about and possibly take some sort of action on"
→ "Customer complaints have risen significantly in recent months and
require action." Highlight explicitly: **redundancy removal** (`due
to the fact that` → `because`, or removed entirely), **precision**
(`take some sort of action` → a specific verb), **directness**
(removing hedging that adds no real information, distinct from Module
4's *genuine* uncertainty-hedging, which should be preserved when the
uncertainty is real). Emphasise: editing for concision never means
cutting genuinely necessary hedges, evidence, or nuance — it means
cutting words that add length without adding meaning.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners edit 8 wordy, cluttered
sentences into concise, precise equivalents, checking that no genuine
meaning is lost.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners bring (or are given) a
piece of their own earlier writing from this level and edit one
paragraph for concision, then compare their edited version with a
partner's edit of the same original, discussing any differences in
what each chose to cut or keep.

**Speaking activity.** The partner comparison exchange above, followed
by a brief whole-class discussion of one edit that surprisingly
changed the meaning (a genuine risk worth naming) versus one that
purely tightened the language.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is there a risk that
editing for concision can accidentally remove genuinely important
nuance or hedging? How do you tell the difference between clutter and
necessary complexity?" — a genuinely sophisticated C1 question about
the real judgement required in editing, not a mechanical rule.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a wordy spoken explanation
and a concise, edited version of the same content, identifying what
changed.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read a short wordy paragraph (150-180 words) and identify at least 5
specific opportunities for concision editing, explaining your
reasoning for each.

**Writing task (5 min).** Edit the paragraph from the reading activity
into a concise version, aiming to reduce its length by at least a
third while preserving its full meaning.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill crisp, efficient delivery
pacing appropriate to concise, edited spoken English — fewer filler
words, more deliberate pauses at genuinely meaningful points rather
than habitual hesitation.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A redundant-phrase-to-concise-equivalent
matching game (`due to the fact that` → `because`; `at this point in
time` → `now`; `in the event that` → `if`) with 8 further examples.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that edits genuinely
preserve meaning while reducing length, and that genuine hedges/
nuance are correctly preserved rather than cut, during independent
practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a real or invented business situation
requiring a concise executive report, and jot down its core
recommendation and 2-3 supporting points, ready for Lesson 9.2's
executive-briefing work.

**Revision.** Lesson 9.2 opens with learners briefly stating their
homework recommendation in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners edit a second paragraph from
an earlier level's own assignment work, reflecting on how their
writing has changed across the programme.

## Lesson 9.2 — "Finding Your Voice — Varying Register & Tone for Audience & Purpose & the Executive Briefing"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
deliberately vary register and tone for a specific audience and
purpose while maintaining a consistent underlying voice; (2) structure
information "bottom-line-up-front" for a time-constrained audience;
(3) deliver an efficient executive briefing under real time pressure;
(4) write a concise, action-oriented executive report.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 9.1 (editing for concision), Level
V Module 1 (register-shifting — this lesson adds "voice," a
consistent identity that persists even as register shifts).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor delivers the same core message in two
different registers (a warm, relationship-focused version and a
brisk, efficient version) but points out one consistent underlying
value or priority present in both, asking learners what stayed the
same despite the register shift, before formal presentation —
introducing "voice" as distinct from "register."

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the bottom-line-up-front executive
report structure explicitly: **recommendation first** (`We recommend
proceeding with X`), **brief supporting rationale** (2-3 concise
points, not extended argument), **key risk or consideration**
(one, clearly flagged), **next step** (a specific, actionable close).
Then model an executive briefing delivered under real time pressure —
noticeably faster pacing than earlier levels' presentations, no
preamble, immediate substance — while still recognisably reflecting
the same underlying "voice" (e.g. a consistently direct,
evidence-focused style) a listener would recognise across that
speaker's different registers.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners restructure a longer, more
narrative piece of writing (provided) into bottom-line-up-front
executive-report format, identifying the core recommendation to lead
with.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 9.1 homework
notes, learners draft a bottom-line-up-front executive report
(recommendation, rationale, risk, next step) and prepare a 60-90
second spoken briefing version of the same content.

**Speaking activity — executive briefing.** Learners deliver their
60-90 second executive briefing to a partner or small group playing
time-constrained senior executives, who may interrupt with one direct
question requiring an immediate, concise answer — this lesson's core
task, and the direct basis for Module 9's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Do you think a person's
underlying 'voice' — their core values, priorities, or way of
reasoning — should stay recognisable across very different registers
(a casual conversation, a formal report, a crisis statement)? Or
should each situation call for a completely different persona?" — a
genuinely reflective, capstone-adjacent C1 question about the
relationship between authenticity and adaptability, appropriate to
this level's overall arc from Module 1's register work onward.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to an executive briefing
delivered under time pressure and identify its bottom-line-up-front
structure and one moment reflecting the speaker's consistent voice.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written executive report
and identify its four structural parts (recommendation, rationale,
risk, next step).

**Writing task (5 min).** Finalise your executive report's written
form, checking it follows the bottom-line-up-front structure
concisely.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill brisk, confident, no-
preamble delivery appropriate to a time-constrained executive
audience — immediate engagement from the first sentence, no
throat-clearing or extended windup.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An executive-communication vocabulary
matching game (bottom line up front, executive summary, action item,
key takeaway) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for a genuine bottom-
line-up-front structure and efficient, substantive delivery under
simulated time pressure during the briefing task.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their executive report and briefing
for Module 9's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 9.1 recommendation
recap. Module 9's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners prepare a second, even
shorter (30-second) version of their briefing for an even more
time-constrained scenario, practising further compression without
losing the core message.

   sentence structure
3. What is the "bottom-line-up-front" structure? — (a) the conclusion
   comes last, after extended context (b) the recommendation comes

4. What is "voice," as distinct from "register" in this module? — (a)
   they are exactly the same thing (b) a consistent underlying

5. An executive report's core recommendation should typically appear:
   — (a) at the very end (b) first ✓ (c) nowhere; it should be
   implied only (d) in a footnote
6. In British English, the common abbreviation for a role supporting a
   senior executive with administrative tasks is: — (a) EA (b) PA ✓
   (c) AA (d) SA
7. Which phrase means "extract the essential meaning from a larger
   body of information"? — (a) trim down (b) pare back (c) distil ✓
   (d) tighten up
8. Which phrase means "state the main idea immediately, without
   preamble"? — (a) get straight to the point ✓ (b) trim down (c)
   pare back (d) tighten up
9. "___, we recommend proceeding with the proposal." (a concision
   marker) — (a) In short ✓ (b) Insofar as (c) Notwithstanding (d) By
   the same token
10. An executive briefing delivered under time pressure should
    generally: — (a) include extensive preamble before the main point
    (b) begin with immediate substance and no preamble ✓ (c) avoid
    stating a recommendation (d) be as long as possible

## Module 9 Assignment — "An Executive Report & Briefing"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Complete two parts on one real
or invented business recommendation of your choice. **Part A
(writing, this level's tenth and final writing genre): an executive
report**, 200-300 words (deliberately concise — length itself is part
of the assessment), structured bottom-line-up-front: recommendation
first, brief supporting rationale, one clearly flagged risk or
consideration, and a specific next step. Edit it rigorously for
concision — no redundant phrasing. **Part B (speaking):** Record
yourself (or perform live) a 60-90 second executive briefing based on
your report, delivered under simulated time pressure, responding
concisely to at least one interrupting question.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, precise, edited sentence
  structures throughout.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 3 distinct concision/executive-
  communication phrases used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/
  collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — recommendation, rationale, risk, and next step
  all present and genuinely concise in Part A; a briefing with a
  concise response to an interruption in Part B.
- **Rhetorical effectiveness** — is the report genuinely concise
  without sacrificing necessary meaning, and does the briefing sound
  efficient and authoritative under time pressure?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the bottom-line-up-front
  structure genuinely followed (not just labelled), and is a
  consistent, recognisable voice maintained across both the written
  report and the spoken briefing?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 9
completed for the learner.
