# WEC-LC — Level V, Module 6: Advanced Media & Discourse Analysis

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 6, the learner
can: form cleft sentences correctly for rhetorical emphasis; analyse
subtext and bias at depth, beyond simply naming a technique; respond
strategically and confidently in a media interview, including to a
loaded or leading question; write a persuasive article using
rhetorical emphasis deliberately and effectively.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 6.1 ("It Was This That Changed Everything — Cleft Sentences for
Emphasis"); Lesson 6.2 ("Beneath the Surface — Analysing Subtext &
Bias at Depth & a Media Interview"); Module 6 Quiz (10 questions);
Module 6 Assignment ("A Persuasive Article & Media Interview").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** It was [X] that... — What
really matters is... — What emerges from this is... — Beneath the
surface,... — On the surface..., but in reality,... — That's an
interesting question, but what I'd really emphasise is...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — revealing
subtext):** *what emerges from this is*, *beneath the surface*, *the
subtext here is*, *on the surface... but in reality...* — language for
articulating an implied meaning beneath a text's literal content, the
core analytical move of this module.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *hint at [something]*
(suggest it indirectly, without stating it outright), *dodge [a
question]* (avoid answering it directly, often evasively), *deflect
[criticism]* (redirect it away from oneself, sometimes skilfully,
sometimes evasively), *come across as [a certain way]* (create a
particular impression, intentionally or not), *spell out
[something]* (state it explicitly and clearly, the opposite of
hinting).

**BrE / AmE note:** British broadcasting uses **newsreader** for the
person presenting televised or radio news, while American broadcasting
uses **news anchor** (or simply "anchor") for the equivalent role —
both terms are widely understood internationally through global media
consumption, but the "default" term differs by variety, much like
earlier levels' everyday-object vocabulary differences, now at a
professional/media register.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** advanced media-analysis vocabulary
(subtext, framing, loaded question, spin, media literacy, discourse),
interview vocabulary (leading question, follow-up, on the record, soundbite).
Intercultural note: expectations around how directly a media
interviewee should answer a difficult question — versus skilfully
redirecting toward a preferred message — vary by media culture and
professional context; this module teaches the *skill of strategic,
honest response*, explicitly distinguishing it from evasive dodging.

## Lesson 6.1 — "It Was This That Changed Everything — Cleft Sentences for Emphasis"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
it-clefts correctly (`It was/is [X] that...`) to emphasise a specific
element of a sentence; (2) form wh-clefts/pseudo-clefts correctly
(`What [X] is...`) to emphasise an action or idea; (3) choose between
a cleft sentence and its plain equivalent based on what needs
emphasis; (4) use cleft sentences deliberately in persuasive writing
and speech.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level V, Module 3 (inversion for
emphasis — cleft sentences are a second major emphasis structure,
worth contrasting directly).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one plain sentence and its
cleft equivalent ("The new policy caused the backlash" vs. "It was the
new policy that caused the backlash") and asks learners what shifted
in emphasis, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model it-clefts: "It was the new policy
that caused the backlash" (emphasising *the policy*, not something
else, as the cause); "It wasn't the cost that concerned investors — it
was the lack of transparency" (emphasising a contrast). Model
wh-clefts: "What really matters here is trust" (emphasising *trust* as
the key point); "What the data actually shows is a more complicated
picture." Highlight explicitly: both cleft types isolate one element
of a sentence and mark it as the important, emphasised part — a
genuinely different rhetorical tool from inversion (Module 3), which
emphasises through front-placement and unusual word order rather than
through this restructuring-and-isolating mechanism.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 plain sentences into
either it-cleft or wh-cleft form (as directed), checking the emphasis
lands on the intended element.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 cleft sentences
(a mix of it-clefts and wh-clefts) about a topic they have an opinion
on, then read them to a partner, who identifies which element is being
emphasised in each.

**Speaking activity.** The partner identification exchange above,
followed by a brief whole-class share of one particularly effective
cleft sentence per pair.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think a writer
or speaker might choose a cleft sentence instead of just adding a
stress mark or saying a word more loudly? What does the sentence
structure itself contribute that emphasis-through-volume alone
doesn't?" — a genuinely sophisticated C1 question distinguishing
written/structural emphasis from purely prosodic emphasis.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short persuasive talk
excerpt (6-7 sentences, including 2-3 cleft sentences) and identify
each cleft and what it emphasises.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read a short opinion/persuasive-article excerpt (180-200 words) using
cleft sentences. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 evaluative questions
("Identify one cleft sentence. Would the article be equally persuasive
without it? What specifically is gained by the cleft structure here?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 3-4 cleft sentences (a mix of both
types) suitable for a persuasive article on a topic of your choice.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill strong stress on the
emphasised element in a cleft sentence ("It was the POLICY that caused
the backlash") and the natural, slightly lower-pitched delivery of the
`that`-clause that follows, which carries less new information.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A cleft-sentence transformation relay:
convert 10 plain sentences into cleft form as quickly and accurately
as possible, alternating it-cleft and wh-cleft patterns.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct cleft formation
(both types) and that the intended element is genuinely emphasised,
during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a topic for their persuasive article and
jot down 2-3 points they'd want to emphasise using cleft sentences,
ready for Lesson 6.2's media interview work.

**Revision.** Lesson 6.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
homework topic.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners write one sentence using a
negative it-cleft ("It wasn't X that mattered — it was Y") to
emphasise a contrast, a more advanced variant of the basic pattern.

## Lesson 6.2 — "Beneath the Surface — Analysing Subtext & Bias at Depth & a Media Interview"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
analyse a text's subtext — what's implied but not stated — at depth,
explaining *how* specific choices create that implication; (2)
identify how rhetorical devices (cleft sentences, framing, word
choice) work together to construct a persuasive effect; (3) respond
to a leading or loaded question in a media interview strategically and
honestly, without either evading or being derailed; (4) write a
persuasive article using rhetorical emphasis deliberately.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 6.1 (cleft sentences), Level IV
Module 7 (identifying purpose/bias/tone/technique — this lesson goes
deeper into *how* techniques combine to construct subtext).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor asks one clearly loaded interview
question ("Given how badly this has gone, don't you think it's time to
admit failure?") and asks learners what assumption is embedded in the
question itself, before formal presentation — surfacing that a
question can carry bias, not just an answer.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model deeper subtext analysis: "On the
surface, this article simply reports the figures. But in reality, the
choice to lead with the most alarming statistic, combined with the
cleft sentence 'It was mismanagement that caused this' rather than a
more neutral phrasing, constructs a subtext of blame before any
evidence is presented." Highlight explicitly: this is *combining*
observations (word choice, sentence structure, ordering) into one
coherent claim about the text's underlying implication — a genuinely
more demanding analytical move than simply naming "this uses a
rhetorical question" in isolation. Then model responding to a loaded
interview question strategically: acknowledging the question's framing
without accepting its embedded assumption ("I'd actually push back on
the framing there — the situation is more nuanced than 'failure,' and
here's why..."), staying honest and substantive rather than dodging.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 3 short media
excerpts and write a combined subtext analysis for each — not just
naming one technique, but explaining how 2-3 techniques work together.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In pairs, Learner A interviews
Learner B (using their Lesson 6.1 homework topic) with at least one
genuinely loaded or leading question; Learner B responds strategically,
acknowledging the framing honestly without being derailed, then swap.

**Speaking activity — media interview.** The interview roleplay above,
extended to include 3-4 questions (a mix of neutral and loaded/
leading), with the interviewee maintaining message discipline while
staying honest and substantive — this lesson's core task, and the
direct basis for Module 6's assignment's interview element.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "What's the difference
between skilfully addressing a loaded question's framing and simply
dodging it? How can a listener tell the difference?" — directly
reinforces the module's own distinction between strategic response and
evasion, a genuinely important media-literacy question from the
interviewee's side, not just the audience's.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short media interview
exchange with one loaded question and evaluate whether the response
addresses the framing honestly or dodges it.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short media article excerpt and
write a combined subtext analysis identifying at least 2 techniques
working together.

**Writing task (5 min).** Draft your persuasive article's opening
paragraph, using at least one cleft sentence deliberately for emphasis.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill composed, credible delivery
for handling a difficult interview question — a brief thoughtful
pause before responding (rather than rushing), and level, confident
intonation that doesn't betray defensiveness.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An interview-language matching game
(leading question, follow-up, on the record, soundbite, dodge,
deflect) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine combined
subtext analysis (not single-technique naming) and honest, substantive
(not evasive) interview responses during the roleplay.

**Homework.** Learners complete their persuasive article draft for
Module 6's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 6.1 topic recap.
Module 6's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners identify a real (or
realistically invented) example of a public figure handling a
difficult question particularly well or poorly, analysing what made
the difference.

## Module 6 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-5.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "It was the new policy ___ caused the backlash." (it-cleft) — (a)
   who (b) that ✓ (c) which is (d) what
2. "What really ___ here is trust." (wh-cleft) — (a) matter (b)
   matters ✓ (c) mattering (d) mattered
3. "It wasn't the cost ___ concerned investors -- it was the lack of
   transparency." — (a) which (b) that ✓ (c) what (d) who
4. What is the main function of a cleft sentence? — (a) to make a
   sentence longer for no reason (b) to isolate and emphasise one
   specific element of a sentence ✓ (c) to avoid using any emphasis
   at all (d) to make a sentence grammatically incorrect
5. A loaded interview question is one that: — (a) is completely
   neutral (b) carries an embedded assumption or bias within the
   question itself ✓ (c) is always illegal to ask (d) has only one
   possible answer
6. What distinguishes strategic, honest handling of a loaded question
   from simply dodging it? — (a) there is no difference (b)
   acknowledging the framing while staying substantive, versus
   avoiding the question entirely ✓ (c) speaking for a very long time
   (d) refusing to answer
7. In British broadcasting, the person presenting televised news is
   often called a: — (a) news anchor (b) newsreader ✓ (c) news host
   (d) presenter only
8. Which phrase means "suggest something indirectly, without stating
   it outright"? — (a) spell out (b) hint at ✓ (c) dodge (d) deflect
9. Which phrase means "avoid answering a question directly, often
   evasively"? — (a) hint at (b) spell out (c) dodge ✓ (d) come
   across as
10. Combined subtext analysis (as taught in this module) means: — (a)
    naming a single rhetorical technique in isolation (b) explaining
    how multiple techniques work together to construct an implied
    meaning ✓ (c) only analysing grammar (d) ignoring word choice
    entirely

## Module 6 Assignment — "A Persuasive Article & Media Interview"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Complete two parts on one topic
of your choice. **Part A (writing, this level's sixth genre): a
persuasive article**, 350-450 words, arguing a clear position. Use at
least 3 cleft sentences (a mix of it-clefts and wh-clefts) deliberately
for rhetorical emphasis, and demonstrate awareness of how your word
choice and framing construct your article's overall persuasive effect.
**Part B (speaking):** Record yourself (or perform with a partner) a
media interview, 90 seconds to 2 minutes, about your article's topic,
including at least one loaded or leading question and a strategic,
honest response that addresses the framing without dodging.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct cleft sentence formation (both
  types).
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct media-analysis/interview
  words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — a clear position, at least 3 cleft sentences,
  and rhetorical awareness in Part A; a loaded question and a
  strategic response in Part B.
- **Rhetorical effectiveness** — do the cleft sentences genuinely
  strengthen the article's persuasive impact, and does the interview
  response handle the loaded question skilfully rather than evasively
  or defensively?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the article's register
  appropriately persuasive and professional throughout, and does the
  interview response sound composed and credible?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 6
completed for the learner.
