# WEC-LC — Level V, Module 2: Academic Writing III

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 2, the learner
can: synthesise a small body of sources (not just two) into a
thematically organised literature review; identify patterns, points of
convergence, and gaps across multiple sources; use author-date
citation consistently; deploy advanced cohesion devices that operate
across paragraphs, not just within a single sentence.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 2.1 ("Across the Literature... — Synthesising a Body of
Sources"); Lesson 2.2 ("Weaving It Together — Advanced Cohesion at
Essay Level & Drafting a Literature Review"); Module 2 Quiz (10
questions); Module 2 Assignment ("A Literature Review").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** A growing body of research
suggests... — Several studies have examined... — However, a gap
remains in... — Taken as a whole, the literature indicates... —
(Author, Year) — This pattern is echoed by...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — essay-level
cohesion, referring back across paragraphs, not just sentences):**
*the aforementioned*, *as noted above*, *in the studies discussed thus
far*, *collectively* — devices that let a writer refer back to
material established several paragraphs earlier, holding a longer
piece of writing together as one coherent whole rather than a string
of locally-cohesive paragraphs.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *zero in on [a specific
issue]* (focus precisely on it after a broader survey), *fill a gap
(in the research)* (address something previous work hasn't covered),
*survey (the field)* (review the broad landscape of existing work),
*converge on [a conclusion]* (multiple sources arrive at a similar
point), *diverge from [a view]* (differ from an established position).

**BrE / AmE note:** the well-known **-ise/-ize** spelling pattern is
genuinely more nuanced than it first appears: American English uses
**-ize** exclusively (*analyze, synthesize, organize*); British
publishing convention commonly uses **-ise** (*analyse, synthesise,
organise*), but Oxford University Press's own house style (sometimes
called "Oxford spelling") actually prefers **-ize** too, following the
words' Greek etymology — meaning a British writer using **-ize**
consistently is not necessarily wrong or "Americanising," a genuinely
useful nuance for academic writing where consistency matters more than
which specific convention is chosen.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** literature-review vocabulary (corpus,
consensus, divergence, methodology, theoretical framework, research
gap). Intercultural note: what counts as a sufficiently thorough
literature review, and which citation conventions are expected, varies
by academic discipline and country; this module teaches the underlying
skill (synthesis, gap-identification, consistent citation) that
transfers across specific formal systems, deferred fully to Level VI.

## Lesson 2.1 — "Across the Literature... — Synthesising a Body of Sources"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) read
and accurately summarise multiple sources' main claims; (2) identify
where sources converge, diverge, or address different aspects of a
topic; (3) organise findings thematically (by pattern or trend) rather
than source-by-source; (4) identify a genuine gap the existing sources
leave unaddressed.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level IV, Module 9 (two-source synthesis
— this lesson extends that skill to a small body of 3-4 sources and
adds thematic organisation).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor presents three short, generic invented
"finding" statements on a related topic and asks learners to group
them by theme rather than by which statement came from which source,
before formal presentation — surfacing that literature-review
organisation is thematic, not source-by-source.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model reading 3 short supplied source
excerpts (generic/invented, 70-90 words each) on a related topic and
organising the synthesis thematically: "A growing body of research
suggests that remote work improves focus (Smith, 2021; Lee, 2022).
However, both studies note this depends on having a dedicated
workspace — a factor Chen (2023) examines directly, finding it the
single strongest predictor of the effect. Taken as a whole, the
literature indicates that the benefit is real but conditional; however,
a gap remains in understanding how this varies by role type."
Highlight explicitly: this is organised by *theme* (the benefit, the
condition, the gap), not "Source A says X, Source B says Y, Source C
says Z" — the defining structural difference between a literature
review and a simple source-by-source report.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 3 more short source
excerpts and identify: the shared theme, one point of convergence, one
point of divergence or added nuance, and one possible gap — recording
each on a simple synthesis grid.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners read a new set of 3 source
excerpts (provided) and write a short thematically organised synthesis
paragraph (5-6 sentences), using author-date citation for each claim,
then swap with a partner, who checks whether the synthesis is
genuinely thematic (not source-by-source) and whether a gap is
identified.

**Speaking activity.** Partners discuss the gap they each identified
and whether they agree it's a genuine, meaningful gap or a fairly
trivial one — a direct check on critical judgement, not just mechanical
gap-naming.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think
identifying a genuine gap in existing research is harder than it
sounds? What's the difference between a real gap and something that's
simply outside a study's stated scope?" — a genuinely sophisticated C1
question about the difference between a meaningful critical
observation and a superficial one.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone verbally
synthesising three sources thematically (7-8 sentences) and identify
the theme, a convergence point, and the stated gap.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read three short source excerpts (100 words each) on a generic
academic/professional topic. Answer 2 literal questions and 2
evaluative questions ("Which source's methodology or evidence seems
strongest, and why? Is there a genuine gap across all three, or does
one source already partially address it?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a synthesis paragraph (4-5 sentences)
using the reading-activity sources, correctly citing each with
author-date style.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill clear, natural delivery of
author-date citations when speaking ("Smith, twenty twenty-one" —
correct, natural year pronunciation) so citations integrate smoothly
into spoken academic register rather than sounding bolted-on.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A literature-review vocabulary matching
game (corpus, consensus, divergence, methodology, theoretical
framework, research gap) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine thematic
organisation (not source-by-source) and a meaningful, well-reasoned
gap identification during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a topic for their literature review
assignment and identify (or invent, generically) 3-4 source
"findings" they'll synthesise, ready for Lesson 2.2's drafting work.

**Revision.** Lesson 2.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
chosen topic and sources.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners identify a second, more
subtle gap in their chosen sources — one requiring closer critical
reading than the first, obvious one.

## Lesson 2.2 — "Weaving It Together — Advanced Cohesion at Essay Level & Drafting a Literature Review"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
advanced cohesion devices that refer back across paragraphs, not just
within a sentence; (2) structure a full literature review with a
clear thematic organisation and a stated gap; (3) maintain consistent
author-date citation throughout an extended piece; (4) draft a
complete literature review.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 2.1 (synthesis, thematic
organisation), Level IV Module 7 (ellipsis/substitution at sentence
level — this lesson extends cohesion to the essay/paragraph level).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows a short multi-paragraph excerpt
where each paragraph feels disconnected from the last (no essay-level
referencing) and a revised version using `as noted above/the
aforementioned`, asking learners what specifically changed, before
formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model essay-level cohesion explicitly: "As
noted above, workspace quality appears to moderate the productivity
effect. This pattern is echoed by more recent findings in adjacent
fields... Collectively, these studies point toward a conditional, not
universal, benefit." Highlight explicitly: **sentence-level** cohesion
(ellipsis, substitution — Level IV) connects clauses within or
between adjacent sentences; **essay-level** cohesion connects a
current point back to something established several paragraphs
earlier, holding a longer piece together as one coherent argument, not
a sequence of locally-connected but globally disjointed paragraphs.
Then model the literature review's typical structure: **introduction**
(the topic, its significance, and a preview of the review's
organisation); **thematically organised body** (each section
synthesising sources around one theme, not one source); **gap
statement** (what remains unaddressed); **brief conclusion** (why the
gap matters, previewing what further work might address it).

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners revise a provided multi-
paragraph excerpt with weak essay-level cohesion, adding appropriate
back-referencing devices (`as noted above, the aforementioned,
collectively`) to connect later paragraphs to earlier ones.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 2.1 synthesis
work, learners draft their literature review's introduction and first
thematic section, deliberately including at least one essay-level
cohesion device referring back to an earlier point.

**Speaking activity.** Learners read their draft introduction and
first section aloud to a partner, who identifies the essay-level
cohesion device used and whether it successfully connects the two
parts.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why might a literature
review structured by theme be more useful to a reader than one
structured source-by-source, even though the source-by-source version
might be easier to write?" — directly reinforces the lesson's core
structural principle by asking learners to justify it, not just apply
it.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short literature-review-
style talk and identify at least 2 essay-level cohesion devices used
to connect points across the talk.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a model literature review excerpt
(200-220 words) and annotate its thematic structure, citation
consistency, and essay-level cohesion devices.

**Writing task (5 min).** Continue drafting your literature review's
gap statement and brief conclusion.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill sustained, well-organised
delivery appropriate to presenting a literature review's findings
aloud — clear signalling of thematic transitions (recycling Level IV's
presentation-signposting skill in an academic-synthesis context).

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An essay-level cohesion device
identification game: learners find examples of back-referencing
devices in a provided multi-paragraph text and identify what each one
refers back to.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for genuine essay-level
cohesion (not just sentence-level) and a clear, consistently
maintained thematic structure during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners complete their full literature review draft for
Module 2's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 2.1 topic/source
recap. Module 2's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a brief methodological
comment on one source (e.g. a small sample size, a narrow context) as
a more sophisticated form of critical evaluation than simple
agreement/disagreement.

## Module 2 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

`sql/seed-curriculum-level-5.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. What is the defining structural feature of a literature review, as
   opposed to a source-by-source report? — (a) it only uses one
   source (b) it is organised thematically, not source-by-source ✓
   (c) it never cites sources (d) it is always written in the first
   person
2. "___, the literature indicates that the benefit is real but
   conditional." — (a) Taken as a whole ✓ (b) According to (c)
   Because (d) So
3. Which phrase signals an essay-level cohesion device, referring back
   across paragraphs? — (a) however (b) as noted above ✓ (c) and (d)
   because
4. "This pattern is ___ by more recent findings in adjacent fields."
   — (a) echoed ✓ (b) echoing (c) echo (d) echoes
5. What does "converge on a conclusion" mean, in the context of
   multiple sources? — (a) they completely disagree (b) they arrive at
   a similar point ✓ (c) they use the same methodology (d) they were
   published in the same year
6. In American English, the spelling suffix used exclusively is: —
   (a) -ise (b) -ize ✓ (c) -yse (d) -isze
7. Which phrase means "address something previous work hasn't
   covered"? — (a) zero in on (b) fill a gap in ✓ (c) survey (d)
   diverge from
8. A well-structured literature review typically includes an
   introduction, a thematically organised body, and: — (a) only a
   list of sources, nothing else (b) a stated gap and a brief
   conclusion ✓ (c) no conclusion at all (d) a single source summary
9. Which phrase means "focus precisely on a specific issue after a
   broader survey"? — (a) zero in on ✓ (b) fill a gap in (c) survey
   (d) converge on
10. "However, a gap ___ in understanding how this varies by role
    type." — (a) remain (b) remains ✓ (c) remaining (d) remained

## Module 2 Assignment — "A Literature Review"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write a literature review,
400-500 words, on a topic of your choice, synthesising 3-4 source
"findings" (these may be genuinely researched from real, appropriately
cited material, or generic/invented for practice purposes — either is
acceptable for this exercise). This is this level's second writing
genre — **the literature review**. Your review must include: an
introduction stating the topic's significance and previewing the
review's organisation; a thematically organised body (not
source-by-source) with consistent author-date citation; at least 2
essay-level cohesion devices connecting later points to earlier ones;
a clearly stated gap in the existing material; and a brief conclusion.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, varied sentence structures
  appropriate to formal academic synthesis writing.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct literature-review words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — introduction, thematic body, consistent
  citation, a stated gap, and a conclusion all present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the thematic organisation
  genuine (not disguised source-by-source reporting), and is the
  identified gap meaningful rather than trivial?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — does the review read as one
  connected whole, using essay-level cohesion devices to link distant
  paragraphs, with a consistently formal academic register throughout?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 2
completed for the learner.
