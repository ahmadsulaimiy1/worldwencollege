# AIPC — Level V, Module 4: Complex Systems (Science, Economics, Policy)

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-5-advanced.md`'s Level V build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 4, the learner
can: form dense, technical nominalised noun phrases appropriate to
scientific, economic, and policy writing; hedge and qualify claims
with precision, avoiding both overclaiming and excessive vagueness;
participate in a stakeholder meeting discussing a complex issue with
competing priorities; write a concise policy brief presenting an
issue, evidence, and a recommendation.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 4.1 ("The Implementation of... — Advanced Nominalisation for
Technical Register"); Lesson 4.2 ("It Could Be Argued That... —
Hedging & Qualifying Claims & a Stakeholder Meeting"); Module 4 Quiz
(10 questions); Module 4 Assignment ("A Policy Brief").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** The widespread adoption
of... — This appears to suggest... — It could be argued that... — To
some extent,... — Broadly speaking,... — This tends to result in...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — hedging and
qualifying claims):** *it could be argued that*, *to some extent*,
*broadly speaking*, *this appears to suggest* — precise ways of
qualifying a claim's strength, essential in scientific, economic, and
policy writing where overclaiming damages credibility and excessive
vagueness makes a claim useless.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *factor in [a
variable]* (include it in a calculation or consideration), *level
off* (stop increasing or decreasing; stabilise), *trickle down*
(spread gradually from a source to wider effect, often used of
economic benefit), *offset [a cost]* (counterbalance it with a
corresponding gain), *tip the balance* (be the deciding factor between
two possibilities).

**BrE / AmE note:** the word **billion** is a rare case of full
historical convergence worth knowing: British English traditionally
used the "long scale" (a billion = a million million, 10^12), while
American English always used the "short scale" (a billion = a
thousand million, 10^9); the United Kingdom officially adopted the
short scale in 1974, and virtually all modern British usage — in
government, finance, and media — now matches the American figure, so
this is a genuine historical difference rather than a live one, worth
knowing when reading older British texts.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** economics/policy vocabulary (inflation,
regulation, subsidy, market failure, externality, fiscal policy),
science-as-vehicle vocabulary (variable, correlation, causation,
threshold, systemic). Intercultural note: policy priorities and
economic philosophy (the appropriate role of regulation, redistribution,
market mechanisms) vary significantly by country and political
tradition; this module's materials present issues and evidence
generically, without endorsing one policy tradition as objectively
correct.

## Lesson 4.1 — "The Implementation of... — Advanced Nominalisation for Technical Register"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
dense, technical nominalised noun phrases from full clauses; (2) use
nominalisation to compress information efficiently in technical/
policy writing; (3) recognise when nominalisation aids clarity versus
when it obscures meaning through excessive density; (4) use
science/economics/policy vocabulary accurately as a vehicle for
precise expression.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level IV, Module 6 (basic nominalisation —
this lesson extends it to longer, more technically dense
constructions).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one verb-based sentence and its
increasingly nominalised versions ("Companies are adopting automation
widely" → "The widespread adoption of automation..." → "The
widespread corporate adoption of automation technologies...") and
asks learners what's gained and potentially lost at each step, before
formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "The widespread adoption of
automation technologies has led to significant productivity gains.
The implementation of this policy will require careful monitoring of
its distributional effects." Highlight explicitly: advanced
nominalisation chains multiple nominalised elements together
("adoption... technologies... productivity gains") to compress a lot
of information into a formal, technical register — genuinely useful
in policy/scientific writing where density and precision matter, but
also genuinely risky: overuse produces text that's technically correct
but hard to parse, a real stylistic trade-off worth naming directly
rather than presenting nominalisation as an unconditional good.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 verb-based sentences
about science/economics/policy topics into nominalised technical
register, then identify which conversions genuinely improve precision
and which start to feel overly dense.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 sentences about a
science, economics, or policy topic of their choice, using nominalised
technical register, then read one to a partner, who tries to
"un-nominalise" it back into a plainer verb-based sentence, checking
that no meaning was lost either direction.

**Speaking activity.** The partner "un-nominalisation" exchange above
— a genuine test of whether the technical version actually preserved
precise meaning, not just formal-sounding language.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is highly nominalised,
technical writing always a sign of rigour, or can it sometimes be used
to make a weak or vague claim sound more authoritative than it really
is?" — a genuinely important critical-reading question, since
technical register can obscure as easily as it can clarify, worth
naming explicitly at this level.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short technical policy
briefing (6-7 sentences, heavily nominalised) and identify the
underlying, simpler claims beneath the technical phrasing.

**Reading activity — extended reading & critical evaluation (8 min).**
Read a short policy-report-style excerpt (180-200 words) using dense
nominalisation. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 evaluative questions
("Identify one sentence where nominalisation genuinely aids precision,
and one where it might be obscuring a simpler, vaguer claim").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 sentences about a complex systems
topic (a scientific, economic, or policy issue), using nominalised
technical register deliberately and precisely.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill stress patterns across long,
multi-syllable technical nominalised phrases (imPLEmentAtion,
diSTRIButional, REGuLAtory) and clear, measured pacing appropriate to
technical/policy delivery.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An economics/policy vocabulary matching
game (inflation, regulation, subsidy, market failure, externality,
fiscal policy) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct nominalisation
formation and a genuine understanding of when it aids versus obscures
clarity, during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose one real complex issue (a science,
economics, or policy topic) with multiple affected stakeholders and
jot down 3-4 notes on the different interests involved, ready for
Lesson 4.2's stakeholder meeting.

**Revision.** Lesson 4.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
chosen issue.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners take one of their nominalised
sentences and deliberately "de-nominalise" it into plainer language,
comparing which version would suit a general-audience report versus a
technical one.

## Lesson 4.2 — "It Could Be Argued That... — Hedging & Qualifying Claims & a Stakeholder Meeting"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) hedge
a claim appropriately using a precise range of qualifying language;
(2) distinguish a well-calibrated hedge from either overclaiming or
excessive, meaningless vagueness; (3) participate in a stakeholder
meeting, representing one perspective while genuinely engaging with
others'; (4) write a concise policy brief presenting an issue,
evidence, and a recommendation.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 4.1 (nominalisation, technical
register), Level III Module 7 (modals of deduction — a foundation this
lesson extends into formal hedging language).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one overclaimed statement ("This
policy will definitely solve the problem"), one excessively vague one
("Some people think this might possibly help, maybe"), and one
well-calibrated one ("This policy is likely to reduce the problem to
some extent, though its full impact remains uncertain"), asking
learners which is most credible and why, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "It could be argued that this policy
addresses the core issue, though its effectiveness may vary by
region. This tends to result in a modest but measurable improvement.
Broadly speaking, the evidence supports the proposal, to some extent."
Highlight explicitly: well-calibrated hedging communicates genuine
uncertainty precisely — neither claiming more certainty than the
evidence supports, nor hedging so much the claim becomes meaningless.
Then introduce the stakeholder meeting format: each participant
represents a distinct stakeholder perspective (e.g. economic growth,
environmental impact, social equity) on a complex issue, states their
position with appropriately hedged claims, and must genuinely engage
with — not dismiss — other perspectives before the group works toward
a recommendation.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 6 claims at varying
strength and revise each to be appropriately, precisely hedged for the
strength of evidence described.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In small groups, learners are
assigned distinct stakeholder perspectives on their Lesson 4.1
homework issue and prepare 2-3 hedged claims supporting their assigned
perspective.

**Speaking activity — stakeholder meeting.** Groups hold a structured
stakeholder meeting: each stakeholder states their position with
appropriately hedged claims, responds to at least one other
stakeholder's point, and the group works toward a brief joint
recommendation (or an honest acknowledgement that positions remain
unresolved) — this lesson's core task, and the direct basis for
Module 4's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "In a real stakeholder
meeting about a complex issue, is it more useful to reach a quick
compromise, or to genuinely understand where the positions
irreconcilably differ, even if no agreement is reached? Why?" — a
genuinely realistic C1 question about the actual purpose of
multi-stakeholder deliberation.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short stakeholder meeting
exchange and identify each speaker's hedged claims and their
stakeholder perspective.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written stakeholder
position statement and identify its hedging language and how well-
calibrated it is to the evidence described.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short summary (5-6 sentences) of
your stakeholder meeting's discussion and outcome, using at least 2
hedging expressions.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill measured, credible delivery
for hedged claims — avoiding both overly confident, unhedged-sounding
intonation and overly tentative, undermining intonation — the
prosodic skill of sounding appropriately, not excessively, uncertain.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A hedging-strength card-sorting game:
sort 9 example claims from strongly hedged to strongly asserted.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that hedging is genuinely
well-calibrated (not overclaiming or excessively vague) and that
stakeholders genuinely engage with other perspectives, during the
meeting.

**Homework.** Learners draft their policy brief based on their
stakeholder meeting's issue and discussion, ready for Module 4's
assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 4.1 issue-naming
recap. Module 4's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners identify one claim from
their group's discussion that was, on reflection, under- or
over-hedged, and revise it to better match the actual strength of
evidence.

## Module 4 Assignment — "A Policy Brief"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write a policy brief, 350-450
words, on a real or invented complex issue (a science, economics, or
policy topic) of your choice. This is this level's fourth writing
genre — **the policy brief**. Your brief must include: a concise
statement of the issue; a summary of relevant evidence, using at least
2 well-calibrated hedged claims; at least 2 instances of dense,
technical nominalisation used precisely (not obscuring meaning); an
acknowledgement of at least one competing stakeholder perspective; and
a clear, specific recommendation.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct nominalisation formation, correct
  hedging language.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct economics/policy/science
  words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — issue statement, evidence summary, a
  stakeholder acknowledgement, and a recommendation all present.
- **Evidence & argument quality** — is the hedging genuinely
  well-calibrated to the strength of the evidence presented (not
  overclaiming or excessively vague), and is the recommendation
  actually justified by the evidence?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register appropriately
  formal, technical, and policy-appropriate throughout, and does the
  nominalisation aid clarity rather than obscure it?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 4
completed for the learner.
