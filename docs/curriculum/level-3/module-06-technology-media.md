# AIPC — Level III, Module 6: Technology & Media

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-3-intermediate.md`'s Level III build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 6, the learner
can: report what someone said using reported speech (statements),
applying correct backshift; attribute a claim to its source using
formal reporting language; evaluate a media claim critically,
distinguishing fact from opinion and noticing bias; discuss the impact
of technology on daily life or society, with reasons.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 6.1 ("She Said That... — Reported Speech for Statements");
Lesson 6.2 ("Is That Actually True? — Media Literacy & Evaluating
Claims"); Module 6 Quiz (10 questions); Module 6 Assignment ("A
Technology Review & Media Critique").

---

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** She said (that).../He told me
(that)... — According to [the article/the review],... — It is
reported that... — Apparently,... — That's a fact, not just an
opinion. — The headline suggests..., but the article actually says...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — reporting and
attributing):** *according to*, *it is reported that*, *apparently* (a
hedge marking information as secondhand, not personally verified) —
formal ways to attribute a claim to its source, distinct from Module
2's `according to` (there, attributing an idea in an academic context;
here, attributing a claim in a media-literacy context).

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *come out* (be
published/released — "the update comes out next week"), *go viral*
(spread very quickly online), *keep up with [the news/technology]*
(stay current with fast-changing information), *log into/log onto
[an account]*, *roll out [a new feature]* (gradually release
something to users).

**BrE / AmE note:** British **mobile phone** (or simply "mobile") vs.
American **cell phone** (or simply "cell") for the same device —
one of the most common everyday technology vocabulary differences
learners will actually hear; both are universally understood by
international audiences, but the "default" term differs by variety.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** media/technology vocabulary (headline,
source, bias, credible, algorithm, platform, update, feature),
media-literacy vocabulary (fact-check, misleading, evidence, context).
Intercultural note: trust in different types of media (state
broadcasters, independent outlets, social platforms) varies
significantly by country and personal experience; this module's
critical-evaluation skill is about the *method* (checking evidence,
noticing bias, verifying claims) that applies everywhere, not about
telling learners which specific sources to trust.

## Lesson 6.1 — "She Said That... — Reported Speech for Statements"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) report
what someone said using `said (that)`/`told [someone] (that)`; (2)
apply the correct backshift (present → past, `will` → `would`, `can`
→ `could`) when reporting a statement; (3) attribute a claim formally
using `according to`; (4) recognise when backshift is *not* required
(when the reported statement is still generally true).

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level I, Module 7 (past simple); Level II,
Module 4 (opinion language, now reported rather than given directly).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor makes one true direct statement ("I
love this new app") and immediately reports it in third person ("I
just said that I loved this new app"), asking learners what changed,
before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: direct: "This app is really easy to
use." → reported: "The reviewer said (that) the app was really easy to
use." Direct: "I'll update it next week." → reported: "She said (that)
she would update it next week." Highlight the backshift pattern
explicitly (is → was, will → would, can → could) and the important
exception: if a statement is still generally true at the time of
reporting, backshift is often optional ("She said the app is/was easy
to use" — both acceptable, a genuinely useful nuance worth naming
rather than presenting backshift as an absolute rule).

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 8 direct statements
(a mix of technology/media opinions and simple facts) into reported
speech, applying correct backshift.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In pairs, Learner A makes 4 real
direct statements about technology they use or a piece of media
they've seen recently; Learner B reports each one back correctly in
reported speech, then swap.

**Speaking activity.** The partner reporting exchange above, followed
by 2-3 learners reporting one interesting statement about their
partner to the whole class ("[Name] told me that...") — a natural,
functional use of the grammar as genuine information relay, not just a
drill.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "When you repeat something
someone else said, do you think it's possible to accidentally change
its meaning, even a little? Can you think of an example?" — connects
directly to Lesson 6.2's media-literacy focus: reporting isn't always
neutral, even grammatically correct reporting.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short interview, then
listen to someone reporting 5 statements from it, and check whether
each reported statement accurately reflects what was actually said.

**Reading activity — extended reading & inference (8 min).** Read a
short news-style text (150-180 words) about a new piece of technology,
containing several reported statements from named (generic, non-real)
sources. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 inference questions ("The
article doesn't say this directly, but what can you infer about
whether the writer is generally positive or sceptical about this
technology?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 reported-speech sentences based
on real or invented statements someone has made to you about
technology or media, applying correct backshift.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill the natural, unstressed
pronunciation of `that` in reported speech (often dropped or reduced
in fast, natural speech — "She said she'd update it") versus its
fuller pronunciation in careful or written-style speech.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A backshift-pairs matching game (is/was,
will/would, can/could, have/had) drilled as a quick-recall set.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct backshift
application (and correct recognition of when it's optional) during
independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners find (or recall) one real headline or claim
about technology/media they've recently seen and bring a one-sentence
note of it to Lesson 6.2.

**Revision.** Lesson 6.2 opens with learners reporting their homework
headline using reported speech.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners convert one reported
statement back into a `according to [source]` attribution sentence,
practising the module's two reporting styles side by side.

## Lesson 6.2 — "Is That Actually True? — Media Literacy & Evaluating Claims"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
distinguish a fact from an opinion in a media text; (2) identify
signs of possible bias or a misleading headline; (3) evaluate whether
a claim is adequately supported by evidence; (4) discuss the impact of
technology on daily life or society, with justified reasons.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 6.1 (reported speech, attributing
claims), Level III Module 4 (opinion/argument language, now applied
critically to someone else's claims rather than the learner's own).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows two headlines about the same
generic, invented event — one neutral, one deliberately sensational —
and asks learners which one seems designed to grab attention rather
than simply inform, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model a simple evaluation checklist: **Is
this a fact or an opinion?** (a fact can be checked; an opinion is a
judgement); **Does the headline match the article?** (headlines
sometimes overstate or oversimplify); **Is a source given?** (a claim
with no named source is harder to verify); **Could there be bias?**
(who benefits if you believe this claim?). Model applying it to one
short example text aloud, thinking through each question explicitly.

**Guided practice (10 min).** In pairs, learners apply the evaluation
checklist to 3 short example texts (a mix of well-supported and
poorly-supported claims, all generic/invented, not tied to real
named organisations), discussing their conclusions.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 6.1 homework
headline (or a new one), learners apply the full evaluation checklist
in writing, then discuss their evaluation with a partner, who can
challenge or agree with the reasoning.

**Speaking activity.** The partner evaluation-discussion above, then a
short whole-group discussion: "How has technology changed the way we
get and evaluate information, compared to twenty years ago?" — a
genuine, open discussion connecting media literacy to a broader
societal reflection.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Do you think it's becoming
easier or harder to know what's true online? What's one thing you
personally do (or could do) to check whether something is reliable?"
— a genuinely practical, metacognitive B1 question with real everyday
relevance.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to two short reported claims
about technology (one well-supported, one poorly-supported/vague) and
identify which is which and why.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read two short article excerpts on the
same topic from differently-toned (generic, invented) sources and
compare how each frames the same underlying facts.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short evaluation paragraph (5-6
sentences) of one claim (from this lesson's texts or the homework
headline), using reported speech to state the claim and your own
opinion language to evaluate it.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill sceptical, questioning
intonation ("Is that ACTually true?") versus neutral, reporting
intonation, and how rising pitch on a key word can itself signal doubt
without needing extra words.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A media-literacy vocabulary matching
game (fact-check, misleading, evidence, context, credible, bias) with
simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that evaluations are
genuinely reasoned (referencing the checklist criteria), not just a
gut reaction of "true" or "false," during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose one piece of technology they use
regularly and prepare 3-4 notes (a fact, an opinion, and a claim about
its impact) for Module 6's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 6.1 headline-reporting
recap. Module 6's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners find (or invent, plausibly)
a counter-claim to their chosen technology's impact, applying the
evaluation checklist to both sides.

## Module 6 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-3.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. Direct: "This app is easy to use." Reported: "The reviewer said
   (that) the app ___ easy to use." — (a) is (b) was ✓ (c) be (d) has
   been
2. Direct: "I'll update it next week." Reported: "She said (that) she
   ___ update it next week." — (a) will (b) would ✓ (c) updates (d)
   updated
3. "___ the article, the update improves battery life." — (a) Told (b)
   According to ✓ (c) Reported (d) Said
4. Which is a fact, not an opinion? — (a) This is the best app ever
   made. (b) The app was released in March. ✓ (c) Everyone should use
   this app. (d) This app is amazing.
5. Which is a sign a claim might be less reliable? — (a) A clear
   source is named. (b) No source is given at all. ✓ (c) The claim
   can be checked. (d) The headline matches the article.
6. In British English, the device most people carry is usually
   called a: — (a) cell phone (b) mobile phone ✓ (c) hand computer (d)
   pocket device
7. "___, the company has released a statement about the issue." (a
   hedge for secondhand information) — (a) Apparently (b) Certainly
   (c) Definitely (d) Obviously
8. Which phrase means "spread very quickly online"? — (a) come out (b)
   go viral ✓ (c) log into (d) roll out
9. "He told me ___ he could fix it himself." — (a) that ✓ (b) if (c)
   when (d) so
10. When evaluating a headline, a critical reader should ask: — (a)
    Does the headline match the actual article? ✓ (b) Is the headline
    exciting? (c) Is the headline short? (d) Does the headline use
    capital letters?

## Module 6 Assignment — "A Technology Review & Media Critique"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write (or record) a short
review and critique, 10-12 sentences, of one piece of technology or
one media claim you've recently encountered. Include: at least 2
reported-speech sentences relaying what someone else said or claimed
(a reviewer, an article, a friend), with correct backshift; at least
one `according to`/`apparently`-style attribution; an evaluation of
whether the claim is well-supported, referencing at least one
checklist criterion from Lesson 6.2 (source, evidence, headline-vs-
content match, possible bias); and your own reasoned opinion on the
technology's impact.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct reported-speech formation and
  backshift.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct media/technology words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — reported claim, attribution, a reasoned
  evaluation, and a personal opinion all present.
- **Communicative quality** — is the critique genuinely reasoned
  (referencing specific evidence or its absence), not just a stated
  gut feeling?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — does the piece distinguish
  clearly between what was reported (someone else's claim) and what is
  the writer's own evaluation, and is the register appropriately
  measured and critical rather than either uncritically accepting or
  dismissively cynical?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 6
completed for the learner.
