# WEC — Level III, Module 7: Health, Body & Mind

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-3-intermediate.md`'s Level III build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 7, the learner
can: make a logical deduction about a present situation using
`must/might/could/can't`; make a deduction about a past situation
using `must have/might have/can't have + past participle`; describe
physical and mental wellbeing with appropriate register; conduct (or
take part in) a structured healthcare-style interview, asking and
answering about symptoms clearly and sensitively.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 7.1 ("He Must Be Exhausted — Modals of Deduction, Present");
Lesson 7.2 ("She Might Have Been Stressed — Modals of Deduction, Past
& a Healthcare Interview"); Module 7 Quiz (10 questions); Module 7
Assignment ("A Healthcare Interview — Roleplay & Reflection").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** He must be.../She might be...
— That can't be right. — It's likely that.../It's possible that... —
Could you describe your symptoms? — I've been feeling... — There's no
way that...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — degrees of
certainty):** *it's likely that*, *it's possible that*, *there's no
way that* — hedging language expressing how confident the speaker is
in a claim, functioning alongside (not replacing) the modal verbs
themselves — a genuinely useful paraphrase set for the same meaning
(`he must be tired` ≈ `it's likely that he's tired`).

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *come down with [an
illness]* (start to suffer from an illness), *look after [yourself/
someone]* (take care of), *get over [an illness]* (recover from), *wear
off* (an effect gradually disappears — e.g. of medicine or an
anaesthetic), *feel run down* (feel exhausted or below your normal
level of health/energy — a genuinely common idiomatic collocation).

**BrE / AmE note:** British **chemist's** (the shop, also "pharmacy")
vs. American **drugstore/pharmacy** for the same kind of shop; British
**GP** (general practitioner, one's regular family doctor) vs. American
**primary care physician** (often just "my doctor" in everyday
speech); British **off sick** ("I was off sick yesterday") vs.
American **out sick** — the same small preposition-choice pattern
already seen with "at/on the weekend," now applied to health.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** physical health vocabulary (symptom,
diagnosis, prescription, recovery, exhausted, dizzy), mental wellbeing
vocabulary (stress, anxious, overwhelmed, burnout, cope with).
Intercultural note: openness about discussing mental health, and what
counts as an appropriate level of detail to share, varies significantly
by culture and personal comfort — this module's speaking tasks are
built around invented, generic scenarios specifically so no learner is
ever required to disclose anything real or personal.

## Lesson 7.1 — "He Must Be Exhausted — Modals of Deduction, Present"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
`must + base verb` to express a confident deduction based on evidence;
(2) use `might/could + base verb` to express a possible, less certain
deduction; (3) use `can't + base verb` to express that something is
logically impossible given the evidence; (4) support a deduction with
a stated reason (`...because...`).

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 3 (modal-adjacent
conditional forms — the deduction modals are grammatically related but
functionally distinct, worth contrasting briefly).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor describes a simple scenario with clear
evidence ("My colleague has been yawning all day and drinking a lot of
coffee") and asks learners to guess what's true, before formal
presentation, surfacing the natural instinct to deduce before naming
the grammar.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "He's been yawning all day. He must
be exhausted. She's smiling and humming — she might be in a good mood.
It can't be that serious — she's still laughing about it." Highlight
explicitly: `must` = a confident, evidence-based conclusion (not
obligation, the more familiar meaning of `must` from earlier levels —
worth contrasting directly, since this is a genuinely different use of
the same word); `might/could` = a plausible but uncertain guess;
`can't` = confident that something is *not* true, based on evidence
that contradicts it.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 8 short scenario
cards (each with 2-3 pieces of evidence) and make a deduction for each
using `must/might/can't`, stating their reason.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In pairs, Learner A describes a
short invented scenario with some evidence (about a friend, a
colleague, a classmate — always invented/generic, never a real
disclosure); Learner B makes a deduction using the correct modal and
explains their reasoning, then swap.

**Speaking activity.** The partner deduction exchange above, followed
by a short whole-class "mystery scenario" where the instructor gives
evidence one clue at a time and the class collectively deduces what's
happening.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Is it possible to be
completely confident about a deduction and still be wrong? Can you
think of a time you were sure about something and later found out you
were mistaken?" — a genuinely humbling, reflective B1 question that
tempers the grammar's apparent certainty with real epistemic humility.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short conversation in
which two people make deductions about a third person's wellbeing
(5-6 exchanges) and note which modal each speaker uses and why.

**Reading activity — extended reading & inference (8 min).** Read a
short text (150 words) describing someone's recent behaviour (without
stating their health directly). Answer 2 literal questions and 2
inference questions using deduction modals in your answers ("Based on
the text, what must be true about this person? What might be true?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 deduction sentences about an
invented scenario of your choice, using `must/might/could/can't`
correctly with a stated reason for each.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill the contracted, connected
form `can't` /kɑːnt/ (BrE) vs. /kænt/ (AmE) — genuinely one of the
clearest BrE/AmE pronunciation differences learners will hear — and
stress on the modal itself when the deduction is the main point of
the sentence ("She MUST be exhausted").

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A deduction-strength card-sorting game
(sort 9 example sentences by must/might/can't) reviewed against the
evidence given for each.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that the chosen modal
matches the strength of the evidence given (not just grammatically
correct, but logically appropriate) during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners prepare one invented healthcare-style scenario
(3-4 symptoms or pieces of evidence, entirely fictional) ready for
Lesson 7.2's interview roleplay.

**Revision.** Lesson 7.2 opens with learners briefly describing their
homework scenario in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one sentence using
`must not` (for a confident negative obligation-based deduction, a
subtly different meaning from `can't` worth flagging as a nuance for
later, more advanced study).

## Lesson 7.2 — "She Might Have Been Stressed — Modals of Deduction, Past & a Healthcare Interview"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
past deduction modals correctly (`must have/might have/can't have +
past participle`); (2) use them to deduce about a past situation based
on later evidence; (3) ask and answer healthcare-interview-style
questions clearly, sensitively, and with appropriate formality; (4)
describe both physical and mental wellbeing using appropriate
vocabulary and register.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 7.1 (present deduction modals),
Level III Module 1 (present perfect, the past participle's other major
use, worth contrasting briefly).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor describes a simple past scenario with
evidence discovered afterward ("The meeting room light was still on
when I left last night") and elicits a guess using `must have/might
have`, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "She looked exhausted this morning —
she must have slept badly. He didn't answer any calls yesterday — he
might have been busy, or he might have been unwell. That can't have
been easy for them." Highlight the direct present→past mapping
(`must be` → `must have been`; `might be` → `might have been`; `can't
be` → `can't have been`) — the same logical-strength distinctions from
Lesson 7.1, now applied to something already finished. Then introduce
the healthcare-interview structure: a clear opening question (`Could
you describe your symptoms?`), specific follow-up questions (`How long
have you been feeling this way? / Have you noticed anything that makes
it better or worse?`), and a clear, calm closing summary.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 6 present-deduction
sentences into past-deduction sentences, checking past participle
accuracy.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In pairs, Learner A plays a patient
describing their invented (fictional, generic) symptoms using present
perfect (`I've been feeling...`) from their Lesson 7.1 homework
scenario; Learner B plays a healthcare professional, asking clarifying
questions and making one appropriate past-deduction statement
(`It sounds like you might have been overdoing it recently`), then
swap roles.

**Speaking activity — interview task.** The full patient/professional
interview roleplay above — this lesson's core task, and the direct
basis for Module 7's assignment. Pairs are reminded explicitly that
all scenarios are invented, and that the goal is clear, sensitive
communication, not medical accuracy.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think it's
important for a healthcare professional to ask several specific
questions, rather than just guessing what's wrong? How is this similar
to the fact-finding questions you practised in Level II?" — deliberately
connects back to Level II Module 8's fact-finding sequence, showing
learners how a familiar skill has matured into a more sensitive,
higher-stakes register.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short healthcare-style
interview (5-6 exchanges) and note the symptoms described and the
professional's closing summary.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written healthcare-advice
text (generic, not real medical advice — a fictional example for
language purposes only) and identify its opening question, follow-up
questions, and closing summary structure.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short written summary (5-6
sentences) of an invented healthcare interview, using at least one
present-deduction and one past-deduction sentence.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill the connected, weak-form
pronunciation of `have` in past deduction modals ("must have" →
/ˈmʌstəv/) in natural fast speech, and calm, reassuring intonation
appropriate to a healthcare-interview context (recycling Level II's
complaint-handling "reassuring" intonation work in a more sensitive
register).

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A wellbeing-vocabulary matching game
(symptom, diagnosis, prescription, recovery, stress, anxious,
overwhelmed, cope with) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct past-deduction
modal formation and a genuinely sensitive, appropriately formal
interview register during the roleplay.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their interview roleplay (or a written
summary of it) for Module 7's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 7.1 scenario recap.
Module 7's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one `must not have`
sentence, correctly distinguishing it from `can't have` as a subtle
nuance worth previewing.

## Module 7 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-3.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "He's been yawning all day. He ___ be exhausted." (confident
   deduction) — (a) must ✓ (b) might (c) can't (d) should
2. "She's smiling and humming. She ___ be in a good mood." (a
   plausible, less certain guess) — (a) must (b) might ✓ (c) can't
   (d) has to
3. "It ___ be that serious — she's still laughing about it." (evidence
   contradicts it) — (a) must (b) might (c) can't ✓ (d) could
4. "She looked exhausted this morning. She ___ badly." (past
   deduction) — (a) must sleep (b) must have slept ✓ (c) must slept
   (d) must sleeping
5. "He didn't answer any calls. He ___ busy." (a plausible past guess)
   — (a) might be (b) might have been ✓ (c) might been (d) might has
   been
6. "___, he's just tired from the journey." (a paraphrase for "must
   be") — (a) It's likely that ✓ (b) There's no way that (c) It's
   impossible that (d) It's certain that never
7. In British English, you would go to the ___ to buy over-the-counter
   medicine. — (a) drugstore (b) chemist's ✓ (c) clinic (d) surgery
   store
8. Which phrase means "feel exhausted or below your normal energy
   level"? — (a) come down with (b) get over (c) feel run down ✓ (d)
   wear off
9. "Could you describe your ___?" (an opening healthcare-interview
   question) — (a) opinion (b) symptoms ✓ (c) history (d) plans
10. "That ___ have been easy for them." (a sympathetic past deduction)
    — (a) can't ✓ (b) mustn't (c) shouldn't (d) won't

## Module 7 Assignment — "A Healthcare Interview — Roleplay & Reflection"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Record yourself (or perform
with a partner) a healthcare-interview roleplay, 90 seconds to 2
minutes, using entirely invented/fictional symptoms and details (never
real personal health information). Include: a healthcare professional
opening with `Could you describe your symptoms?` and at least 2
specific follow-up questions; a patient describing symptoms using
present perfect (`I've been feeling...`); at least one present-
deduction sentence (`must/might/can't be`); at least one past-
deduction sentence (`must have/might have/can't have been`); and a
clear closing summary from the professional.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct present- and past-deduction modal
  formation, correct present perfect for describing ongoing symptoms.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct health/wellbeing words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — opening question, follow-up questions,
  symptom description, both deduction types, and a closing summary all
  present.
- **Communicative quality** — does the interview sound genuinely
  attentive and sensitive, not mechanical or rushed?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register calm,
  professional, and appropriately formal throughout, and does the
  closing summary genuinely and accurately reflect what was discussed?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 7
completed for the learner.
