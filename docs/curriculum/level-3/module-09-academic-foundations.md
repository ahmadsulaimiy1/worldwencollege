# WEC — Level III, Module 9: Academic Foundations

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-3-intermediate.md`'s Level III build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 9, the learner
can: write a clear topic sentence and support it with specific
details; structure a paragraph and a short essay (thesis + two
supporting points + conclusion) coherently; acknowledge where an idea
or fact comes from using basic citation awareness; use the level's
full discourse-marker toolkit (cause, contrast, illustration,
conclusion) within one connected piece; deliver a longer, structured
prepared talk and respond to follow-up questions.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 9.1 ("One Idea, Well Supported — Paragraph Structure & Topic
Sentences"); Lesson 9.2 ("According to the Source... — Acknowledging
Ideas & a Structured Talk"); Module 9 Quiz (10 questions); Module 9
Assignment ("A Short Structured Essay & Talk").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** This paragraph will focus
on... — For example,.../For instance,... — In conclusion,.../To sum
up,... — According to [the source],... — This idea comes from... —
Firstly,.../Secondly,.../Finally,...

**Discourse markers this module (a consolidation set — the level's
full connector toolkit, used together for the first time):** cause
(*because, so*), contrast (*although, however*), illustration
(*for example, for instance*), sequencing an argument (*firstly,
secondly, finally*), and closing (*in conclusion, to sum up*) — this
module deliberately draws together connector families introduced
separately across Modules 1-8 into one coherent piece of structured
writing, the level's culminating academic-writing skill.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *draw on [a source/
idea]* (use it as a basis for your own point), *point out [something]*
(highlight or mention it explicitly), *set out [an argument]*
(present it clearly, in order), *sum up* (summarise, especially at the
end of a piece), *build on [an idea]* (develop or extend it further).

**BrE / AmE note:** two punctuation-naming differences genuinely worth
knowing for academic writing: British **full stop** vs. American
**period** for the "." punctuation mark; British **brackets** vs.
American **parentheses** for "( )" (a British "bracket" alone can also
mean the square kind "[ ]," where American English would more often
specify "square brackets" — worth a moment's clarification, since
instructions referencing punctuation can otherwise genuinely confuse a
learner used to the other variety).

**Key vocabulary previewed:** academic-writing vocabulary (topic
sentence, supporting detail, thesis, structure, coherence, source),
consolidating academic vocabulary from Module 2 (lecture, seminar,
coursework). Intercultural note: expectations around how directly a
thesis or main point should be stated up front, versus built up to
gradually, can differ across academic and rhetorical traditions; this
module teaches WEC's direct, topic-sentence-first structure as one
widely useful international-academic-English convention, not the only
valid way to structure an argument.

## Lesson 9.1 — "One Idea, Well Supported — Paragraph Structure & Topic Sentences"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) write
a clear topic sentence stating a paragraph's one main idea; (2)
support it with 2-3 specific details or examples, using `for example/
for instance`; (3) use `because/so/although/however` to connect ideas
smoothly within the paragraph; (4) write a concluding sentence that
closes the paragraph without simply repeating the topic sentence
word-for-word.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 2 (note-taking,
summarising — the raw material a paragraph often organises); Modules
3-8 (all the discourse markers this lesson now consolidates).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shows one well-organised paragraph and
one disorganised list of the same facts with no clear structure, and
asks learners which is easier to follow and why, before formal
presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model a full paragraph, annotated aloud:
"**Topic sentence:** Remote work has changed how many people balance
their professional and personal lives. **Supporting detail:** For
example, employees no longer lose time commuting, which many use for
exercise or family time instead. **Supporting detail, with
contrast:** Although some workers miss daily in-person contact with
colleagues, most report feeling more in control of their schedule.
**Concluding sentence:** Overall, remote work seems to offer more
flexibility than it costs in connection." Highlight explicitly: one
paragraph = one main idea (the topic sentence), never several unrelated
ideas crammed together; supporting details make the topic sentence
concrete and specific, not just repeated in different words; the
concluding sentence closes the thought, often by restating the main
idea's *significance* rather than its exact wording.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given a jumbled paragraph
(topic sentence, 2 supporting details, and a conclusion, all mixed up)
and reorder it correctly, identifying each part's function.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners choose one topic they
know well (a hobby, their field of study or work, a place they know)
and write a full structured paragraph (topic sentence + 2 supporting
details using `for example` + one `although/however` contrast +
a concluding sentence), then swap with a partner, who identifies each
structural part.

**Speaking activity.** Learners read their paragraph aloud to a
partner, who then summarises it back in one sentence — a direct,
functional test of whether the topic sentence and structure genuinely
communicated the main idea clearly.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think starting
a paragraph with the main idea (rather than building up to it slowly)
is a useful convention in a lot of academic and professional English
writing? Can you think of a situation where a different structure
might work better?" — directly connects to the module's intercultural
note about varying rhetorical traditions, avoiding a simplistic
"this is the only correct way" framing.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short spoken explanation
structured as a paragraph (topic sentence, details, conclusion) and
identify each part on a simple worksheet.

**Reading activity — extended reading & inference (8 min).** Read a
well-structured paragraph (150-180 words) on a generic academic or
professional topic. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 inference
questions ("The paragraph doesn't state this directly, but what can
you infer is the writer's overall attitude toward the topic?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Revise your independent-practice paragraph
based on your partner's one-sentence summary — does it match what you
intended? Adjust the topic sentence or details if not.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill clear, deliberate pausing
between a paragraph's structural parts when reading aloud (a brief
pause after the topic sentence, between supporting details, and before
the conclusion) — a genuine fluency point about how spoken delivery
can make structure audible, not just visible on a page.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An academic-writing vocabulary matching
game (topic sentence, supporting detail, thesis, structure, coherence,
source) with simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that each paragraph has
one clear main idea (not several), genuine specific supporting detail
(not vague restatement), and a real concluding sentence, during
independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a topic they'd like to argue an opinion
about (for Lesson 9.2's structured talk) and jot down a thesis
statement and 2 supporting points.

**Revision.** Lesson 9.2 opens with learners briefly stating their
homework thesis in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a second supporting
paragraph on a different aspect of the same topic, practising the
one-paragraph-one-idea principle across multiple paragraphs.

## Lesson 9.2 — "According to the Source... — Acknowledging Ideas & a Structured Talk"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
acknowledge where an idea, fact, or opinion comes from using basic
citation-awareness language (`according to.../this idea comes
from...`); (2) outline a short structured essay or talk (thesis + two
supporting points + conclusion); (3) deliver a longer (2-3 minute)
structured prepared talk using the level's full discourse-marker
toolkit; (4) respond calmly and clearly to two follow-up questions.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 9.1 (paragraph structure), Level
III Module 3 (presentation skills, now extended to a longer, more
structured talk), Level III Module 6 (attributing a claim to a
source, now formalised as basic citation awareness).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor makes one claim and then immediately
models acknowledging where it came from ("Remote work has grown
significantly in recent years — this is based on what I've read in
several general news reports, not a specific statistic I can cite
precisely") — modelling honest, appropriately hedged acknowledgement
rather than either an unsupported claim or a fabricated precise
citation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the thesis + two supporting points +
conclusion structure explicitly, as an outline: **Thesis** (your main
argued position, one sentence) → **Point 1** (with a supporting
example, `for example`) → **Point 2**, ideally acknowledging a
counter-consideration (`although`) → **Conclusion** (`in conclusion/to
sum up`, restating the thesis's significance). Then model basic
citation awareness: when relaying a specific fact, name where it
generally comes from (`According to several studies I've come
across.../This idea draws on something I read recently...`) rather
than stating it as pure personal knowledge — explicitly note that full
citation mechanics (exact sources, formal reference lists) are a
Level IV+ skill; this level's goal is simply the *habit* of
acknowledging sources honestly.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners take their Lesson 9.1 homework
thesis and 2 points and build a full outline using the four-part
structure, checking with a partner that the thesis is a genuine,
arguable position (not just a topic).

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners develop their outline into
a full short talk script (using `firstly/secondly/finally` to sequence
the points, `for example` for support, `although/however` for a
counter-consideration, and `in conclusion` to close), and rehearse it
once.

**Speaking activity — presentation task (capstone).** Learners deliver
their 2-3 minute structured talk to a small group (or the class), who
listen for the four structural parts and ask 2 follow-up questions
each, which the presenter answers on the spot — this level's most
substantial, sustained speaking task, consolidating Module 3's pitch
and Module 5's case-study presentation skills into a single
longer-form talk, and the direct basis for Module 9's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "When you don't have an
exact source for something you believe to be true, do you think it's
better to state it plainly, acknowledge the uncertainty honestly, or
avoid mentioning it at all? Why?" — a genuinely important intellectual-
honesty question, directly relevant to the citation-awareness skill
just practised.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short structured talk (the
four-part thesis/points/conclusion shape) and take notes identifying
each part, recycling Module 2's note-taking skill in its most
demanding application yet.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written short-essay excerpt
using the same four-part structure and one basic citation-awareness
phrase, and label each structural part.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write your talk's four-part outline as a
clean short written version (a short essay, 8-10 sentences).

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill confident, clearly-paced
delivery across a longer, multi-part talk — including deliberate,
brief pauses at each structural transition (`firstly`, `however`, `in
conclusion`) so a listener can follow the shape by ear, and calm,
composed intonation when answering an unscripted follow-up question.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A citation-awareness phrase-matching
game (according to, this idea comes from, based on, draws on) matched
to appropriate contexts.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks for a genuine four-part
structure (not a list of unconnected points), at least one honest
source-acknowledgement, and composed responses to follow-up questions
during the presentation task.

**Homework.** Learners refine their talk script based on any
questions or feedback received, ready for Module 9's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 9.1 thesis recap.
Module 9's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a third supporting point
to their talk, adjusting their `firstly/secondly/finally` sequencing
language accordingly.

## Module 9 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-3.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. What is the main function of a topic sentence? — (a) to end the
   paragraph (b) to state the paragraph's one main idea ✓ (c) to give
   an example (d) to cite a source
2. "___, employees no longer lose time commuting." (introducing an
   example) — (a) However (b) For example ✓ (c) In conclusion (d) So
3. "Although some workers miss in-person contact, ___ report feeling
   more in control of their schedule." — (a) most ✓ (b) none (c)
   nobody (d) any
4. What should a concluding sentence generally do? — (a) repeat the
   topic sentence word-for-word (b) close the paragraph, often
   restating the main idea's significance ✓ (c) introduce a brand new
   idea (d) ask a question
5. Which phrase shows honest, basic citation awareness (not full
   formal citation)? — (a) This is definitely 100% true. (b) According
   to several studies I've come across... ✓ (c) Everyone knows this.
   (d) I read it somewhere, so it must be true.
6. In British English, the "." punctuation mark is usually called a: —
   (a) period (b) full stop ✓ (c) point (d) dot
7. A short structured talk in this module generally follows which
   order? — (a) conclusion, thesis, points (b) thesis, supporting
   points, conclusion ✓ (c) points, conclusion, thesis (d) no
   particular order
8. Which phrase means "use something as a basis for your own point"? —
   (a) point out (b) sum up (c) draw on ✓ (d) set out
9. "___, remote work seems to offer more flexibility than it costs in
   connection." (closing a paragraph) — (a) For example (b) Overall ✓
   (c) Firstly (d) Because
10. What is this level's stated goal regarding citation, as opposed to
    full academic citation mechanics? — (a) memorising formal
    reference formats (b) the habit of honestly acknowledging where an
    idea comes from ✓ (c) avoiding mentioning sources at all (d)
    quoting sources word-for-word only

## Module 9 Assignment — "A Short Structured Essay & Talk"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Prepare and deliver (record
yourself, or perform live) a structured talk, 2-3 minutes, on a topic
of your choice that you can genuinely argue a position on. Your talk
must include: a clear one-sentence thesis; two supporting points, each
with a specific example (`for example/for instance`); at least one
`although/however` acknowledgement of a counter-consideration; at
least one honest citation-awareness phrase; and a conclusion that
restates your thesis's significance (`in conclusion/to sum up`). Also
submit your talk's written outline (8-10 sentences).

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct, varied use of the discourse
  markers practised this module.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct academic-writing words
  used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this module.
- **Task completion** — thesis, two supported points, a
  counter-consideration, a citation-awareness phrase, and a conclusion
  all present in both the spoken talk and the written outline.
- **Fluency and delivery** — reasonably fluent and clearly paced for
  B1, with composed responses to any follow-up questions received.
- **Discourse coherence & register** — does the talk read/sound as one
  connected, logically structured argument (not a list of disconnected
  points), and is the register appropriately academic/professional
  throughout?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 9
completed for the learner.
