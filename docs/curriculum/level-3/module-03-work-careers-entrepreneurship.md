# AIPC — Level III, Module 3: Work, Careers & Entrepreneurship

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-3-intermediate.md`'s Level III build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 3, the learner
can: describe general workplace facts, routines, and policies using
the zero conditional; describe real future workplace possibilities and
plans using the first conditional; give a short, structured prepared
talk (a business-idea "pitch") and respond to a follow-up question;
discuss what makes an effective leader, with reasons.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 3.1 ("If You Meet the Deadline... — Zero Conditional for
Workplace Facts & Policies"); Lesson 3.2 ("If We Launch This
Product... — First Conditional & Pitching a Business Idea"); Module 3
Quiz (10 questions); Module 3 Assignment ("Pitch a Business Idea").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** If you..., you... (general
fact) — If we..., we'll... (real future possibility) — I'd like to
propose... — One challenge we might face is... — My plan is to... —
Any questions?

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — cause/effect,
professional register):** *as a result*, *therefore*, *this means
that* — used to connect a workplace cause to its consequence in a
more formal register than Level II's `so`/`because` ("Demand has
grown. As a result, we need to hire more staff.").

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *set up a business*
(start a company), *come up with [an idea]* (think of/invent an idea),
*take a risk/take on a risk* (accept a possible negative outcome for a
potential gain), *run a company* (manage/operate a business day to
day), *scale up* (grow a business's operations).

**BrE / AmE note:** job-application vocabulary differs: British
**CV** (curriculum vitae) vs. American **résumé/resume** for the same
document; British **made redundant** (a position is eliminated) vs.
American **laid off** (though both varieties use "laid off"
informally, "redundant/redundancy" is the more distinctly British
formal term); British **line manager** vs. American **manager/
supervisor** for one's direct superior.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** workplace vocabulary (deadline, target,
client, colleague, promotion, workload), entrepreneurship vocabulary
(start-up, funding, investor, launch, competitor, revenue), leadership
vocabulary (decisive, accountable, delegate, inspire). Intercultural
note: attitudes toward risk-taking, hierarchy, and directness in the
workplace vary significantly across cultures — worth naming before
this module's discussion activities, so no single workplace culture is
treated as the automatic "normal" one.

## Lesson 3.1 — "If You Meet the Deadline... — Zero Conditional for Workplace Facts & Policies"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
the zero conditional correctly (`If + present simple, present
simple`); (2) use it to state general workplace facts, routines, and
policies (not one-off future possibilities); (3) distinguish a general
truth ("If a business doesn't adapt, it fails") from a specific,
real-future prediction (previewed here, formally taught in Lesson
3.2); (4) use `as a result`/`therefore` to connect a workplace cause
to its effect.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level I, Module 3 (present simple for
routine/fact — the zero conditional's grammatical foundation).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one true general workplace rule
("If you're late three times, you get a formal warning at most
companies") and asks learners whether the same rule is generally true
where they've worked or studied, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "If you meet a deadline, your
manager is satisfied. If a team communicates well, projects usually
go more smoothly. Demand has grown this year. As a result, the company
needs to hire more staff." Highlight explicitly: the zero conditional
states something generally, reliably true — a fact or a policy, not a
single future event — using present simple in both clauses; `as a
result` and `therefore` connect a stated fact to its typical
workplace consequence, a more formal register than `so`.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners complete 8 zero-conditional
sentence halves about general workplace facts/policies (some
deliberately generic and safe, e.g. company rules; some about broader
truths, e.g. market behaviour), then add an `as a result`/`therefore`
sentence to two of them.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 4-5 zero-conditional
sentences about general truths in a workplace or field they know
(their own job, a family member's job, or a field they're studying
toward), then explain one to a partner, who asks one clarifying
question.

**Speaking activity.** The partner explanation exchange above, plus a
short whole-class share of one particularly interesting workplace
"general truth" per pair.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Do you think most
workplace rules exist for good reasons, or are some just tradition
with no real purpose anymore? Can you think of an example of each?" —
a genuinely critical B1-level question inviting learners to evaluate,
not just describe, workplace norms.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone describing their
company's general policies (5-6 zero-conditional sentences) and
complete a simple policy-summary worksheet.

**Reading activity — extended reading & inference (8 min).** Read a
short workplace-culture article excerpt (150 words) describing general
practices at a generic, unnamed company. Answer 2 literal questions
and 2 inference questions ("The text doesn't say the company values
work-life balance directly — what evidence suggests it does or
doesn't?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write 4-5 zero-conditional sentences about
general facts or policies in a field you're interested in, including
at least one `as a result`/`therefore` connector.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill sentence stress that falls
on the key content words in a zero-conditional sentence ("If you MEET
the DEADline, your MANager is SATisfied") rather than on the function
words (if, the, is) — a fluency point about which words genuinely
carry meaning.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A workplace-vocabulary matching game
(deadline, target, client, colleague, promotion, workload) with
simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct zero-conditional
formation (present simple in both clauses, not a mix with `will`) —
the lesson's core target error — during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners think of one real or realistic business idea
(a product, a service, or an improvement to something that already
exists) and jot down 3-4 rough notes about it, ready for Lesson 3.2's
pitch task.

**Revision.** Lesson 3.2 opens with learners briefly describing their
homework business idea in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one zero-conditional
sentence about a scientific or natural general truth (not
workplace-related) — e.g. "If you heat water to 100°C, it boils" — to
see the same structure applied outside the module's main context.

## Lesson 3.2 — "If We Launch This Product... — First Conditional & Pitching a Business Idea"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
the first conditional correctly (`If + present simple, will + base
verb`); (2) use it to describe a real, specific future possibility
(not a general truth); (3) structure and deliver a short prepared talk
(a "pitch") with a clear idea, at least one benefit, and one
acknowledged challenge; (4) respond calmly and clearly to an
unscripted follow-up question.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 3.1 (zero conditional, workplace
vocabulary).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor contrasts one zero-conditional
sentence and one first-conditional sentence about the same general
topic ("If a business doesn't adapt, it fails" vs. "If we don't adapt
this quarter, we'll lose customers") and asks learners to identify the
difference in meaning before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "If we launch this product in
spring, we'll reach more customers before summer. If demand is high,
we'll need more funding. One challenge we might face is competition —
if a competitor launches something similar first, we'll need a clear
advantage." Highlight: first conditional describes one real,
specific possible future situation and its likely result — distinct
from the zero conditional's general, always-true pattern. Then model
a short pitch structure explicitly: **the idea** (what it is, in one
sentence) → **the benefit** (why it matters, using a first-conditional
sentence) → **one challenge, acknowledged honestly** → **a brief
closing invitation for questions**.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Pair work: learners take turns pitching
a simple, pre-given business idea (from prompt cards) to a partner
using the four-part structure, with the partner asking one follow-up
question at the end.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners develop their own homework
business idea into a full short pitch (4-5 sentences: idea, benefit
using first conditional, one honest challenge, an invitation for
questions), and rehearse it once, silently or to themselves.

**Speaking activity — presentation task.** Learners deliver their
60-90 second pitch to a small group (or the whole class, group size
permitting), who listen and ask at least one genuine follow-up
question each — this level's first structured presentation task, and
the direct basis for Module 3's assignment. The presenter responds to
at least one question on the spot, unscripted.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "When someone pitches you
an idea and honestly admits a challenge or weakness, does that make
you trust the idea more or less? Why?" — connects the pitch
structure's deliberate inclusion of an "honest challenge" to a real
judgement about credibility and communication style.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short business pitch and
identify its idea, benefit, acknowledged challenge, and the presenter's
answer to a follow-up question.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written pitch summary (a
one-paragraph business proposal) and label its four structural parts.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write your pitch (from independent practice)
as a clean short written version (5-6 sentences), checked for correct
first-conditional formation.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill confident, clear delivery
pace and pausing for a prepared talk — noticeably more measured and
deliberate than casual conversational speech, plus falling intonation
on statements of fact to sound assured rather than hesitant, a genuine
public-speaking fluency point.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An entrepreneurship-vocabulary matching
game (start-up, funding, investor, launch, competitor, revenue) with
simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct first-conditional
formation and a genuine four-part pitch structure (not just a list of
features) during the presentation task.

**Homework.** Learners refine their pitch based on any feedback
received, ready for Module 3's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 3.1 business-idea
one-sentence recap. Module 3's Quiz and Assignment draw on both
lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a second first-
conditional sentence addressing what happens if the acknowledged
challenge actually occurs ("If a competitor does launch something
similar, we'll focus on..."), showing contingency thinking.

## Module 3 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-3.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "If you ___ a deadline, your manager is satisfied." (a general
   fact) — (a) meet ✓ (b) will meet (c) met (d) meeting
2. "If we launch this product in spring, we ___ more customers." (a
   real future possibility) — (a) reach (b) will reach ✓ (c) reached
   (d) reaching
3. Which sentence is a general truth (zero conditional), not a
   specific future prediction? — (a) If we hire more staff, we'll meet
   the target. (b) If a business doesn't adapt, it fails. ✓ (c) If it
   rains tomorrow, we'll cancel the launch. (d) If sales increase next
   month, we'll expand.
4. "Demand has grown this year. ___, the company needs to hire more
   staff." — (a) If (b) As a result ✓ (c) Even though (d) Unless
5. "If demand is high, we ___ more funding." — (a) need (b) will need
   ✓ (c) needed (d) needing
6. In British English, the document you send when applying for a job
   is usually called a: — (a) résumé (b) CV ✓ (c) portfolio (d) bio
7. A good short pitch typically includes the idea, the benefit, and
   also: — (a) a list of every possible feature (b) an honestly
   acknowledged challenge ✓ (c) a guarantee of success (d) no
   questions allowed
8. Which phrase means "start a company"? — (a) run a company (b) set
   up a business ✓ (c) scale up (d) take a risk
9. "If a competitor launches something similar, we ___ a clear
   advantage." — (a) need (b) will need ✓ (c) needed (d) needing
10. Which word describes someone who takes responsibility for their
    decisions? — (a) decisive (b) accountable ✓ (c) delegate (d)
    inspire

## Module 3 Assignment — "Pitch a Business Idea"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Record yourself (or perform
live) delivering a short business pitch, 60-90 seconds. Your pitch
must include: at least one zero-conditional sentence stating a general
truth relevant to your idea or its market; at least two first-
conditional sentences describing real future possibilities (a benefit,
and what happens if a challenge occurs); one honestly acknowledged
challenge; and a closing invitation for questions, followed by a
written answer to one likely question someone might ask.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct zero-conditional and
  first-conditional formation, used in the right context for each.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct workplace/entrepreneurship
  words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — idea, benefit, honest challenge, and
  question-response all present.
- **Communicative quality** — is the idea genuinely specific and
  understandable, and does the acknowledged challenge sound honest
  rather than token?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — does the pitch use `as a
  result`/`therefore`-level professional register appropriately, and
  does it flow as one persuasive, connected talk rather than a list of
  disconnected claims?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 3
completed for the learner.
