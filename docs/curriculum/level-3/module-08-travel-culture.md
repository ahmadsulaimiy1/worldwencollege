# WEC-LC — Level III, Module 8: Travel & Culture

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-3-intermediate.md`'s Level III build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 8, the learner
can: describe hypothetical or imaginary travel situations using the
second conditional; distinguish a real future possibility (first
conditional) from a purely hypothetical one (second conditional);
compare cultural norms and customs respectfully, without ranking one
as superior; discuss what "culture shock" or adjusting to a new place
might involve.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 8.1 ("If I Travelled More... — Second Conditional"); Lesson 8.2
("That Would Be Considered Rude Where I'm From — Cultural
Comparison"); Module 8 Quiz (10 questions); Module 8 Assignment
("Comparing Customs — A Respectful Cultural Comparison").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** If I travelled more, I
would... — If you could live anywhere, where would you live? — That
would be considered.../In my culture,... — Whereas.../In contrast,...
— I don't think there's a "right" way — just a different one.

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — comparison across
two things):** *whereas*, *in contrast*, *similarly* — used to compare
two customs, places, or norms directly and evenhandedly, without
implying one is better ("In my country, people often arrive a few
minutes late. In contrast, punctuality is taken very seriously in
some other cultures.").

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *fit in* (feel/be
accepted as part of a group or place), *stand out* (be noticeably
different from those around you — not necessarily negative), *pick up
[a language/habit]* (learn something informally, through exposure
rather than formal study), *settle in [a new place]* (become
comfortable and adjusted somewhere new), *adjust to [a new culture]*
(gradually adapt).

**BrE / AmE note:** **gap year** — a British-originated term (a year
students often take between school and university to travel, work, or
volunteer) that has spread internationally, including into American
English, though American speakers have traditionally been just as
likely to say "a year off"; British **fancy dress** (a costume worn
for a themed party) vs. American **costume** for the same idea —
"fancy dress party" can genuinely confuse an American English speaker,
who might expect formal clothing rather than a costume.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** travel vocabulary (itinerary, excursion,
accommodation, local customs, jet lag), intercultural vocabulary
(etiquette, norm, custom, culture shock, hospitality). Intercultural
note: this module is explicitly about comparing cultures *respectfully*
— every activity is framed around "different, not better or worse,"
and learners are never asked to rank cultures or make sweeping
generalisations about an entire country or people.

## Lesson 8.1 — "If I Travelled More... — Second Conditional"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) form
the second conditional correctly (`If + past simple, would + base
verb`); (2) use it for hypothetical, imaginary, or unlikely situations
(distinct from the first conditional's real future possibility); (3)
ask and answer classic hypothetical questions (`If you could...,
where/what would you...?`); (4) use the second conditional to imagine
a travel scenario in reasonable detail.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level III, Module 3 (first conditional —
the essential contrast point for this lesson).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor asks one classic hypothetical question
("If you could visit any country tomorrow, where would you go?") and
takes a few quick answers before formal presentation, without yet
naming the grammar.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "If I travelled more, I would learn
about different cultures. If I had more time off, I'd visit South
America." Then explicitly contrast with a first-conditional sentence
about a real plan: "If I travel next year, I will visit three
countries" (a real, likely plan) vs. "If I travelled every year, I'd
run out of savings" (a more hypothetical, less certain framing).
Highlight: second conditional uses past simple in the `if`-clause
(despite referring to present/future meaning — a genuinely
counter-intuitive point worth naming directly) and `would + base verb`
in the result clause.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners complete 8 second-conditional
sentence halves about hypothetical travel scenarios, then identify
which of 4 additional example sentences are first conditional (real
possibility) vs. second conditional (hypothetical), explaining their
reasoning.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners write 5 second-conditional
sentences about hypothetical travel scenarios (a dream destination, an
unlimited budget, unlimited time), then interview a partner using at
least 3 `If you could..., would you...?` questions.

**Speaking activity.** The partner interview above, followed by a
short whole-class share of one especially interesting hypothetical
travel answer per pair.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Do you think imagining a
hypothetical situation (like an unlimited travel budget) can actually
teach you something real about your own priorities? Why or why not?"
— connects the grammar's imaginative function to a genuine, reflective
question about what hypothetical thinking is actually useful for.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone describing a
hypothetical travel dream (6-7 sentences, second conditional) and
complete a simple "if / would" grid worksheet.

**Reading activity — extended reading & inference (8 min).** Read a
short "If I could travel anywhere..." blog-style excerpt (150 words).
Answer 2 literal questions and 2 inference questions ("The writer
doesn't say this directly, but what can you infer about what they
value most in travel?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short paragraph (5-6 sentences)
describing your own hypothetical dream trip, using second conditional
correctly at least 3 times.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill the contracted, connected
form `'d` for `would` in natural speech ("I'd visit South America")
and the correct, non-reduced pronunciation of `if` at the start of a
sentence versus its weak, unstressed form mid-sentence.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A travel-vocabulary matching game
(itinerary, excursion, accommodation, local customs, jet lag) with
simple definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks correct second-conditional
formation (past simple in the `if`-clause, not present simple) and
correct distinction from first conditional, during guided practice.

**Homework.** Learners think of one real custom or norm from their own
culture or background that might surprise someone from elsewhere, and
jot down a few notes about it, ready for Lesson 8.2's comparison
activity.

**Revision.** Lesson 8.2 opens with learners briefly naming their
homework custom in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one sentence using `were`
instead of `was` in the second conditional for all subjects ("If I
were you, I'd..." — recycling Level II's `if I were you` advice
structure and connecting it explicitly to this lesson's grammar as the
same underlying pattern).

## Lesson 8.2 — "That Would Be Considered Rude Where I'm From — Cultural Comparison"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
the second conditional to describe how a hypothetical action would be
perceived in a particular cultural context; (2) compare two customs or
norms respectfully using `whereas/in contrast/similarly`; (3) discuss
what "culture shock" or adjusting to a new place might involve; (4)
avoid overgeneralising when describing a culture, using appropriately
hedged language.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 8.1 (second conditional), Level III
Module 4 (formal opinion/contrast language, now applied to cultural
comparison specifically).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor shares one genuine, respectfully-
framed example of a cultural norm difference (e.g. gift-giving customs,
meal-time customs) and models hedged language ("In many places I've
read about... though this certainly isn't true everywhere") before
formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "If you arrived a few minutes late
to a casual meeting in my country, people probably wouldn't mind much.
In contrast, in some other places, that would be considered quite
rude." Highlight two things explicitly: the second conditional
describing a hypothetical social reaction, and the deliberate hedging
language (`in many places`, `some other places`, `this varies`) that
avoids sweeping claims about an entire country or culture being one
fixed way — a genuinely important intercultural-competence skill, not
just a grammar point.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 6 "custom" prompt
cards (generic, non-stereotyping — e.g. gift-giving, greetings,
punctuality, meal customs) and build a second-conditional + `whereas`
comparison sentence for each, comparing two hypothetical/generalised
contexts without naming specific real countries in a reductive way.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 8.1 homework
notes, learners prepare a short comparison (3-4 sentences) of a real
custom from their own background against a different one they've
learned about or experienced, using hedged, respectful language and at
least one second-conditional sentence.

**Speaking activity.** Learners share their comparison in a small
group, with the group discussing: "What's something you found
surprising or interesting (not right or wrong) about a custom
different from your own?" — this lesson's core task and the direct
basis for Module 8's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Why do you think it's
easy to accidentally make a whole culture sound like it's 'one way,'
even when that's not true? How can careful language help avoid that?"
— directly reinforces the hedging-language skill as a genuine critical
and ethical communication concern, not just a grammar nicety.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone describing a moment
of "culture shock" or adjustment (6-7 sentences) and note what
surprised them and how they adapted.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short respectful cultural-
comparison text (generic, hedged, non-stereotyping) and identify its
`whereas/in contrast/similarly` comparison points and its hedging
language.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short paragraph (5-6 sentences)
comparing two customs respectfully, using `whereas/in contrast` and at
least one second-conditional sentence.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill warm, curious intonation for
asking about someone's customs ("What would happen if...?") versus
flat, potentially judgemental-sounding intonation — a genuine fluency/
attitude point about how tone can undermine even grammatically
respectful language.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An intercultural-vocabulary matching
game (etiquette, norm, custom, culture shock, hospitality) with simple
definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that comparisons stay
hedged and respectful (no sweeping "all people from X do Y"
statements) during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners finalise their cultural comparison paragraph
for Module 8's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 8.1 custom-naming
recap. Module 8's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add one sentence
acknowledging that customs vary *within* a culture too, not just
between cultures ("Even within my own country, this varies by region
or family") — a genuinely sophisticated nuance for this level.

## Module 8 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-3.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "If I ___ more, I would learn about different cultures." (second
   conditional) — (a) travel (b) travelled ✓ (c) will travel (d) am
   travelling
2. "If I had more time off, I ___ visit South America." — (a) will
   (b) would ✓ (c) am (d) do
3. Which sentence describes a real, likely plan (first conditional),
   not a hypothetical? — (a) If I travelled every year, I'd run out of
   savings. (b) If I travel next year, I will visit three countries.
   ✓ (c) If I could live anywhere, I'd choose the coast. (d) If money
   were no object, I'd travel constantly.
4. "If you ___ late to a casual meeting here, people probably wouldn't
   mind." — (a) arrive (b) arrived ✓ (c) will arrive (d) arriving
5. "In my country, that's fine. ___, in some other places, it would be
   considered rude." — (a) Similarly (b) In contrast ✓ (c) Because (d)
   Therefore
6. Which phrase means "become comfortable and adjusted somewhere new"?
   — (a) stand out (b) fit in (c) settle in ✓ (d) pick up
7. What does "gap year" traditionally refer to? — (a) a type of
   passport (b) a year students take between school and university to
   travel, work, or volunteer ✓ (c) a discount on flights (d) a
   type of visa
8. Which is the more respectful, hedged way to describe a custom? —
   (a) All people from that country do this. (b) In many places I've
   read about, this is common, though it varies. ✓ (c) Everyone there
   believes this. (d) This is simply how that culture is.
9. "If I ___ you, I'd try the local food." (recycling a familiar
   structure) — (a) am (b) was (c) were ✓ (d) will be
10. Which phrase means "be noticeably different from those around
    you"? — (a) fit in (b) stand out ✓ (c) settle in (d) adjust to

## Module 8 Assignment — "Comparing Customs — A Respectful Cultural Comparison"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Write (or record) a short
respectful comparison, 10-12 sentences, of one custom or norm from
your own background and a different one you've learned about or
experienced (through travel, media, or people you know). Include: at
least 2 second-conditional sentences describing a hypothetical
situation and how it might be perceived; at least one `whereas/in
contrast/similarly` comparison sentence; hedged, non-stereotyping
language throughout (avoid claims like "all people from X..."); and a
brief reflection on what you found interesting about the difference.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct second-conditional formation.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct travel/intercultural
  words used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — hypothetical comparison, a direct
  `whereas/in contrast` comparison, and a personal reflection all
  present.
- **Communicative quality** — is the comparison genuinely specific and
  thoughtful, not generic?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the language appropriately
  hedged and respectful throughout (no sweeping generalisations about
  an entire culture), and does the piece read as a genuine, curious
  comparison rather than a ranking of "better" and "worse"?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 8
completed for the learner.
