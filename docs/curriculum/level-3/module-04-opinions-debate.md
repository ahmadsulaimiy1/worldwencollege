# WEC-LC — Level III, Module 4: Opinions & Debate

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-3-intermediate.md`'s Level III build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 4, the learner
can: state and justify an opinion using formal opinion language;
contrast two ideas within a single argument using `although/however`;
formally agree, partially agree, or disagree with someone else's
opinion; participate in a structured debate, presenting a position and
responding to a counter-argument; discuss what makes an effective
leader, with reasons.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 4.1 ("In My View... — Structured Opinion Language"); Lesson 4.2
("I See Your Point, But... — Formal Agreement, Disagreement &
Debate"); Module 4 Quiz (10 questions); Module 4 Assignment
("Structured Debate — What Makes a Good Leader?").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** In my view,.../From my
perspective,... — I would argue that... — Although..., I still
think... — I see your point, but... — I'd have to disagree, because...
— That's a fair point, however...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — contrast within an
argument):** *although*, *however*, *on the other hand*, *whereas* —
used to hold two ideas in tension within a single, more sophisticated
opinion, rather than presenting only one side ("Although the plan has
risks, I still think it's worth trying." / "Some people prefer working
alone, whereas others do their best work in a team.").

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *back up [an argument]*
(support a claim with evidence or reasons), *bring up [a point]*
(introduce a topic or idea into a discussion), *get across [an idea]*
(successfully communicate an idea so others understand it), *stand by
[an opinion]* (continue to support an opinion, especially when
challenged), *weigh up [the pros and cons]* (carefully consider both
sides before deciding).

**BrE / AmE note:** the word **quite** is a genuine false-friend
between the two varieties when used with a gradable adjective: in
British English, "That's quite good" often means *moderately* good
(a mild, somewhat qualified compliment); in American English, "That's
quite good" more often means *very* good (a stronger compliment) —
worth flagging explicitly in an opinions module, since misreading this
one small word can genuinely change how strong a stated opinion sounds.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** opinion/argument vocabulary (perspective,
standpoint, counter-argument, evidence, concede, persuasive),
leadership vocabulary recycled from Module 3 (decisive, accountable,
delegate, inspire) now used as debate content rather than isolated
vocabulary. Intercultural note: how directly people are expected to
disagree in a discussion — and how much open disagreement is
considered acceptable or even respectful — varies significantly by
culture; this module's debate format is explicitly a practised,
structured skill, not a claim that one cultural style of disagreement
is correct.

## Lesson 4.1 — "In My View... — Structured Opinion Language"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) state
an opinion using a range of formal opinion phrases (`in my view, from
my perspective, I would argue that`); (2) justify an opinion with at
least one clear reason; (3) use `although`/`however` to acknowledge a
counter-point within your own argument, making it more balanced and
persuasive; (4) recognise the difference in strength between a
tentative opinion and a firmly stated one.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level II, Module 4 (basic opinion-giving:
`I think.../I don't think...`) — this lesson formalises and extends
that foundation considerably.

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states one opinion using only "I
think..." and then restates the same opinion using formal language
("In my view..."), asking learners which sounds more suited to a
serious discussion or debate, before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model: "In my view, remote work benefits
most employees. I would argue that it improves focus and reduces
wasted commuting time. Although some people miss the social side of an
office, I still think the benefits outweigh that drawback." Highlight
explicitly: formal opinion phrases sound more considered than `I
think`; a reason (`because`/`since`, or simply a following sentence)
makes an opinion an *argument*, not just a preference; `although`
acknowledges a real counter-point without abandoning your position —
a genuinely more sophisticated, balanced way of arguing than simply
ignoring the other side.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 6 opinion prompts
(deliberately debatable, everyday topics) and build a full opinion
statement for each using a formal phrase + a reason + an `although`
counter-point acknowledgement.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners choose one topic they
genuinely have an opinion on and prepare a 3-4 sentence structured
opinion (formal phrase, reason, `although` counter-point), then share
it with a partner, who identifies which part was the opinion, which
was the reason, and which was the counter-point.

**Speaking activity.** The partner identification exchange above,
followed by a brief whole-class share of one particularly persuasive
opinion per pair.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Do you think acknowledging
the other side of an argument makes your own opinion seem weaker, or
actually stronger? Why?" — directly interrogates the lesson's core
technique, encouraging genuine critical reflection rather than rote
application.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to someone giving a structured
opinion (formal phrase, reason, counter-point acknowledgement) and
identify each of the three parts on a simple worksheet.

**Reading activity — extended reading & inference (8 min).** Read a
short opinion-column excerpt (150-180 words) on a generic, everyday
debatable topic. Answer 2 literal questions and 2 inference questions
("The writer doesn't say this outright, but what can you infer about
their view of the opposing side?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a structured opinion paragraph (4-5
sentences) on a topic of your choice, using a formal opinion phrase, a
reason, and an `although`/`however` counter-point.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill falling intonation for a
firmly stated opinion ("In my VIEW, remote work BENefits most
employees") versus a more level, tentative intonation for a hedged
opinion ("I would SAY that... perhaps...") — an intonation-carries-
attitude fluency point, directly relevant to sounding appropriately
confident (not aggressive) when arguing a position.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An opinion-strength card-sorting game:
sort 9 opinion phrases from tentative to firm (I'd say perhaps... / I
think... / In my view... / I would strongly argue that...).

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that opinions include a
genuine reason and a genuine `although`/`however` counter-point (not
just a bare formal phrase) during independent practice.

**Homework.** Learners choose a position on the question "What makes a
good leader?" and prepare 2-3 reasons for Lesson 4.2's debate.

**Revision.** Lesson 4.2 opens with learners briefly stating their
homework position in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners add a second `although`
counter-point addressing a different possible objection to their
opinion, showing they've considered more than one angle.

## Lesson 4.2 — "I See Your Point, But... — Formal Agreement, Disagreement & Debate"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) fully
agree, partially agree, or disagree with an opinion using appropriate
formal language; (2) respond to a counter-argument without becoming
personal or dismissive; (3) participate in a structured debate:
state a position, respond to a challenge, and (where genuinely
warranted) concede a fair point; (4) discuss leadership qualities with
justified reasons.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 4.1 (structured opinion language),
Level II Module 4 (basic agree/disagree language, now formalised).

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor states a debatable opinion and asks
3 learners in turn to respond with full agreement, partial agreement,
and disagreement respectively, modelling the difference in language
before formal presentation.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the agreement/disagreement scale
explicitly: **full agreement** ("I completely agree — in fact,..."),
**partial agreement** ("I see your point, but I'm not sure that's true
in every case..."), **disagreement** ("I'd have to disagree, because
...", "That's a fair point, however I think..."), and **conceding a
point** ("That's true, I hadn't considered that — although I still
think..."). Highlight that a strong debater genuinely listens and can
concede a fair point without abandoning their overall position — this
is a mark of a *stronger* arguer, not a weaker one, directly connecting
back to Lesson 4.1's discussion prompt.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Pair work: Learner A states an opinion
from a prompt card; Learner B responds using one of the four response
types (instructor assigns which type, rotating), then swap and
discuss what changed about the tone of the conversation with each
response type.

**Independent practice (10 min).** In small groups, learners hold a
structured 3-round mini-debate on "What makes a good leader?": Round 1
— each person states their position with a reason; Round 2 — each
person responds to one other person's position (agree/partially
agree/disagree, with reasoning); Round 3 — each person gives a brief
closing statement, conceding at least one fair point raised by someone
else if genuinely warranted.

**Speaking activity — presentation/discussion task.** The 3-round
mini-debate above — this lesson's core task, and the direct basis for
Module 4's assignment. Groups briefly report their range of
positions to the class afterward.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Have you ever changed your
mind during a discussion because someone made a good point? What was
it that convinced you?" — a genuinely reflective question about the
real purpose of debate (understanding, not just winning), fitting
closing framing for this module.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a short debate exchange (2
speakers, 3-4 turns) and classify each response as full agreement,
partial agreement, disagreement, or a conceded point.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short written debate transcript
excerpt and identify one example of each of the four response types.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write a short written response (4-5
sentences) to an opinion given in a prompt, using partial agreement
followed by a clearly reasoned disagreement on one specific point.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill calm, level intonation for
disagreement (avoiding a sharp, confrontational pitch, recycling Level
II's complaint-handling pronunciation work in a more formal register)
and warmer, validating intonation for conceding a point genuinely.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A response-type matching game (opinion
statements matched to an appropriate full-agreement, partial-
agreement, disagreement, or concession response).

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that disagreement stays
respectful and reasoned (never personal) and that at least one genuine
concession occurs somewhere in each group's debate.

**Homework.** Learners write a short reflection (3-4 sentences) on
which leadership quality they found most convincing across the
debate and why, ready for Module 4's assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 4.1 position recap.
Module 4's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners research (or reason through,
without external sources) a counter-argument to their own original
position and present it fairly, as an exercise in genuine
perspective-taking.

## Module 4 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-3.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "___, remote work benefits most employees." (a formal opinion
   phrase) — (a) I think (b) In my view ✓ (c) Maybe (d) Sort of
2. "Although some people miss the office, I ___ think the benefits
   outweigh that." — (a) still ✓ (b) never (c) don't (d) barely
3. Which response shows partial agreement? — (a) I completely agree.
   (b) I see your point, but I'm not sure that's true in every case. ✓
   (c) I'd have to disagree. (d) That's completely wrong.
4. "That's a fair point, ___ I still think my original idea is
   stronger." — (a) so (b) however ✓ (c) because (d) if
5. Which phrase means "support a claim with evidence or reasons"? —
   (a) bring up (b) back up ✓ (c) get across (d) weigh up
6. In British English, "That's quite good" often means: — (a) very
   good (b) moderately good ✓ (c) not good at all (d) perfect
7. Conceding a fair point during a debate generally suggests: — (a) a
   weak argument (b) a thoughtful, genuinely listening arguer ✓ (c)
   that you have lost (d) that you agree with everything
8. "___ some people prefer working alone, others do their best work in
   a team." — (a) Whereas ✓ (b) Because (c) So (d) If
9. Which phrase means "introduce a topic or idea into a discussion"? —
   (a) stand by (b) weigh up (c) bring up ✓ (d) back up
10. "I'd have to disagree, ___ the evidence doesn't support that
    claim." — (a) because ✓ (b) although (c) however (d) but still

## Module 4 Assignment — "Structured Debate — What Makes a Good Leader?"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Record yourself (or perform
live with a partner or small group) a structured discussion, 90
seconds to 2 minutes, on "What makes a good leader?" Your contribution
must include: a clearly stated position using a formal opinion phrase
and at least one reason; at least one `although`/`however` counter-point
acknowledgement within your own argument; at least one response to
someone else's point using full agreement, partial agreement, or
disagreement language; and one genuinely conceded point (something you
changed your mind about, even slightly, based on what someone else
said).

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correct formal opinion structures, correct
  `although`/`however` contrast formation.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 3 distinct opinion/argument words or
  phrases used correctly, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module.
- **Task completion** — position, reason, counter-point
  acknowledgement, a response to another view, and a genuine
  concession all present.
- **Communicative quality** — is the argument genuinely reasoned and
  specific, not a vague generalisation?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the register appropriately
  formal for a structured debate (not casual), and does the
  contribution stay respectful and evidence-based throughout, even in
  disagreement?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 4
completed for the learner.
