# WEC-LC — Level VI, Module 6: Innovation & Emerging Technologies

*Part of `docs/curriculum-level-6-mastery.md`'s Level VI build-out.*

**Module learning objectives.** By the end of Module 6, the learner
can: use speculative and counterfactual register with calibrated
confidence when discussing what does not yet exist; translate
technical material for a non-specialist audience without distorting
it; deliver a keynote address that carries a general audience through
unfamiliar material; write a grant or project proposal that states a
problem, an approach, a deliverable, and an honest risk.

**Content map (5 learning items):** module overview & key phrases;
Lesson 6.1 ("Were This to Succeed — Speculative Register &
Technical-to-General Translation"); Lesson 6.2 ("From the Stage —
The Keynote Address & the Grant Proposal"); Module 6 Quiz (10
questions); Module 6 Assignment ("A Grant Proposal & Keynote
Address").

## Reference reading: Module overview & key phrases

**Key phrases introduced this module:** Were this to succeed,... —
Had the approach been available earlier,... — This is best understood
as... — To put it in non-technical terms,... — The honest answer is
that we do not yet know whether... — What would have to be true for
this to work is...

**Discourse markers this module (functional set — calibrated
speculation):** *were this to succeed*, *what would have to be true
for this to work is*, *the honest answer is that we do not yet know
whether*, *on current evidence, the most we can say is* — language for
discussing the not-yet-existent without either overclaiming or
retreating into uselessness. The module's central discipline is
**stating what would have to be true** — a technique that converts a
vague optimism into a testable proposition, and the single most useful
habit in evaluating innovation claims.

**Phrasal verbs & collocations this module:** *scale up* (increase
capacity or output, distinguished carefully from *scale back*, Module
3), *bear out [a hypothesis]* (of evidence, to confirm it), *pan out*
(work out successfully in the end), *rule out [a possibility]*,
*build on [prior work]* — with a note that *bear out* and *pan out*
are near-synonyms differing in register (*bear out* is neutral-formal
and takes an object; *pan out* is informal and intransitive).

**BrE / AmE note.** Technical and infrastructural vocabulary diverges
in ways that matter in proposals and specifications: British
**aluminium** vs. American **aluminum** (a genuine spelling *and*
pronunciation difference, four syllables vs. three); British
**maths** vs. American **math**; British **specialisation** vs.
American **specialization** (the `-ise/-ize` pattern from Level V,
Module 2, now applied to technical register). More consequentially for
proposals: British **grant funding body** language uses **bid** and
**tender** where American usage often prefers **proposal** and
**solicitation** — and a British **tender** is a formal competitive
procurement process, not merely an offer.

**Key vocabulary previewed:** innovation vocabulary (proof of
concept, prototype, iteration, adoption curve, technical debt,
failure mode, feasibility), proposal vocabulary (deliverable,
milestone, work package, principal investigator, dissemination).
Intercultural note: norms around how confidently an innovation should
be pitched vary sharply — some funding and investment cultures reward
bold projection, others penalise it as unserious; this module teaches
calibrated confidence as the standard that travels best across both,
while naming that the calibration point itself differs by context.

## Lesson 6.1 — "Were This to Succeed — Speculative Register & Technical-to-General Translation"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1) use
`were + to`-infinitive and inverted conditional forms (`Were this to
succeed...`, `Had the approach been available...`) accurately in
formal speculative register; (2) state what would have to be true for
a proposition to hold, converting vague claims into testable ones; (3)
translate technical material for a non-specialist audience without
distorting it; (4) recognise the two failure modes of technical
translation — distortion and condescension.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Level V, Module 1 (mixed conditionals) and
Level VI, Module 5 (conditional precision) — this lesson adds the
*inverted*, high-formal conditional and applies it to speculation
about the future.

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor writes "If this were to succeed, it
would change the field" and "Were this to succeed, it would change the
field" and asks what differs — establishing that the second is the
same meaning at a markedly higher register, achieved by inversion and
the deletion of `if`.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model inverted conditionals: `Were this
to succeed, ...` (present/future hypothetical); `Had the approach
been available five years ago, ...` (past counterfactual); `Should
the trial confirm these results, ...` (open future condition, formal).
Highlight explicitly: the inversion replaces `if`, and `if` must then
be omitted — `*If were this to succeed` is an error. Then model the
**what would have to be true** technique: instead of "this technology
could transform logistics" (unfalsifiable optimism), write "for this
to transform logistics, three things would have to be true: unit cost
would have to fall below X, regulatory approval would have to extend
to Y, and existing infrastructure would have to accommodate Z" — the
same claim, now testable. Finally, model technical translation and
name its two failure modes: **distortion** (simplifying until the
claim is no longer true) and **condescension** (signalling to the
audience that they are being talked down to). The test of good
translation is that a specialist would accept it as accurate *and* a
non-specialist would find it useful.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners convert 6 `if`-conditionals
into inverted form, then rewrite 4 vague innovation claims as
"what would have to be true" propositions.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Learners take a technical concept
they genuinely understand (from any field) and write two versions —
one for a specialist, one for an intelligent non-specialist — then
exchange with a partner, who states what they now understand and
whether anything felt condescending.

**Speaking activity.** The partner translation exchange above, with
the writer confirming whether the partner's understanding is accurate
or whether the simplification distorted something.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "Every simplification loses
something. How do you decide which losses are acceptable and which
make the translation dishonest?" — a genuine question in science
communication, with no clean rule, appropriate to C2 judgement.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a technical explanation
delivered to a general audience and identify one point where
simplification was well-judged and one where it may have distorted.

**Reading activity — extended reading & independent judgement (8
min).** Read a short innovation-claim text (180-220 words). Answer 2
literal questions and 2 independent-judgement questions ("Rewrite the
central claim as a 'what would have to be true' proposition. On
current evidence, which of those conditions is least likely to
hold?").

**Writing task (5 min).** Write three inverted-conditional sentences
about a technology or approach you know, and one "what would have to
be true" proposition.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill the distinct intonation
contour of an inverted conditional read aloud — the fronted `Were` /
`Had` / `Should` carries a lifted, sustained delivery, with a clear
juncture before the main clause, which is what signals to a listener
that a conditional (not a question) has begun.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** An innovation-vocabulary matching game
(proof of concept, prototype, iteration, adoption curve, technical
debt, failure mode, feasibility) with precise definitions.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that `if` is correctly
omitted in inverted conditionals, and that "what would have to be
true" propositions are genuinely testable rather than restated
optimism.

**Homework.** Learners select a real or invented project they could
plausibly seek funding for, and note the problem it addresses, the
approach, one deliverable, and one honest risk — ready for Lesson
6.2's proposal and keynote work.

**Revision.** Lesson 6.2 opens with learners stating their project's
problem in one sentence.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners write the same technical
explanation for a *third* audience — a funder who is neither
specialist nor lay, but who must judge whether to invest.

## Lesson 6.2 — "From the Stage — The Keynote Address & the Grant Proposal"

**Learning objectives.** By the end of this lesson you can: (1)
structure a keynote address around a single controlling idea rather
than a survey of information; (2) carry a general audience through
unfamiliar material using an anchoring analogy; (3) write a grant or
project proposal with a stated problem, approach, deliverable,
timeline, and an honestly disclosed risk; (4) close a keynote on a
resolution the audience can carry away.

**Prerequisite knowledge.** Lesson 6.1 (speculative register,
technical translation); Level V, Module 7 (the research-informed
presentation) — this lesson shifts from *informing an informed
audience* to *moving a general one*.

**Warm-up (5 min).** Instructor delivers 30 seconds of a talk
organised as a list of facts, then 30 seconds of the same material
organised around one controlling idea, and asks which the class could
still summarise a week later.

**Presentation (10 min).** Model the keynote's architecture: **one
controlling idea** (a keynote that tries to convey five ideas conveys
none — the single most common failure); **an opening that earns
attention** without gimmick; **an anchoring analogy** that a general
audience can hold onto while unfamiliar material is introduced;
**two or three movements** that each return to and deepen the
controlling idea; and **a resolution** — not a summary, but a
statement of what the idea *asks of the audience*. Then model the
grant/project proposal structure: **problem** (specific, and
demonstrably unsolved), **approach** (what you will actually do, in
enough detail to be assessed), **deliverables and milestones**
(dated, verifiable), **risk** (honestly disclosed, with mitigation)
— and name why disclosed risk *strengthens* a proposal: assessors
discount proposals that claim no risk, because they conclude the
applicant has not thought hard enough.

**Guided practice (10 min).** Learners are given 4 keynote outlines
and identify which have a genuine controlling idea and which are
surveys; then draft an anchoring analogy for one unfamiliar concept.

**Independent practice (10 min).** Using their Lesson 6.1 homework
project, learners draft their proposal's problem and approach
sections, and outline a keynote built on one controlling idea derived
from the project.

**Speaking activity — keynote address.** Learners deliver a 3-4
minute keynote extract to the group — establishing the controlling
idea, deploying the anchoring analogy, and closing on a resolution.
The audience is asked afterwards to state the controlling idea in one
sentence; if they cannot, the keynote has not yet worked. This
lesson's core task and the direct basis for Module 6's assignment.

**Critical thinking / discussion prompt.** "A keynote is designed to
move an audience, and a grant proposal is designed to persuade
assessors. Both are advocacy. What distinguishes legitimate advocacy
for an idea from overselling it — and does disclosing risk really
help, or is that just a convention?" — a genuine professional
question this module takes a position on and invites learners to test.

**Listening activity (5 min).** Listen to a keynote extract, state its
controlling idea in one sentence, and identify the anchoring analogy.

**Reading activity (5 min).** Read a short proposal extract and assess
whether its stated risk is genuine or token — the distinction
assessors are trained to make.

**Writing task (5 min).** Write your proposal's risk section: one
genuine risk, why it is genuine, and what you would do if it
materialised.

**Pronunciation practice (5 min).** Drill keynote delivery — a wider
pitch range and more deliberate pausing than any register practised so
far, with the controlling idea receiving its own isolated, unhurried
delivery each time it recurs.

**Vocabulary reinforcement.** A proposal-vocabulary matching game
(deliverable, milestone, work package, principal investigator,
dissemination) plus the BrE/AmE `bid`/`tender` vs.
`proposal`/`solicitation` distinction.

**Formative assessment.** Instructor checks that the audience can
independently state the keynote's controlling idea, and that the
proposal's disclosed risk is genuine rather than token.

**Homework.** Learners complete their grant proposal for Module 6's
assignment.

**Revision.** This lesson opens with the Lesson 6.1 project-problem
recap. Module 6's Quiz and Assignment draw on both lessons.

**Extension activity.** Stronger learners write the single sentence a
sceptical assessor would use to reject their proposal, then revise the
proposal to answer it.

## Module 6 Quiz (auto-graded, 10 questions)

*Seeded verbatim into `quiz_questions` — see
`sql/seed-curriculum-level-6.sql`. Answer key marked with ✓.*

1. "___ this to succeed, it would change the field." (inverted
   conditional) — (a) If were (b) Were ✓ (c) Was (d) If it were to
   were
2. "___ the approach been available five years ago, the outcome would
   have differed." — (a) If had (b) Had ✓ (c) Have (d) Should have
3. In an inverted conditional, "if" must be: — (a) retained before the
   inversion (b) omitted ✓ (c) replaced with "whether" (d) moved to
   the end
4. Which converts a vague innovation claim into a testable one? — (a)
   "This technology could transform logistics." (b) "For this to
   transform logistics, unit cost would have to fall below X and
   regulatory approval would have to extend to Y." ✓ (c) "This
   technology is revolutionary." (d) "Experts are excited about this."
5. What are the two failure modes of technical-to-general
   translation? — (a) length and brevity (b) distortion and
   condescension ✓ (c) formality and informality (d) speed and
   hesitation
6. A keynote address should be organised around: — (a) as many ideas
   as time allows (b) a single controlling idea ✓ (c) a list of facts
   (d) the speaker's biography
7. Why does honestly disclosing risk generally strengthen a grant
   proposal? — (a) it shortens the proposal (b) assessors discount
   proposals claiming no risk, concluding the applicant has not
   thought hard enough ✓ (c) it is legally required everywhere (d) it
   removes the need for milestones
8. In British usage, a formal competitive procurement process is
   called a: — (a) solicitation (b) tender ✓ (c) requisition (d)
   docket
9. Which phrase means "of evidence, to confirm a hypothesis"? — (a)
   pan out (b) bear out ✓ (c) scale up (d) rule out
10. Which pair differ mainly in register, meaning roughly "work out
    successfully"? — (a) rule out / build on (b) bear out (neutral-
    formal, transitive) / pan out (informal, intransitive) ✓ (c) scale
    up / scale back (d) build on / bear out

## Module 6 Assignment — "A Grant Proposal & Keynote Address"

**Instructions given to the learner:** Complete two parts on one real
or invented project. **Part A (writing, this level's sixth genre): a
grant or project proposal**, 700-850 words, stating a **specific,
demonstrably unsolved problem**; an **approach** described in enough
detail to be assessed; at least two dated, verifiable **deliverables
or milestones**; and one **honestly disclosed risk** with its
mitigation. Include at least 2 inverted conditionals and one "what
would have to be true" proposition. **Part B (speaking): a keynote
address extract**, 3-4 minutes, built on a single controlling idea
derived from the project, using an anchoring analogy to carry a
general audience through the unfamiliar material, and closing on a
resolution rather than a summary.

**Grading rubric (for the instructor):**
- **Grammatical accuracy** — correctly formed inverted conditionals
  with `if` omitted; accurate speculative register throughout.
- **Vocabulary range** — at least 4 distinct innovation or proposal
  terms used precisely, plus one phrasal verb/collocation from this
  module at an appropriate register.
- **Task completion** — problem, approach, dated deliverables, and a
  genuine disclosed risk with mitigation in Part A; a single
  controlling idea, an anchoring analogy, and a resolution in Part B.
- **Independent judgement** — is the stated problem genuinely
  unsolved (not merely unaddressed by the applicant), and is the
  disclosed risk the one that would actually threaten the project,
  rather than a safe one?
- **Rhetorical effectiveness** — could a listener state the keynote's
  controlling idea in one sentence afterwards, and does the anchoring
  analogy genuinely aid understanding rather than decorate?
- **Discourse coherence & register** — is the technical material
  translated without distortion or condescension, and is speculative
  confidence calibrated — neither overclaiming nor so hedged as to say
  nothing?

A grade at or above the platform's pass threshold marks Module 6
completed for the learner.
