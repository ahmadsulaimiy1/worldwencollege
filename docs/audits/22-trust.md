# Audit 22 — Trust

**Method.** Every served page read against one question: *why should I
trust this institution?* Not "is this page accurate" — accuracy was
audited elsewhere. Whether the page gives a stranger a reason.

---

## The College's trust strategy, stated plainly

It is unusual enough to name before judging it.

Most institutions build trust by **display**: accreditation marks,
partner logos, rankings, testimonials, photographs of a campus, numbers
of alumni. Worldwide English College has none of these and could not
manufacture them without lying.

So it builds trust by **disclosure** instead: it publishes what it
cannot do, before being asked, in more detail than anyone asks for.

The strategy is coherent and it is the correct one for its position. The
question this audit asks is whether it *works*, and where it stops
working.

---

## Where disclosure works

**The awards page.** States that no award can be conferred because no
External Examiner is appointed. A page whose whole subject is the
qualification, leading with the fact that there is not one. Nothing else
on the site buys as much credibility as this single paragraph.

**The evidence record.** 45 items an external reviewer would ask for, 0
approved, empty rows visible with the reason for each emptiness. This is
the document institutions prepare *under duress* during a review.
Publishing it unprompted, incomplete, is a strong signal.

**The decisions register.** 30 in force, 2 outstanding, each naming its
authority and date — and the outstanding ones named rather than counted.

**The curriculum status callout.** The academics page states that the
level sizes are designed sizes, not published content, and that lessons
are still being written.

**The privacy page's self-correction.** It records that the page
previously said retention was undecided when the decision had been taken
eight days earlier, and that the software did not implement it for a
further eight days. It explains why it says so: *"a privacy page that
edits its own history is not one you should trust."*

**The tuition ledger's two cents.** Six levels at $3,166.67 come to
$19,000.02, not $19,000. The page publishes both totals and explains the
difference. Nobody would have noticed. That is the point.

---

## Where trust breaks

### Break 1 — the homepage was the least trustworthy page on the site

Every page behind it disclosed more than it did. It claimed an
accreditation candidacy that did not exist, retained counsel that did
not exist, and a programme "published in full" that is roughly a quarter
written.

Corrected. The structural point survives: **the trust strategy was
applied everywhere except the page most people read.** A reader who
started at the homepage and stopped there received the opposite
impression from a reader who went one click deeper — and the deeper
reader was getting the truth.

### Break 2 — disclosure has no floor

The College says what it cannot do. It does not say what it *will* do,
or by when.

No External Examiner — no date. No accreditation — no application. No
refund policy — no decision date. No start date. No registered address.
No academic calendar.

Candour without commitment eventually reads as an alibi. A reader can
admire the honesty on the first visit and, on the second visit six
months later, notice that the same list is the same length. **The
absence of a single date anywhere in the outstanding list is the largest
untreated trust risk on the site.**

### Break 3 — the institution has no body

Trust in an institution is partly trust in people, and there is almost
nobody visible.

Twenty faculty are named with qualifications and expertise, attested by
the College, with the provenance of each field recorded honestly — this
is properly done. But there are **no photographs**, no biographies
beyond a line, no engagement dates in the record, and no named
individual accountable for anything: no principal, no academic director,
no data protection officer, no press contact. The privacy page says the
College "cannot yet name a person accountable for how personal data is
handled".

A reader cannot answer "who *is* this?" and that is a different question
from "what is this?".

### Break 4 — no way to reach a person

One email address. No telephone. No postal address. A contact form that
opens the reader's own mail client, which the page admits.

Disclosure about the absence of a phone line does not substitute for a
phone line.

### Break 5 — no contract

No terms and conditions on any page. No governing law, no jurisdiction,
no statement of what happens if the College ceases to operate, no
complaints route that is not an academic appeal — and the appeals
procedure itself is recorded as not adopted.

The College asks a stranger for $19,000 with nothing that a lawyer would
recognise as an agreement. **Every disclosure on the site is a statement
of intent; a contract is the only thing on it that would be a promise.**

### Break 6 — the empty machinery

Attendance, student voice, intervention triggers, quality review cycles,
graduation audits, misconduct records, appeals — all built, all empty,
several with their thresholds explicitly unset.

For a careful reader this cuts both ways. It is evidence of seriousness,
and it is evidence that nothing has ever run. At some point the ratio of
apparatus to activity becomes its own signal, and a reader starts to
wonder whether the institution is preparing or performing preparation.

## The trust ledger

|---|---|
| Publishes what it cannot do, unprompted | **Very high** |
| Publishes its own corrections in place | **Very high** |
| Evidence register with empty rows visible | High |
| Curriculum published before payment | High |
| Rubrics published before assessment | High |
| Arithmetic explained rather than rounded | Moderate, disproportionate to size |

| Liability | Weight |
|---|---|
| No legal entity, no contract | **Very high** |
| No dates against anything outstanding | **High and worsening** |

| No phone, no address | Moderate |
| Homepage overclaimed (corrected) | Moderate, was severe |
| Apparatus far ahead of activity | Moderate, rising |

---

## Verdict

**The trust strategy is right and it has reached its ceiling.**

Disclosure has bought this institution as much credibility as disclosure
can buy. What it cannot buy is the next increment, because the remaining
questions are not "what are you hiding" — the College has answered that
better than anyone in its sector. They are **"are you a real
organisation"** and **"when"**.

Those two are answered by a company registration, a contract, and a date
against each outstanding item. None of the three is expensive. All three
are administrative. And all three would do more for trust than any
further governance system the College could build.
