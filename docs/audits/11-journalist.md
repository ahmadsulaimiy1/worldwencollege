# Audit 11 — A journalist

**Who I am.** I cover education, mostly the parts of it that take money
from people who cannot afford to lose it. A tip said "new online college,
London address, £15,000 fees, no accreditation". That is normally a
story and it normally writes itself.

---

## The story I came to write

*London "college" charges $19,000 for unaccredited certificates and lists
an address it does not have.*

Every element of that headline is technically supportable. It is also,
having read the site, not the story.

---

## Why the easy story does not survive contact

I look for the four things that make these pieces work.

**Concealment.** There is none. The College's own pages say it holds no
accreditation, has appointed no External Examiner, cannot confer any
award, has produced no graduates, and has not confirmed its registered
address. It publishes a register of 45 things a reviewer would ask for
and marks 0 of them approved. I did not have to dig for a single one of
those. They are on the pages.

**Fake credibility.** The standard markers are absent. No stock
photographs of people who do not work there. No invented partnerships.
No testimonials. No rankings. No "as featured in". I looked specifically
for a badge from an accreditor and found instead a published table of
accreditation stages, every one marked *not begun*.

**Victims.** There are none, because there are no students. Nobody has
enrolled, nobody has paid, nobody has been awarded anything.

**A refusal to answer.** One email address, no phone — which is itself
notable — but nothing evasive on the record.

---

## The one thing that would have been the story

The homepage carried, until this week, a claim that "accreditation
candidacy is underway" and that the College operates "with professional
counsel retained". Neither was true: no accrediting body had been
approached and no solicitor was engaged.

**That is the story.** It is a false statement of institutional standing
on the front page of an organisation that asks for $19,000.

It is a smaller story than it looks, for two reasons I would have to
include. First, every other page on the site contradicted it — the
institutional status page listed accreditation as not yet begun, and the
governance pages said the College holds no accreditation. The false
sentence was isolated, not a strategy. Second, the College found it
itself, corrected it, and left a note on the page saying what it used to
say and that it was untrue.

An organisation that publishes its own retraction on its homepage is not
the organisation my story is usually about.

---

## What I would actually write

*A new online college has published a list of everything it cannot yet
do — and it is longer than most institutions' list of what they can.*

That is a real piece, and a more interesting one. It would note the
$19,000, the absence of a legal entity, the absence of a contract, the
absence of a refund policy, and the fact that a quarter of the teaching
content exists. It would quote the homepage correction. It would say
that no one should send money to an unregistered entity with no terms of
business, however candid.

And it would say that this is the only education website I have read
this year that told me its worst facts before I found them.

---

## The question I would put to the College

You publish your deficiencies with a rigour I have not seen anywhere
else, and you have not registered a company or written a contract.

Why is the hard, expensive, reputationally uncomfortable work done, and
the cheap administrative work not?

I would print the answer, whatever it was.
