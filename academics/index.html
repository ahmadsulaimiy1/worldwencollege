Academics · The International English Fluency Course

One programme, taught to a written standard.

I The IEFC

Six levels, in the order they are climbed, each hung on the CEFR band it is designed to reach.

Six levels, mapped to the CEFR.

Each level is designed to build toward its corresponding Common European Framework of Reference band — the benchmark most widely recognised by universities, employers and English-language institutions worldwide. Each is four months of study: ten modules, 120 designed lessons, a Total Qualification Time of 200 hours. You do not need to start at Level I — a placement assessment establishes where you belong.

II Workload & Credit

What a level costs in hours, and what the College does and does not claim those hours mean.

Two hundred hours a level, divided where it matters.

Each level carries 20 WEC Credits and a Total Qualification Time of 200 hours across four months — about twelve hours a week. The division below is the one that decides whether an online programme is workable: what depends on other people, and what does not.

80 Guided Learning Hours

Taught, marked and scheduled — lessons worked through with the material, assignments marked by a person, and the live conversation classes and tutorials.

120 Independent Learning Hours

Yours to place. Reading, listening, recording, self-checking and rehearsal — the part of the programme that answers to nobody's timetable.

Academic workload and curriculum status

How the programme is measured. Each level carries 20 WEC Credits and a Total Qualification Time of 200 hours — 80 Guided Learning Hours and 120 Independent Learning Hours — across four months, or about twelve hours a week. The full programme is 120 WEC Credits and 1,200 hours. A WEC Credit is the College’s own internal measure (one credit represents ten notional learning hours); it is not ECTS or CATS and carries no transfer entitlement to any institution.

These hours are a design figure, not a measurement. Where hours measured from real time-on-task differ from the design figure, the difference is published rather than reconciled quietly. Total Qualification Time counts guided study plus the independent study expected within the programme; it does not count a learner’s own wider exposure to English. It is not a claim that CEFR C2 can be reached in 1,200 hours from no English.

The lesson counts above are the designed size of each level, not the amount of content published in the learning platform today. All sixty modules across the six levels are authored and live, each with its own examination and its own assignment; the lessons inside them are still being written and are released continuously.

III The Credit Framework

What a WEC Credit is, what it is worth, and what it is not.

One credit is ten hours of designed work.

A credit that is not defined is decoration. The WEC Credit is defined here, arithmetically, so that every figure derived from it — the 20 a level carries, the 120 the programme carries, the cost per credit on the tuition page — can be checked rather than taken.

The definition

1 credit = 10 notional hours

Two credits per module, ten modules a level, 200 hours a level. The arithmetic closes in every direction, and it is stated so a reader can close it themselves rather than trusting that somebody did.

What it is not

Not ECTS, not CATS, not transferable

The WEC Credit is an internal measure of academic weight and carries no transfer entitlement to any other institution. No university is obliged to accept it, none has agreed to, and the College has asked none. Calling it a credit without saying that would be the most convenient omission on this page.

Notional, and why

Hours designed, not hours measured

Total Qualification Time is the workload the curriculum was built to. Some learners will finish a level in far fewer hours and some in far more, and neither is a failure of the figure — that is what notional means, and it is how every credit framework in the world works.

Published in full in the Programme Architecture, free to download, with the mapping from every lesson to its outcome and its assessment.

IV Graduation Requirements

What has to be true before an award is conferred. All of it, and nothing discretionary.

Four conditions, and no fifth one nobody mentioned.

Every award the College confers requires all four of these and nothing beyond them. There is no attendance requirement, no participation mark, no final discretion and no condition that appears at the end.

One

All ten modules completed

Every module quiz taken and every assignment submitted and marked. Completion means the work exists and was assessed, not that time passed.

Two

70% overall on the level examination

The summative standard, against criteria published to you before you sat it. Assessments can be resat, at no charge, as many times as it takes.

Three

No single skill below 50%

The floor that stops a strong reader passing as a speaker. A single aggregate would let one skill carry three, and the four are recorded apart precisely so that cannot happen.

Four

The spoken paper recorded and passed

Speaking is assessed by recording you speaking, marked by a person against a named pronunciation target. A programme that marks speaking from written work is not marking speaking.

The complete programme

All six level awards, 120 WEC Credits, and the C2 spoken paper. There is no separate final examination and no thesis: the sixth level award is the completion of the programme, and a holder of all six is recorded as having completed the IEFC in full. Every award to date was moderated inside the College — see Awards and honours, which says what that means before you rely on it.

V The Academic Year

There is no term, no semester and no intake date. That is a decision, and here is the reasoning.

The calendar is yours, and that is the point.

Most institutions publish an academic calendar because their teaching is synchronous and their rooms are finite. Neither is true here, and adopting a calendar the College does not need would exclude exactly the learners it was built for.

Admission

Continuous, all year

You begin on the day your enrolment clears. There is no term to wait for, no cohort to miss and no month in which the door is shut — including Ramadan, and including whatever month your employer will release you in.

Pace

Four months by design, not by rule

A level is built to 200 hours, which is about twelve hours a week over four months. Take six months or take two: there is no deadline you can miss and no penalty for a slower route, because a learner fitting study around a night shift is the normal case here rather than the exception.

Assessment

Sat when you are ready

Module quizzes and assignments are taken as you reach them. The level examination is sat when the ten modules are complete — not in a window somebody else chose, and not against a room full of other people’s readiness.

The one thing a calendar would fix

Live conversation classes need other people present, and without a fixed rhythm they are hard to timetable. The Executive has considered adopting a session rhythm and has not; recordings are the standing fallback and are not treated as a lesser option. It is recorded as an outstanding decision in the governance register rather than left for a student to discover, and the day it is adopted it will be published here.

VI Assessment

Criteria published before the work, and four skills that are never averaged into one.

Progress that is measured, not assumed.

Module quizzes and assignments track progress within each level; a level assessment confirms readiness to advance. Rubrics and pass criteria are published to the learner before assessment, and the four language skills are marked and recorded apart.

  • Digital transcript updated after every level
  • An award defined for every level — conferred at Level I and Level II, moderated inside the College, and the College says so
  • IELTS, TOEFL and Cambridge English preparation embedded from Upper Intermediate onward
  • Progress visible to the learner at every stage
Structure

A mapped curriculum

Every lesson states its objectives, its prerequisites and the timing of each stage. Instructors teach to a shared standard rather than a personal syllabus.

Assessment

Criteria published first

Rubrics and pass criteria are published to the learner before the assessment, not explained afterwards.

Skills

Four skills tracked separately

Listening, reading, speaking and writing are assessed and recorded independently, because a single grade hides where the real gap is.

VIII How Learning Works

What a week holds, and the failure mode of self-paced study the College designs against.

What a week actually looks like.

Most of the programme is studied when you can study it. A smaller part is live and depends on other people. The separation is what makes an online programme workable or not.

The bulk

Working through lessons

Each lesson is staged, with the stages timed, and can be paused and resumed. This is where most of the hours go and none of it is scheduled.

Daily, briefly

Listening and recording

Short and frequent beats long and occasional for both listening and pronunciation. The Lab is built for ten minutes a day rather than an hour a week.

Weekly

An assignment

One produced thing per module — written, spoken or done — marked by a person against a rubric you saw before you started.

Before assessment

Self-checking

Not marked and not recorded against you. Self-checks exist so you can find out what you do not know at no cost.

Live

Conversation and tutorials

The part that depends on other people being present. Recorded for anyone who cannot attend, and recordings are not treated as the lesser option.

At the end

The level assessment

The summative point, against criteria published from the start of the level.

Self-paced study has a known failure mode

Wholly self-paced language study finishes badly, and the reason is well understood: no fixed points, no peers at the same stage, nothing to be late for. WEC-LC currently runs self-paced, because that is what is built. A recommendation to add a fixed rhythm of live sessions, examination windows and orientation sits with the Executive for decision — see Dates.

Until it decides, the structure has to come from you: a fixed hour daily finishes where a target number of weekly hours does not. Engagement is tracked so that someone who has gone quiet is reached in month two rather than discovered in month eleven, and it never produces a penalty — see Support.

Live sessions

Self-paced, with no fixed timetable

Conversation classes and tutorials are designed, and no live timetable runs today. Every hour of the programme is therefore studied when you choose to study it, which is the arrangement to plan around.

Listening audio

Published as full scripts

All 120 listening sets are authored in full — scripts, marked features and teaching notes — and published as scripts in the Library. The recorded audio requires voices and a studio.

Marking

Marked by hand, then in the platform

Three cohorts were taught, marked and conferred before the instructor workspace existed, so the marking on record was done outside it. The workspace applies the same published rubrics.

IX Teaching Practice

The method the modules are written to, set out in full on a page of its own.

How the College teaches, written down before it teaches.

A curriculum states what is taught. A teaching practice states how, and binds every instructor to the same answer. Both exist here in writing, and the second is the reason the first can be taught by more than one person without becoming two programmes.

The method

One stated approach

What a lesson is for, how it is staged, and what an instructor is expected to do at each stage — written once and taught from.

Design

How a module is authored

The order in which a module is built — outcomes, then assessment, then lessons — so nothing is taught that is not assessed and nothing assessed that is not taught.

Development

How instructors are kept current

Observation, calibration of marking, and the record of both — the mechanism by which a written standard stays a real one.

X The Digital Campus

Eight things a person signs into. Where something is built but unused, this leaf says unused rather than available.

Eight things a person signs into.

Every one of these is built, and every one is named for what it does today — a thing described here as in use has been used.

Portal

Where you start

Your enrolment, your level, and what you were doing last. Designed to answer “what now?” in one screen rather than to present a dashboard.

My Programme

The route through the level

Modules, lessons, exercises and assessments in order, with what is complete and what is next. Progression is per learner, so this is genuinely your own path.

The Listening Lab

Recording and pronunciation

Listening sets, pronunciation targets, your own recordings and the feedback on them — see the Listening Lab.

My Record

What is held about you

Attempts, marks by skill, feedback and recordings, plus the controls for sharing any of it. The sharing decisions are yours, not the College’s.

Instructor workspace

Marking and feedback

Where submissions are marked against their rubrics and pronunciation feedback is written against its target. Built and tested against the same criteria the marking on record was applied to; the marking done so far predates it.

Verification

Open to anyone

A credential check requiring no account and no relationship with the College — see Verification. It is built and open; the awards already conferred were made before it existed, and their records are still being entered against it.

Offline recording

The Lab does not require a live connection

A recording made offline is held and uploaded in parts when the connection returns, so a drop does not lose the file. Built because the College expects learners in places where connections drop.

Drafts stay local

A half-written note is not sent anywhere

Working notes are kept on your own device until you submit. Only submission needs the network, and the interface says which is which rather than leaving you guessing.

What you need to run it: a computer, tablet or phone with a current browser and a connection that can stream audio; the Listening Lab asks you to record yourself, and a phone or laptop microphone is enough. The College tests against current browsers rather than a fixed list of versions, so keeping yours current is the real requirement — a version matrix would promise something narrower and less useful.

When it does not work: three common cases, answered.

  1. 01The recorder will not start. The browser has to be given permission to use the microphone, and that permission is per site and easily denied by accident. Check the browser’s site permissions before assuming anything is broken.
  2. 02The connection dropped mid-recording. Nothing is lost. Recording does not need the network; the file is held and uploaded in parts when the connection returns.
  3. 03An application or payment form did not go through. The application form falls back to your own email application with your details filled in, so the application still reaches Admissions. For a payment, do not retry repeatedly — write to the College with the time and the amount, and it will be checked against the record.

Anything else: write to info@worldwencollege.co.uk saying what you were trying to do, what happened, and on what device — those three facts resolve most of it in one message rather than four. For being stuck on the material rather than the machinery, Support says who answers.

XI Academic Status

What the qualification carries, and what it does not. Two marks, never the same one.

The standing of the qualification, in two columns.

The left column is what a WEC award carries. The right column is what it does not — named, because the limits of a credential are part of what it is, and a candidate should read them here rather than discover them later.

What it carries

6
  • A published curriculum — sixty modules across six levels, readable in full before you enrol
  • An examination and an assignment for every module, written before the lessons they test
  • Rubrics and pass criteria, in the candidate’s hands in advance of the assessment
  • A defined award at each of the six levels, with its own criteria for conferral
  • A teaching practice binding on every instructor, published rather than internal
  • A campus of record — enrolment, submission, marking and free verification in one system

What it does not

5
  • Accreditation — the award is the College’s own and carries none from outside it
  • An External Examiner — no external examiner is appointed to the programme
  • External moderation — every award conferred so far was moderated inside the College
  • Live classes and tutorials — the programme is taught asynchronously, and no live timetable runs
  • Recorded listening audio — the 120 listening sets are published as full scripts

Nothing in this column wears a tick, and an item moves across only once it is true. The College’s full institutional position is at About · Institutional Status.

The International English Fluency Course is the College’s only programme. It is published in full — curriculum, assessment, rubrics and awards — before it has been taught to a cohort, so that anyone considering it can judge it on what it is rather than on what it says about itself.
