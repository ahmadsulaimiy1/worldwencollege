Academics · The Tutor Handbook

What a tutor is held to, and what holds a tutor up.

A teaching standard says what good teaching is. It does not say what to do on the Tuesday when a batch of scripts is due and a learner has stopped submitting. This is the operating half, and every commitment in it carries a working-day count.

The standard a tutor teaches to is published at Teaching Practice, and the standard a tutor marks to at How you are assessed. This handbook turns both into the working week: the post, the hour, the clock on a mark, and the routes a concern takes in either direction.

Marked work
Back to the learner in 10 working days
A learner who slips
Reached within 3 working days
Every judged mark
Second-marked before release
A tutor’s concern
Answered within 20 working days

“A deadline that binds one side is not a deadline.”

I The Appointment

What a post is offered against, and the file that is complete before a first session.

A tutor is appointed against a written specification.

Every teaching post carries a specification naming the levels it covers, the skills it marks and the hours it runs to. It travels with the offer, so the work is settled before it is accepted.

Qualification

A teaching qualification, or the degree behind one

A recognised English-teaching certification — CELTA, DELTA, TESOL or equivalent — or a degree in English, Linguistics or Education. The bar is at The Faculty.

Language

English at C2, evidenced on file

Command of English at the Common European Framework’s Mastery band, shown by a certificate, a degree taught in English, or an assessment the College sets. The evidence is held on file, never inferred from an interview.

Standing

Identity, and the right to work where you teach from

Identity, and the right to work in the tutor’s own jurisdiction, verified before the first session and re-checked as a permission nears expiry. The College teaches online from many countries, and the check follows each tutor’s law.

References

Two references, taken up in writing

Two professional references, one from a teaching post, taken up in writing and read before an offer is confirmed. A reference nobody read is a formality; two asked properly beat four asked nominally.

What the College hands over before a first session

  • The specification for the post, naming its levels, skills and hours
  • The current rubric for every assessment the tutor marks
  • Every lesson plan the tutor teaches from, with its prerequisites and stage minutes
  • A named academic contact, from the day the offer is accepted
  • This handbook, issued with the offer rather than after it
II The Session

One prepared hour, in five stages, with the tutor talking for under a third of it.

The house shape of a live hour.

A lesson is planned before it is taught, and the plan is the tutor’s to run rather than to redesign. These five stages are the shape every live session keeps, and the minutes are the plan’s own.

StageMinutesWhat it is for
Recall6The named prerequisite, checked aloud. A prerequisite assumed is an hour taught to the wrong class.
Presentation12The new point, in English, once. The plan already holds a second explanation for whoever the first route misses.
Controlled practice12Accuracy under correction, before an error is rehearsed into place.
Speaking22Every learner speaks. The tutor’s share stays under a third of the stage.
Consolidation8The learner demonstrates the outcome, and the next prerequisite is named.
Preparation, and the twenty-minute rule

The plan is read before the session, never during it. A tutor’s own talking time across the hour stays within twenty of the sixty minutes: a learner who did not speak in a speaking lesson did not have one. The anatomy behind each plan is at Lesson design.

When the hour does not go as planned

A stage collapses, a point lands in half its minutes, a class arrives small. The tutor runs the hour and writes what happened into the session note that day — worth more to the next tutor than a silently corrected plan.

III Marking

The College promises a learner ten working days. This is the clock that keeps it.

Marked work, and the clock behind it.

The College returns marked work within ten working days of submission, and a capstone within fifteen. Those are the learner’s dates. The dates below are the tutor’s, set so the learner’s are kept without a rush at the end.

The workThe tutor’s clockThe second markerTo the learner
Module assignment7 working days2 working daysWorking day 10
Recorded speech3 working days1 working dayWorking day 5
Level examination11 working days3 working daysWorking day 15
Capstone11 working days3 working daysWorking day 15
Module quizMachine-marked, returned at onceRead on challengeSame day

Recorded speech runs short on purpose: feedback on pronunciation is worth most while the attempt is still remembered. Every clock starts at the submission deadline rather than when a script is opened.

By day three

Say so before the date, never after it

A tutor who can see a batch running late tells the Registrar by the end of the third working day, while there is room to act. A date reported after it passes has already cost the learner what it was for.

Within one day

The Registrar reallocates, or sets a new date

On notice the Registrar moves the batch to another marker or fixes a fresh date, and writes to every affected learner within one working day naming it. A learner hears of a change from the College, before it lands.

Once only

A second extension is a resourcing matter

A batch is extended once by this route. A second request goes to the Assessment Integrity Committee, which examines the allocation rather than the marker: a tutor holding more scripts than the clock allows is a scheduling fault, and calling it anything else hides it.

IV Second Marking

What a rubric settles, what a second reader settles, and the tolerance between them.

Two readers, one published standard.

Every judged piece is marked against criteria published before the task was set, and read by a second marker before the mark is released. The two readers do different jobs, and confusing them is the commonest error a careful tutor makes.

The rubric

Mark the criterion, not the impression

Each criterion is scored on its own descriptor, and the score written against the words that earned it. A mark reaches the learner attached to its rubric, so they see which criterion cost them what.

The comment

Three things, in this order

What the learner did well, the single change that would raise the mark most, and how to make it. Feedback naming five faults and no priority is a list; naming one is teaching, and is acted on that week.

The tolerance

Three points, and two absolute cases

Where the two marks fall within three percentage points, the first stands. Beyond that the markers reconcile in writing. A disagreement crossing an honours threshold or a skill floor is reconciled whatever its size: a point either side of a floor is no small thing to the learner.

The third reader

Two working days, then a third marker

A reconciliation that settles within two working days is recorded and released. One that does not goes to a third marker, whose mark stands. Both original marks stay on the record, so the committee reads how the standard moved.

The thresholds a mark has to clear

Pass, Merit, Distinction and High Distinction each carry an overall mark and a floor under every skill, published at the rules that produce a mark. A tutor marks to the rubric. The scale is applied afterwards, never in the marker’s head while the script is open.

V Moderation

What is sampled, who reads it, and the one thing moderation may not do.

Moderation reads the standard, not the student.

A second marker asks whether this mark is right. Moderation asks whether this marker marks the way every other marker marks. The sample is fixed before marking starts, and never drawn at random alone.

What every moderation sample contains

  • Every script in a level examination cohort
  • One in ten module assignments per module, and never fewer than five
  • Every fail, every High Distinction, and every mark within two points of a threshold
  • The first batch marked by any tutor new to that level
  • The written reconciliation from every case a second marker escalated
Why the sample is weighted rather than random

A random hundred scripts are mostly ordinary scripts. Consequence sits at the edges: the fail that stops a level, the mark two points under a threshold, the highest honour in the cohort. Weighting puts the reading where the cost is.

What moderation may do, and what it may not

It may confirm a marking standard, return a batch for re-marking, or refer a rubric to the Curriculum Standards Committee where the criterion rather than the marking is at fault. It may not move one learner’s mark on its own: a single mark lifted inside an untouched batch creates an inconsistency instead of removing one.

VI Escalation

Three situations a tutor carries to somebody else, and the day each one moves.

Three routes out, each with a day on it.

A tutor is responsible for noticing and for reporting. Investigating, deciding and resolving belong to other offices, and the College asks a tutor for none of them. It asks for speed, the part a tutor holds.

Within 3 working days

A learner who is slipping

Two missed submissions in a row, or a piece well below a learner’s running standard, and the tutor offers a tutorial within three working days and records the offer. The regulations trigger that tutorial at two failed summative assessments; this threshold sits earlier, so the conversation arrives first. Only the Registrar changes a learner’s standing.

Within 2 working days

Work that may not be the learner’s own

The tutor holds the mark, marks nothing, puts nothing to the learner, and sends the work and the reason to the Registrar within two working days. A learner may answer before any finding is made, and a tutor who raises it first has made one. See Academic integrity.

The same working day

A learner who may be at risk of harm

Where a tutor has reason to believe a learner is at risk of serious harm, it reaches the Registrar the same working day, in writing, carrying what was seen or said and nothing added. The tutor makes no enquiry and tells no one else. Delay is the part a tutor governs, so the rule fixes that and leaves the rest with the Registrar.

A learner registered as under 18

An application from someone under 18 is handled individually, with a parent or guardian in the correspondence from the first reply, as Admissions sets out. A tutor assigned such a learner routes every message through the Registrar.

VII Confidence

Who may hear what about a learner, and what leaves a tutor’s own machine.

A learner’s work belongs to the learner.

A tutor sees a learner’s marks, submissions, pace and sometimes circumstances. The rule is short: that material travels along the marking and support chain, and nowhere else.

WhoWhat they may be told
The learnerEverything the College holds about them, and their mark with its rubric.
The second marker and the moderating panelThe work and the rubric, for the purpose of marking it.
The RegistrarAnything escalated under Leaf VI, and anything the record must carry.
The Student Success OfficeWhat it needs to reach a learner who is struggling.
Anyone elseNothing at all, including another tutor outside the marking chain.

Four duties that follow

  • A mark reaches a learner through the College’s record, on the day it is released
  • Learner work stays inside the College’s systems, and a local copy is deleted five working days after release
  • A reference is written on the learner’s own written request, and a copy goes to them
  • What the College holds, and the right to correct it, is at Privacy and your data
VIII Standing

What the College undertakes toward a tutor, and the route for a concern about the College.

The obligations that run the other way.

Everything above is a duty on the tutor. This leaf is the College’s own side of the instrument, written with the same working-day counts, because an obligation carrying no date binds nobody.

Each term

An observation, written up within five days

Teaching is reviewed in observation cycles against eight declared areas, and the write-up reaches the tutor within five working days with its strengths, recommendations and action points. An observation is a development record and stays out of any matter of conduct. See The Quality Architecture.

Thirty hours a year

Development time, paid, with a floor on assessment

Every tutor holds thirty hours a year of paid development time, at least ten of them on assessment and marking. Thirty hours is about a working week — enough to finish something rather than attend something. The floor sits on assessment because that is where a tutor’s errors cost most.

Every term

Your own reliability, shown to you

Each term a tutor is shown the agreement between their own marks and their second markers’, with the cases that diverged. A marker told only that a standard drifted can do nothing; one shown where and by how much corrects it in the next batch.

3 days, then 20

Raising a concern about the College

A concern goes to the Registrar, acknowledged within three working days and answered within twenty, in writing and with reasons. One about academic management goes to the Governor for Academic Affairs, and one about fair treatment to the Governor for Ethics and Institutional Values. Concerns are accepted anonymously.

The protection that makes the last card mean something

A tutor’s engagement, allocation, observation record and pay are settled on the work and on nothing else. Where any of them changes within three months of a concern being raised, the Board of Governors reviews the change first. A right to complain that an institution can quietly price is a right on paper.

Where this handbook is amended

The Executive adopts amendments on the recommendation of the Teaching Excellence Committee, and each carries the date it took effect. A tutor is notified before an amendment binds them, and no working-day count is shortened inside a batch in progress.
