I The Stated Use

One line on the application form, and what it changes.

The answer that shapes the rest.

The application asks what the learner wants the English for. That answer is recorded against the enrolment and read by the tutor before the first tutorial. It sets the elective reading, the register the writing is marked in, and the brief of the capstone at every level.

Use one

University entry

The work is set in the academic register: reading a source and using it without copying its shape, citing, arguing against a position fairly, taking notes from a spoken lecture, and writing to time. The capstone brief becomes an argued essay with sources, marked against the published rubric.

Use two

Professional work

The language of a working week: the meeting where you have to interrupt politely, the brief that has to fit one page, the message that has to be right the first time, and the difficult conversation held in a second language. The capstone becomes a proposal delivered aloud and defended.

Use three

Government and public service

The official register: the minute, the note to file, the formal letter that commits an institution, and the discipline of turning a policy into English a citizen can read. Precision and restraint are marked here as strengths rather than as an absence of style.

Use four

An external examination

Technique under time: reading fast enough to answer, planning in four minutes, managing turn-taking in a spoken test, and finishing. The College teaches the skill an examination measures. It enters candidates for no other body’s examination and forecasts no score.

The limit, stated once and meant

The College teaches English and certifies what a learner can do with it. It places nobody in employment, sponsors no visa, enters candidates for no external examination, and speaks to no institution on a learner’s behalf. Where a graduate needs a document for any of those purposes, the Registry issues it to the graduate, who decides where it goes.

III Verification

What a check returns, what it withholds, and who decides.

An employer checks it without asking us.

An employer’s problem with a certificate is old and simple: a document arrives and there is nothing behind it. The College’s answer is that every award carries a code, and that the check runs in public without the College’s help.

The code

What a check returns

The award and its level, the holder’s name as the Register now holds it, the date of conferral, the register number, and the current standing — in force, replaced or withdrawn. A withdrawn award still answers, with its date, because a check that simply failed would teach a reader nothing.

The link

Who opens the rest of it

Marks and the transcript are reached through a share link the graduate issues, with the sections and the expiry the graduate chooses. Thirty days is the default and one hundred and eighty is the ceiling. It is withdrawn in one action, at which point the link stops resolving for everyone holding it.

What an employer can do today, without asking anyone

  • Enter a code at Verification and receive the answer from the register itself.
  • Follow the permanent link or scan the code printed on the credential, which reaches the same answer.
  • Read the rubric the work was marked against, which the College publishes before the work is set.
  • Read what each award means and what its post-nominal signifies, on Awards and honours.
  • Write to the Registry about a document’s authenticity, and have an answer within ten working days.
What a check returns to nobody

Marks, feedback, recordings, attendance, measured study time, contact details and whether a person is studying now. Each of those reaches a third party only through a share link the graduate issued. The College’s written answer to an employer is confined to what the code already confirms, unless the graduate has consented in writing to more. See your academic record for the field-by-field position.

IV Sponsorship

The account, the seat, and the day the invoice falls due.

How an organisation sponsors learners.

An employer, a school or a ministry buying places deals with the College under a corporate account. These are the terms, and they are the same terms for every organisation: the price is the published partner band on Tuition & Fees rather than an outcome of negotiation.

TermThe rule
Minimum sizeFive seats on one account. Below that, a third party pays for a named learner in the ordinary way.
InvoicingOne consolidated invoice per intake, in the currency recorded on the account.
Purchase orderRequired where the organisation records it as required; the invoice then carries the number.
PaymentThirty days from the date of the invoice.
Assigning a seatThe sponsor names the person. The seat stays unclaimed until that person accepts it.
Withdrawing a seatAn unclaimed seat is revoked at any time and is uninvoiced. A claimed seat runs to the end of the level begun.
PriceThe published partner band, applied by band and by nothing else.
RefundsThe refund window belongs to the learner and runs from that learner’s own start date.
Opening

Opening an account

Write to Admissions with the organisation’s legal name, its billing address and email, the billing currency, whether a purchase order is required, and the number of seats wanted. The Registry opens the account and records a named contact on each side, so neither party is corresponding with an inbox.

Seats

The three states of a seat

A seat is unclaimed while it waits for the person the sponsor named. It becomes claimed when that person accepts it and enrols. It is revoked when the sponsor releases it. Only a claimed seat is invoiced, and a claimed seat cannot be un-claimed by the sponsor mid-level.

V Sponsor & Learner

Two lists, drawn so that neither can be read into the other.

What is bought, and what is not for sale.

Sponsorship is a commercial arrangement about places. The academic relationship it pays for belongs to the learner, and the two lists below draw the boundary so that it needs no inference.

What a sponsor receives

  • One consolidated invoice in the account’s currency, carrying the purchase-order number where the account requires one.
  • A seat register showing which seats are unclaimed, which are claimed, and which names the sponsor assigned.
  • Confirmation that a named learner enrolled, and that a level was completed, where that learner consented in writing.
  • The published partner band, applied by the size of the order and by nothing about the buyer.
  • A named contact in the Registry, who answers a written question within ten working days.

What stays with the learner

  • Marks, feedback, submitted work, recordings and measured study time, which reach the sponsor through nothing.
  • The transcript and the academic record, issued to the learner and to whoever the learner directs in writing.
  • Consent to progress reporting, given separately from the sponsorship and withdrawable at any moment without penalty.
  • The place itself: a learner who leaves the organisation keeps the level begun and everything already recorded.
  • The award, which a sponsor may neither surrender, withdraw, transfer nor decline on the graduate’s behalf.
Admission is not a purchase

Every sponsored learner is admitted on their own application and their own placement assessment, on the same criteria as everyone else. A seat is a paid place in a cohort and it carries no entitlement to admission. Where an applicant is not admitted, the seat returns to the account unused and is never invoiced. The College would rather refund a sponsor than admit someone to satisfy an order.

VI Outcomes

The method, adopted and published before the first figure.

The method is published before the figures.

An outcomes claim without a method is an advertisement. So the method is adopted first, printed here in full, and printed again beside every figure the College reports. A reader who disagrees with a number can then argue with the thing that produced it.

Element of the methodThe rule adopted
PopulationEvery graduate of a given award in a given academic year, counted from the Register of Awards.
Census dateSix months after the last conferral of that academic year.
InstrumentOne written questionnaire, sent twice, in English and in Arabic, with the questions published.
Reporting floorA percentage is published only where responses reach half the population and at least twenty people.
Below the floorThe College publishes a count, describes it as a count, and prints the population beside it.
Self-reportAn answer the College has seen no evidence for is marked self-reported wherever it appears.
Missing answersReported as missing. Nothing is imputed, weighted or scaled up to the population.
EarningsSalary is outside the questionnaire and outside the report.
PublicationWith the annual quality review, in both editions, on the same day.
Why

Why the reporting floor is set high

A percentage drawn from a handful of replies is arithmetic wearing the clothes of evidence, and the people who answer a survey are rarely the people it most needed to reach. A floor of half the population, and at least twenty people, is the point at which a proportion starts to describe the group rather than the respondents.

Never

Four figures the College keeps out of print

A placement rate built on self-report and presented as measurement. A salary, at any percentile. The name of an organisation a graduate went to, which is the graduate’s to give. And a comparison with another institution, which the College is in no position to compute honestly.

What travels beside every figure

  • The population it was drawn from, named and counted.
  • The census date, so a reader knows how old the answer is.
  • The number of people who answered, printed beside the percentage rather than in a footnote.
  • Whether the answers behind it were verified by the College or self-reported by graduates.
