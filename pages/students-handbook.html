I Enrolment

What the College undertakes, and what it asks back.

What enrolment gives you, and what it asks back.

Enrolment is an exchange, and both halves of it are written down. The College’s half stands from the day your first instalment clears; yours follows it.

The College’s half of the compact

Your level, whole
Ten modules with their lessons, exercises and assessments, open on the first day. Nothing is released on a date.
A named instructor
One member of academic staff carries your level and answers for the marking on it.
Marking by a person
Produced work is read and marked against a rubric published before you attempt it.
A record you hold
Every attempt, every mark by skill, every recording — see Your academic record.
An answer
A reply from the College within three working days of your writing to it.
The rules as they stood
The regulations in force on the day you enrol, for as long as you study under them.

And what is asked of you.

  • That the work you submit is your own production. The whole of that rule sits on Academic integrity.
  • An address that reaches you, kept current. A decision sent to an address you stopped reading is still a decision delivered.
  • Time in the region of the design: twelve hours a week, for about four months, per level.
  • The rubric read before the work rather than after the mark. It is published in advance so that you can.
  • Your own voice recorded, and listened back to before you submit it.
  • A message when something goes wrong, early enough for the College to do something about it.
The rule that travels with you

A regulation adopted after your enrolment reaches you only where its effect is neutral or in your favour. Anything stricter binds learners who enrol after it and leaves you on the standard you accepted. That is what makes reading this page worth your time: the terms you read today are the terms you keep.

II The Shape Of A Level

Ten modules, four months, and where the twelve hours go.

Ten modules, four months, and the shape of one week.

A level is built to carry two hundred hours of work. That is a designed workload rather than a measured average, and across four months it comes to roughly twelve hours a week. The table sets out how the College expects those twelve to divide.

StrandHours a weekWhat it is
Lessons4.5Working the authored lessons in order — presentation, guided practice, the writing task at the foot of each one.
Independent practice3Producing language rather than rereading it: the exercises, the workbook, and anything you make for yourself.
The Listening Lab2Listening against a script, recording yourself against a named target, and listening back — see the Lab.
The assignment1.5The module’s produced piece, marked by a person against a rubric you read first.
Self-check and review1The unmarked self-check, and going back over whatever it found.
One week12Ten modules at roughly a fortnight each brings a level to about four months.

How one module runs, start to finish.

  1. 01Read the outcomes and the rubric. Both are published before the teaching. Ten minutes here tells you what the module is going to ask you to produce.
  2. 02Work the lessons in order. Each assumes the one before it. The order is the argument, not a filing convenience.
  3. 03Take the self-check. It is unmarked and unrecorded, and it exists so that you find a gap before an examiner does.
  4. 04Sit the module quiz. Low-stakes by design. Its job is to tell you what to go back to.
  5. 05Submit the assignment. This is where speaking and writing are actually judged. A person marks it and writes back.
What closes a module, and what closes a level

A module closes at the threshold the platform applies; a level closes when a member of staff confirms it, which is what opens the next one. Both rules, with the marks behind them, are on Academic regulations and How you are assessed.

III Independent Study

Six of the twelve hours are yours to organise.

The half of the programme that happens when nobody is watching.

Of the twelve hours a week, about six are work you set yourself. Learners underestimate this half more reliably than any other, and it is the half that decides how quickly a level goes.

Produce, do not review

Rereading a lesson feels like study and changes very little. Writing six new sentences with the structure it taught changes a great deal. Given a choice, take the one that makes you produce language.

Most days, not one day

Four sittings of ninety minutes across a week outperform one Saturday of six, and the difference is not small. Language is held by frequency of contact rather than by hours accumulated in a block.

Say it aloud

Silent reading trains none of the muscles that speech uses. Read the dialogue out loud, at the pace a person would say it, even when nobody is listening. That is the cheapest practice available to you.

How long a level actually takes you

Twelve hours a week finishes a level in about four months. Six hours a week finishes the same level in about eight. Neither is failing, and neither is measured against you: the programme is asynchronous, so the hours you can give it change when the award arrives and change nothing else.

IV Getting Help

Four offices, and which one owns your question.

Who answers what.

One address reaches the College. Naming the office in your subject line is what gets a message to the right desk on the first pass instead of the second, and it is the single largest saving of time available to you.

Subject line: Registrar

Enrolment, your record, transcripts, a correction to your name, a pause in study, and how a regulation applies to your case. The Registrar holds the register and answers for what is written in it.

The material itself: a point you cannot follow after the second explanation, a tutorial, a rubric you read differently from your marker, or a mark you want reviewed. The appeal route sits on Academic regulations.

Student Success

The office in the subject line, your student reference in the first line, and what you want to happen in the second. Write to info@worldwencollege.co.uk. V Interruption

Three instruments for a study that has to stop.

When study has to stop for a while.

Illness, a birth, a bereavement, a job that changes shape, a move between countries. The College expects all of these across a three-year programme and holds three instruments for them. Each is asked for in writing, and none costs anything.

One · Up to 12 months

A pause, on request

Write to the Registrar and a pause of up to twelve months is granted on request, without documents, without a fee and without a reason. Your level, your marks and your record stay exactly as you left them.

Two · Within 20 working days

An attempt set aside

Where illness or an emergency spoiled an attempt, ask within twenty working days of the mark. A set-aside attempt is struck from the count of resits and leaves the standard untouched — see the resit rule.

Three · The money

What you have paid

A pause changes nothing about a fee, in either direction. What is refundable, and for how long, is answered once and in one place: the refund policy at Tuition and fees.

Coming back

A pause ends on the day you say it does. The College writes to you once, at six months, to ask whether to hold your place; silence holds it. Returning needs no application, no second placement assessment and no explanation of where you were.

VI Equipment

What the programme actually runs on, said before you pay.

What you actually need to study here.

Published before enrolment rather than discovered after it. The programme is designed for a learner on a phone with an uneven connection, because that is where a great many of its learners are.

A phone is enough

Any phone, tablet or computer of the last several years, with a current browser and a microphone. Nothing in the programme requires a desktop machine, and the College builds nothing that would.

No video, anywhere

The teaching is written and the listening material is spoken. There is no video anywhere in the programme, which is a decision about your data bill as much as about how language is best taught.

A connection that drops

Recording continues while you are offline and uploads in parts once the connection returns, so a drop costs you a fragment rather than a file. The Lab is described in full under the Listening Lab.

What the College does not ask you to own.

  • A headset. An ordinary phone microphone carries every feature that gets marked.
  • A camera. No assessment on this programme looks at you.
  • A quiet room of your own. Ordinary background sound does not cost you marks.
  • A fixed hour of the day. Nothing on the programme happens at a scheduled time.
  • A second device. One is enough for every part of a level, recording included.
If the platform loses your work

Tell the Registrar within five working days. The attempt is set aside and counts against nothing, the College restores whatever it holds, and it tells you plainly what it could not restore. You are never asked to prove a failure that happened inside the College’s own system.

VII Your Record

What each line on it actually means.

How to read the record the College keeps.

What is stored, who may see it and what you control are answered on Your academic record. This leaf answers a different question: what the lines on it mean when you open the thing and read it.

Four marks, never one

Listening, reading, speaking and writing are marked apart and stay apart. Look at the lowest of the four rather than at the highest: it is the one that decides what a level costs you in time.

Every attempt, dated

The record shows all your attempts rather than your best one. The mark that stands is the mark that counts; the earlier ones are there so you can see the shape of your own progress.

Your standing

A standing describes where you are rather than what you deserve. Three exist, and each is defined on Academic regulations. Only one of them pauses progression, and none removes your access to the material.

Ask the Registrar and the College sends you everything it holds about your studying, in a form you can keep and read without its platform, within ten working days. There is no charge for this and no limit on how often you ask.

VIII Ten Things

The errors that cost a learner the most time.

Ten things new learners get wrong.

Written down rather than left to be discovered, so that meeting one costs you a week instead of a level. None is a rule and none carries a consequence. They are simply what goes wrong most.

  1. 01Waiting until the grammar is ready before speaking. It is never ready. Speaking badly on purpose, early, is the fastest route through a level.
  2. 02Skipping the listen-back. Recording and submitting without hearing yourself removes the step where most of the learning happens.
  3. 03Treating the quiz mark as the real mark. The quiz tells you what to revisit. The assignment is where speaking and writing are judged.
  4. 04Reading the rubric after the mark. It is published before the work precisely so that you can build the work against it.
  5. 05Studying in weekend blocks. Six hours on a Saturday is worth less than four evenings of ninety minutes, and it feels like more.
  6. 06Asking for a tutorial two modules late. Tutorials are not rationed. Ask on the day a point stops making sense.
  7. 07Deleting a recording you are embarrassed by. That recording is the baseline your sixth month gets measured against. Keep it.
  8. 08Assembling every sentence in translation first. It works at Level I and stops working at Level III, so break the habit while the sentences are still short.
  9. 09Reading the second explanation as remedial. Every lesson carries one, written in advance for a different kind of learner. Using it says nothing about you.
  10. 10Going quiet instead of writing. A message saying what happened takes two minutes and settles almost everything on this page.
