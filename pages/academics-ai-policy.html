Two answers are easy and both fail. A prohibition cannot be applied, and open permission certifies the tool rather than the person. This policy takes the third position: it states, assessment by assessment, what help is allowed.

How You Are Assessed Academic Integrity
I The Position

Both obvious answers were refused. This leaf says why, and what stands in their place.

Two easy answers, and neither is honest.

Each position a college can take without thinking costs it something it cannot afford. The College names both, then states what it runs instead.

The first

A prohibition nobody can apply

Banning these tools requires proving they were used, and that proof cannot be had. A rule nobody can apply is not enforced evenly; it lands on whoever is unlucky. It also teaches a learner to conceal the one thing the College most needs to see.

The second

Open permission certifies the wrong party

If any help is allowed on any task, a mark records what a learner and a machine produced together. The certificate then describes a partnership, and the reader is told about one half of it. This College certifies people.

The third, and its cost

Name the permitted help, task by task

Setting the line separately for each assessment takes authorship. Every rubric, brief and examination paper has to carry it, decided while the assessment is written. That work is done once, and it is why this page can be specific rather than principled.

The rule the rest of this page details

Assistance is legitimate when the assessment permits it and you say you used it. Those two conditions are the whole policy; everything below is their detail and their limits.

II The Four Bands

A graded scale from no assistance to open collaboration, with every assessment placed on it.

Four permissions, printed on the task.

A band is set when the assessment is written, before the teaching it tests, and you meet it beside the rubric — see How you are assessed.

Band A · Closed

No assistance of any kind

You work from your own knowledge and from material the College supplied. A generative tool, a translation tool and a search engine are all outside the band. It is used where the assessment measures what you can do unaided.

Band B · Supported study

Explaining is allowed, composing is not

You may ask a tool to explain a rule, gloss a word, or set you further examples to practise on. Nothing it composes may reach your submission, in its words or in yours.

Band C · Supported drafting

Help on a draft that is already yours

You write first. You may then put your own draft to a tool and ask what is unclear, what repeats, or where the grammar fails. You rewrite it yourself, and the order in which you work is part of the rule.

Band D · Open collaboration

Work with it, and show the working

You may use the tool as far as you like, including to generate text you keep. In exchange you submit the exchange: the prompts, what came back, and an account of what you changed. A Band D rubric marks your editing and your judgement.

Where a task carries no band, Band C applies. That default is set here so an omission on a brief can never become an argument after the marking.

III The Declaration

One line, written by you, at the moment you submit. What it must contain, and what it protects.

What you declare, and why declaring is in your interest.

The declaration is what makes a graded scale workable. It costs two sentences and settles almost every question that would otherwise need an investigation.

The form of it

One line, in your own words

Every submission carries a declaration of assistance. It names the tool, what you asked it for, and what you did with the answer. Two sentences covers most tasks, and “No assistance” covers many.

What it does for you

A true declaration is a defence

Where a marker is uncertain about a piece, the declaration is the first thing read. One that matches the work ends the question before anybody else is involved. That is the practical reason to write it carefully.

The decision behind it

Declaring beyond the band is a matter of marks

If you declare help the band did not permit, the mark is affected and the matter closes there. It is handled as a mark, not as misconduct. Concealment is what these rules deter, and punishing honest disclosure would deter honesty instead.

What a declaration must contain

  • The name of every tool used, including one built into your device or browser.
  • What you asked it for, in a phrase.
  • What you kept from the answer, and what you rewrote.
  • On a Band D task, the prompts and the output, attached to the submission.
  • The words “No assistance”, where you used nothing at all.

A submission without a declaration is returned to you for one, and the deadline moves by the days it takes. Marking begins when the declaration arrives.

IV Worked Examples

One assignment set three ways, so the bands stop being abstract.

The same task under three permissions.

The task: write a letter of complaint of about 350 words to a supplier who has delivered late twice, in a register that keeps the relationship. It belongs to Level IV, and here it is at Bands B, C and D.

At Band B

Everything except the sentences

You ask for the conventions of a complaint in British business English, for the difference between firm and hostile, and for three sample opening lines. You then write your own letter, using none of them. Your declaration says what you asked and that the letter is unaided.

At Band C

Your draft, examined and rewritten

You write the letter, then ask where it repeats and where the tone slips. You are told the third paragraph apologises for a fault that is not yours. You rewrite it in your own words, and say so in the declaration.

At Band D

All of it, with the working shown

You ask for a full draft, cut it from 500 words to 350, replace the closing, and correct two dates the tool invented. You attach the prompt, the output and a note of every change. The mark is for the cutting and correcting.

Outside every band, at all three settings

Submitting text you have not read. Submitting a recording of a voice that is not yours. Writing “No assistance” when something was used. These are the same act at three sizes, and each is dealt with under Academic integrity rather than under this policy.

V Detection

The honest answer about detection, and what carries the weight instead of it.

Detection is unreliable, so the College does not rely on it.

This is the part most policies leave out, and leaving it out is what makes the rest of them unusable.

On detector scores

A probability is not evidence, and no case starts from one

Classifiers that claim to identify generated text return false positives, and they return them unevenly. Writing by a second-language learner is flagged far more often than a native speaker’s. Almost everyone here is a second-language writer, which makes such a tool disqualifying rather than merely imperfect.

Where the weight goes

Assessment design carries the load

The scheme is built so the question is hard to raise: work is defended in conversation, speech is recorded, and a portfolio shows months of development in one voice. The reasoning, and what it costs, is set out under Academic integrity.

What a marker may act on

Three things, and each one is answerable

A defence that cannot account for the work. A recording in a voice unlike the rest of the file. A submission that contradicts its own declaration. Each is a fact a person can put to you in writing, and each is one you can answer.

Where this leaves a learner who did the work

Nothing you submit is passed through software that guesses at your authorship, and you are never asked to prove you wrote something. What the College asks is that you can talk about your own work. That is easy for anyone who did it and impossible for anyone who did not.

VI The College’s Own Use

The rules on the other side of the submission. Four, in force under these regulations.

What the College holds itself to.

A policy that governs only the learner is half a policy. A learner is entitled to know what happens to the work after it is handed in.

Marking

Every mark is decided by a person

The platform holds no automated grading engine and none is claimed. Quizzes are marked by machine against a fixed key. Everything requiring judgement is judged by academic staff against a rubric published before the work, and second-marked by another.

Feedback

The comments on your work are the marker’s own

Where the College offers automated feedback on pronunciation, three conditions hold together: it is labelled automated on your record, it sits beside the instructor’s comment rather than in place of it, and it is never the mark.

Your work

Submissions are not sent to a general-purpose model

No submission, recording or personal record leaves the College’s own storage for a third-party model. Every service that processes learner data is named on the privacy page, and a service reaches that list before it processes anything.

Published material

Somebody signs every line the College publishes

Curriculum, rubrics, examination papers and listening scripts are reviewed and signed off by academic staff answerable for them. A drafting tool used at any stage changes nothing about that review. Responsibility for a published sentence rests with whoever approved it.

The one place a machine decides anything

A quiz is marked against a fixed answer key, and the result opens or does not open the next step. That is the whole of automated decision-making here. Anything carrying a level, an honour or an award passes through a person named in the regulations.

VII Right of Reply

How an allegation under this policy is put, answered and closed.

If it is put to you that a band was exceeded.

The general procedure for a suspected breach lives under Academic integrity. This policy adds three things to it.

One

It is specific, or it is not put at all

An allegation names the assessment, the band that assessment carried, and what in the work is inconsistent with your declaration. A general impression about a piece of writing is not an allegation and never reaches a learner as one.

Two

Ten working days to reply

Counted from the day the allegation reaches you, and no decision is taken inside that period. An extension is granted on request where you are ill, travelling or sitting an examination.

Three

You may answer in conversation instead

You may ask to discuss the work with the person deciding rather than write to them. An oral account of your own writing is the strongest reply anyone who wrote it can give, and this policy grants it as a right.

The outcomes available, and no others

  • The matter is closed and the mark stands.
  • The mark is set aside and the work is re-submitted at the same band.
  • The mark is adjusted for assistance that went beyond the band.
  • The matter is referred to the academic integrity procedure, which has its own reply and its own appeal.

Each outcome is recorded with the reason for it and the evidence relied on, and each may be appealed to somebody who took no part in the decision. The three-stage ladder is set out under Academic regulations.

Questions this policy is asked

A spellchecker built into your browser or word processor is not declarable at Bands B, C and D, and falls outside Band A along with everything else. A translation tool is a different case: translating your own sentence into English is composition, so it is declarable at every band.
Ask before you attempt it and the answer comes in writing. Where nothing is printed, Band C applies, nobody is marked down for relying on it, and the brief is corrected for everyone.
Curriculum, rubrics and site copy are reviewed and approved by people answerable for them, and that review is the same whatever assisted the drafting. What no tool does here is decide a mark, write your feedback, or hold your work.
They will, because the tools change. The band definitions are adopted by the Executive, and the academic ones are subject to ratification by the Academic Senate. A change applies to assessments set after the date it takes effect, never to work already submitted.
